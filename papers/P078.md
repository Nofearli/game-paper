## 2. Research Background

Multi-player pursuit-evasion games (PEGs) model adversarial interactions in biology (predation), economics, traffic, and defense, and are generically non-zero-sum with high-dimensional state spaces (concatenations of all players' positions/velocities) and high-dimensional joint action spaces, so exact solutions are computationally intractable in general. This paper considers one evader that must reach a target location while avoiding capture by N pursuers with single-integrator dynamics in the plane — a multi-player reach-avoid problem. The authors' own prior work (ACC 2017, ref. [20]) reformulated this dynamic non-zero-sum game as a sequence of two-player static zero-sum matrix games whose payoffs were hand-crafted from time-to-capture/time-to-target metrics and solved by a linear program (LP) at each stage. Reinforcement learning for zero-sum Markov games originates with Littman's minimax Q-learning [22], but it had been demonstrated mainly on small problems with discrete state and action spaces. The present paper keeps the matrix-game solution structure of [20] and replaces the hand-crafted payoff entries with payoffs produced by min–max Q-learning, performed in a low-dimensional feature manifold so that the learned policy is independent of the number of pursuers and their speeds.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** Given N pursuers and one evader with integrator dynamics (1)–(2), a capture radius ℓ, a target x_T with tolerance ε, and a horizon bound T_f = K̄Δt, find the evader's control sequence so that ‖x_e(K) − x_T‖ ≤ ε and ‖x_e(k) − x_i(k)‖ > ℓ for all i and all k (Problem 1, eq. (4)). Pursuers have a preferred strategy — relay pursuit with the minimum time-to-capture as relay metric (eq. (3), citing [11]) — but the learned evader must also be robust to fully non-cooperative simultaneous pursuit. Players have perfect state information; the target is known only to the evader.

**[Authors' claim] Gap statements and verification:**

- *Claim:* "Finding the exact solution to such a pursuit-evasion game can be a complex task due to the high dimensionality of the problem" (p. 1). *Verified as a reasonable but informal claim* — no hardness proof is given; the state dimension is 2(N+1) positions in R² and the joint action space is continuous.
- *Claim:* Their previous matrix-game approach [20] is improved "using Q-learning to learn the elements of the payoff/cost matrix" (p. 2). *Verified*: [20] (Selvakumar & Bakolas, ACC 2017) indeed solved an LP per stage with a myopic strategy; the structure (sequence of zero-sum matrix games) is retained.
- *Claim:* Min–max Q-learning "has been demonstrated for simple problems with discrete state and action spaces in [22]" (p. 2). *Verified against the cited text*: Littman (ICML 1994) introduced minimax Q for Markov games with tabular representation; [24] (QL2, Neurocomputing 2009) extends to mixed policies for two-player zero-sum Markov games. So the claimed gap — a minimax-Q scheme that operates on continuous dynamics via a low-dimensional feature state with a constant-size payoff matrix independent of N — is genuine relative to the cited literature.
- *Claim:* The new formulation "renders it independent of the number of pursuers, as well as the number of actions that are available to the evader" (p. 5). *Verified structurally*: the learning payoff matrix has fixed size N_p × N_e = 2 × 4 and the learning state has fixed dimension m = 4 for all N (eqs. (8)–(12), (15)–(16)); transfer across N is demonstrated experimentally (train N=3, test N=4, 5, and LP-timing up to N=60).
- *Gap not addressed by prior matrix-game/geometry work*: prior multi-pursuer approaches ([8]–[17]) use geometric arguments (dynamic Voronoi partitions, switching strategies, greedy pursuit) and the authors' earlier LP payoff was heuristic; none of the cited works learns the payoff function in a player-count-invariant feature space with a minimax backup.

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Modeling (game reduction)**: A systematic reduction of the (N+1)-player dynamic non-zero-sum reach-avoid game to a *sequence of two-player static zero-sum matrix games* — pursuer group treated as a single minimizer P that deploys exactly one pursuer per stage — each stage game solved as a primal–dual LP pair (eqs. (6)–(7)); existence of a mixed-strategy saddle point for the finite zero-sum multi-act game is invoked from [4].
2. **Modeling (invariant abstraction)**: A replacement payoff matrix M_k ∈ R^{N_p×N_e}$_{≥0}$ of *constant size 2×4* for any N, built from feature-based discrete actions: pursuer team actions {p1 relay pursuit, p2 all-pursuers-active} and evader actions {e1 pure evasion from nearest pursuer, e2 "collective evasion" toward the largest angular gap (eq. with bisector direction), e3 head to target, e4 motion normal to the line of sight of the closest pursuer}.
3. **Algorithm**: Min–max Q-learning with *linear function approximation* whose basis features are the heading reward and the first three components of the next learning state (eq. (15)–(16)), with the backup min_{i'}(M_{k+1}π_{e_{k+1}}) (eq. (17)); learning state ψ(k) ∈ R^4$_{≥0}$ composed of sigmoid-squashed ratios of time-to-capture/intercept to time-to-target plus the unscaled time-to-target (eq. (12)); reward = target-heading + state-transition (safety-ratio improvement) + terminal (eqs. (18)–(21)).
4. **Experiments**: Transfer tests with more pursuers than in training (N=3 trained; N=5 and N=4 tested, grids also changed from unit square to [0,10]²), comparison against two non-learning matrix-game baselines (M-2: N×(N+1) analytic payoffs; M-3: 2×4 hand-crafted payoffs), and a computational-efficiency study showing near-constant per-stage LP time for M-1 versus growing time for M-2 up to N=60 (Fig. 9).

No formal theorems are stated in the paper; the contributions are a formulation, an algorithm, and an empirical study (see Section 5).

### 5.1 Dynamics, discretization, information structure, termination

- **Continuous dynamics (p. 2, eqs. (1a)–(1b))**: ẋ_i^c(t) = v_{p_i} u_i^c(t), x_i^c(0) = x_i^0; ẋ_e^c(t) = v_e u_e^c(t), x_e^c(0) = x_e^0, for t ∈ [0, T_f], domain D ⊂ R², i ∈ I := {1,…,N}; speeds v_{p_i} > 0, v_e > 0; input set U := {u ∈ R² : ‖u‖ = 1 or u = 0}; the zero input is used only after termination.
- **Discrete model (p. 3, eqs. (2a)–(2b))**: x_i^d(k+1) = x_i^d(k) + (v_{p_i}Δt) u_i^d(k); x_e^d(k+1) = x_e^d(k) + (v_eΔt) u_e^d(k), k ∈ {0,…,K̄}. Inputs are piecewise constant on [kΔt, (k+1)Δt); the game has at most K̄+1 stages and T_f := K̄Δt.
- **Exactness claim (p. 3)**: under the stated hold, x_i^d(k) = x_i^c(kΔt) and x_e^d(k) = x_e^c(kΔt); "the proposed discretization scheme for the continuous-time systems given in (1) is exact." *Verified*: trivially correct, since the input is constant on each interval and the integrator integrates it exactly (no Euler truncation error).
- **Information structure (p. 3)**: perfect information of all players' states at all times; x_T known only to the evader.
- **Relay pursuit (p. 3, eq. (3))**: u_{i*}^d(x_e^d, x_i^d) = r_{id}/‖r_{id}‖ if i = i*, 0 otherwise, with r_{id} := x_e^d − x_i^d; the active pursuer i* is the one with the least time-to-capture (relay metric: minimum time-to-capture, citing [11] Bakolas & Tsiotras, Automatica 2012).
- **Termination conditions (p. 3)**: capture at stage K ∈ {0,…,K̄} if ∃i: ‖x_i^d(K) − x_e^d(K)‖ ≤ ℓ (ℓ > 0); success if ‖x_e^d(K) − x_T‖ ≤ ε (ε > 0).
- **Problem 1 (p. 3, eq. (4))**: find {u_e^d(k)}_{k=0}^{K−1}, K ≤ K̄ a free terminal stage, such that ‖x_e^d(K) − x_T‖ ≤ ε and ‖x_e^d(k) − x_i^d(k)‖ > ℓ for all i ∈ I and all k ∈ {0,1,…,K̄}. The evader has a twofold goal (reach target, avoid capture); the pursuer group has one (capture ASAP).

### 5.2 Time metrics — the core analytic objects (p. 4, eq. (5))

- **Capture-time quadratic (eq. (5))**: (v_e² − v_{p_i}²)φ² + 2(v_e u_e^T r_i − ℓ v_{p_i})φ + ‖r_i‖² − ℓ² = 0, to be solved for φ, where r_i := x_e − x_i are positions at the beginning of the stage-k game (0 ≤ k < K̄). *Derivation status*: cited to Isaacs [6]; this is the standard simple-motion capture-time relation obtained by writing the evader–pursuer distance at time φ along straight-line motions and setting it equal to ℓ (an Apollonius-type condition). *Analytic, local to a single pursuer–evader pair; global game coupling is absent by design.*
- **Four metrics** (all analytic, closed-form via the quadratic formula):
  1. **φ_c(x_e, x_i)** — min–max time-to-capture ignoring all other players: minimum non-negative real solution of (5) with u_e = r_i/‖r_i‖, assuming pursuer input u_i = r_i/‖r_i‖ (pure pursuit/tail-chase pair). Physical meaning: worst-case delay if the evader runs straight away from pursuer i.
  2. **φ_a(x_e, x_i, u_e)** — minimum time-to-intercept when the evader commits to an arbitrary fixed direction u_e (not a tail pursuit): minimum real non-negative solution of (5). Physical meaning: intercept delay when the evader's heading is known to the pursuer; used for the evader's own candidate evasion directions.
  3. **φ_s(x_e, x_i, x_T)** — minimum time-to-capture when the evader heads straight to the target: minimum real positive solution of (5) with u_e = (x_T − x_e)/‖x_T − x_e‖. Physical meaning: risk of the greedy goal-seeking maneuver.
  4. **φ_T(x_e, x_T)** — minimum time for the evader to reach the target, defined on p. 8 as φ_T(x_e(t), x_T) := ‖x_T − x_e(t)‖/v_e.
- **Assumptions**: single-integrator kinematics; speeds constant; capture when distance ≤ ℓ. **proof gap (stated none by authors, noted here)**: the existence of a real non-negative root of (5) depends on the discriminant, which changes sign with the speed ratio v_e/v_{p_i} and the geometry; the paper never states the feasibility condition. Operationally, Algorithm 1 clamps payoffs via min{φ_a(·), T̄_f} and the text (p. 4) says "If capture is not possible, we set the value to T_f", implying φ_a := +∞ when no real non-negative root exists — this convention is stated in words only, never formalized.
- **Payoff assembly — Algorithm 1 (p. 4, "Payoff Assignment to M_k")**: input x_e, x_T, x_i ∀i ∈ I, k, T̄_f; output M_k. For i ← 1..N, j ← 1..N: u_j = (x_e − x_j)/‖x_e − x_j‖; T_c = min{φ_a(x_e, x_i, u_j), T̄_f}; G_c = ⟨u_j, x_T − x_e⟩/(‖u_j‖‖x_T − x_e‖) (cosine of the angle between the evasion direction and the target bearing); (M_1)_{i,j} = T_c; (M_2)_{i,j} = G_c. For j = N+1: u_T = (x_T − x_e)/‖x_T − x_e‖; for i ← 1..N: T_c = min{φ_a(x_e, x_i, u_T), T̄_f}; G_c = 1; (M_1)_{i,j} = T_c; (M_2)_{i,j} = G_c. Finally M̂_1 = M_1 / max_{i,j}(M_1)_{i,j} (max-normalization of the evasion component) and **M_k = M̂_1 + M_2** — a weighted-sum aggregation of the two objectives described as "a standard practice in multi-objective optimization" citing [36] (p. 5). This is the *hand-crafted* payoff used by the non-learning baselines (M-2 with the N×(N+1) shape; M-3 with the 2×4 shape).
- *Note*: in the first loop the payoff (M_1)_{i,j} uses the intercept time φ_a(x_e, x_i, u_j) of the *active* pursuer i against the evader's *evasion direction from pursuer j* — the off-diagonal entries i ≠ j thus encode the mismatch case "E evades j while i hunts" (p. 3–4, Table 1 illustrates N = 2).

### 5.3 Stage matrix game and LPs (pp. 3, 5)

- **Original matrix game (Section 3)**: M_k ∈ R^{N×(N+1)}$_{≥0}$; row i = pursuer group's pure strategy "only pursuer i pursues"; columns j = 1..N = "E evades pursuer j", column N+1 = "E seeks x_T"; entries are payoffs to E; the group of pursuers is one minimizer, so the stage game is **two-player zero-sum** (the underlying (N+1)-player dynamic game is non-zero-sum — this reduction is the modeling core).
- **Existence claim [authors' claim, p. 5]**: "a two-player zero-sum multi-act game which is finite, admits a saddle point solution in mixed strategies [4]". *Verified*: classical minimax theorem for finite zero-sum games (von Neumann; Basar & Olsder [4]); the paper applies it per stage. The authors correctly note that for an equivalent (N+1)-player non-zero-sum formulation, verifying pure-strategy equilibria "would require an exhaustive search among all possibilities" (p. 5) — their justification for the zero-sum reduction.
- **Mixed strategies**: π_{p_k} ∈ Δ_{N−1}, π_{e_k} ∈ Δ_N (simplices; ⪰ 0, sums 1); one pure strategy is sampled per player per stage; in the infinite-stage limit frequencies converge to the mixed probabilities (p. 5).
- **LP pair (p. 5, eqs. (6)–(7))**:
  - pursuer side (eq. (6)): min_{(a, π_{p_k})} a subject to a·1 ≥ M_k^T π_{p_k}, π_{p_k} ⪰ 0, 1^T π_{p_k} = 1;
  - evader side (eq. (7)): max_{(b, π_{e_k})} b subject to b·1 ≤ M_k π_{e_k}, π_{e_k} ⪰ 0, 1^T π_{e_k} = 1.
  - **Strong duality statement (p. 5)**: max_{π_{e_k}} π*_{p_k}^T M_k π_{e_k} = min_{π_{p_k}} π_{p_k}^T M_k π*_{e_k}.
  - **proof gap (typographical)**: after eq. (6) the paper prints 1 := [1,…,1]^T ∈ R^N, but the constraint a·1 ≥ M_k^T π_{p_k} lives in R^{N+1} (M_k^T π_{p_k} has N+1 components); the printed dimension is correct only for eq. (7). Minor, but affects reproducing the LPs verbatim.
- Both LPs are re-solved at every stage after M_k is recomputed from the new positions (p. 5); actions are random samples from π*_{p_k}, π*_{e_k}.

### 5.4 New (learning) matrix game and discrete action sets (Section 4, pp. 5–6)

- **New matrix (p. 5)**: M_k ∈ R^{N_p×N_e}$_{≥0}$, entries = payoffs (rewards) to E = costs to the pursuer group; zero-sum with E maximizing; rows p_i, i ∈ {1,…,N_p} (pursuer group), columns e_j, j ∈ {1,…,N_e} (E). Chosen N_e = 4, N_p = 2.
- **Pursuer team actions (p. 6)**: p1 = relay pursuit (only closest pursuer active, others static); p2 = all pursuers pursue simultaneously, non-cooperatively. The paper stresses the evader is trained against *both* so its policy is robust; if pursuers were restricted to p1 the stage problem would degenerate to a single-agent MDP, whereas keeping p1/p2 gives a **Markov Game [24] with the opposing agent having two actions** (p. 10).
- **Evader actions (p. 6)**: e1 = pure evasion from nearest pursuer; e2 = "collective evasion" (below); e3 = head directly to target; e4 = move normal to the line of sight of the closest pursuer (i.e., normal to e1's direction), included to give a viable option against symmetric pursuer configurations.
- **Action e2 — largest-gap bisector (p. 6)**: in the evader's frame, let θ_i := ∠r_i be the polar angle of r_i := x_e − x_i; adjacent pursuers are consecutive lines of sight (including "behind" ones); define the angular gaps θ_i := θ_{i+1} − θ_i, θ_i ∈ [0, 2π], i_m := arg max_i θ_i, θ_r := θ_{i_m}/2, with θ_{N+1} := θ_1 for i = N; then
  **u_e = [cos(θ_{i_m} + θ_r), sin(θ_{i_m} + θ_r)]^T** — the bisector of the largest gap (move toward the largest empty angle). Tie-break: if two gaps are equal, favor the gap formed by the longer line of sight (p. 6). Notation caution: θ_i is overloaded (polar angle and gap); the printed formula is verified from the 300-dpi render.

### 5.5 Learning state space (Section 4.3, p. 7, eqs. (8)–(12))

- **Learning state variables** (at stage k; functions of positions at t = kΔt):
  - ψ_c(k) := min_i φ_c(x_e(k), x_i(k)) (eq. (8)) — least min–max time-to-capture;
  - ψ_a(k) := min_i φ_a(x_e(k), x_i(k), u_e(k)) (eq. (9)) — least time-of-intercept for the e2 direction;
  - ψ_T(k) := φ_T(x_e(k), x_T) (eq. (10)) — time-to-target without pursuers;
  - ψ_s(k) := min_i φ_s(x_e(k), x_i(k), x_T) (eq. (11)) — least intercept time when heading to target.
- **State vector (eq. (12))**: ψ(k) := [S(ψ_c/ψ_T), S(ψ_a/ψ_T), S(ψ_s/ψ_T), ψ_T]^T, with S: R → [0,1] a sigmoid; ψ(k) ∈ Ψ ⊂ R^m$_{≥0}$, m = 4.
- **Design rationale [authors' claim]**: the ratios ψ_c/ψ_T, ψ_a/ψ_T, ψ_s/ψ_T "reflect the safety … and target-reaching ability of the evader" (p. 7); the first three are squashed to comparable scales to prevent one feature from dominating the Q-update (interference minimization, p. 9). *Stated attributes (p. 7)*: (i) Ψ ⊂ R^4$_{≥0}$ with constant dimension for all N, reducing per-stage computational expense; (ii) feature-based representation identifies states far apart in position space but similar in imminent-capture/proximity terms (generalization, citing [21] Sutton & Barto). *Verified structurally*: ψ is indeed N-invariant; the min-operators are the only place N enters.
- Note ψ_4 = ψ_T is *unscaled* (not sigmoid-squashed) and not part of the Q-features (see eq. (16)); it is used for the terminal condition in the reward (eq. (20)).

### 5.6 Q-function, features, min–max backup (Section 5, pp. 8–9, eqs. (13)–(17))

- **Transition and heading maps (p. 8)**: T_q: R^m$_{≥0}$ × A_p × A_e → R^m$_{≥0}$, ψ(k+1) = T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j) (computed by propagating the dynamics in position space); R_q: R^m$_{≥0}$ × A_p × A_e → R; H_q: R^m$_{≥0}$ × A_e × R² → [−1,1].
- **Heading function (eq. (13))**: H_q(ψ(k), e_j, x_e, x_T) := −(d/dt) φ_T(x_e(t), x_T)|_{t=kΔt}; since φ_T = ‖x_T − x_e(t)‖/v_e, H_q = cos θ, the cosine of the angle between the evader's unit velocity u_e and the target bearing x_T − x_e — hence the declared range [−1,1]. (Eq. (18) on p. 10 redefines the same quantity with ψ_T instead of φ_T; the two are identical by (10) — a harmless redundancy.)
- **Payoff from Q (eq. (14))**: M_k(p_i, e_j) := Q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j, x_e, x_1, …, x_N, x_T).
- **Linear approximation (eq. (15))**: Q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j) = w_q^T ζ(ψ(k), p_i, e_j; x_T), where both Q and ζ depend on (x_e, x_1, …, x_N, x_T); w_q is independent of k, ψ(k) and the players' actions (p. 9); weights are unconstrained real numbers learned by gradient descent (p. 8).
- **Feature vector (eq. (16))**: ζ(ψ(k), p_i, e_j; x_e, x_T) := [H_q(ψ(k), e_j, x_e, x_T); (T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j))_1; (T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j))_2; (T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j))_3] — i.e., the current heading reward plus the *next* state's first three (squashed ratio) components. The 4th next-state component (ψ_T at k+1) is excluded from the features.
- **Min–max Q fixed-point equation (eq. (17))**: Q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j; x_e, x_T) = R_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j; x_e, x_T) + γ min_{i'} (M_{k+1} π_{e_{k+1}}), where M_{k+1}(p_{i'}, e_{j'}) = Q(ψ(k+1), p_{i'}, e_{j'}, x_e, x_1, …, x_N, x_T) and γ ∈ [0,1] is the discount factor; the paper says the algorithm "aims to iteratively approximate [28]" this Q (p. 9).
  - *Structural reading*: the backup value V(ψ(k+1)) = min_{i'}(M_{k+1}π_{e_{k+1}}) is the minimizer's best pure-row response to the evader's mixed strategy π_{e_{k+1}} obtained from LP (7) at stage k+1; because LP (7) maximizes b subject to b·1 ≤ M_{k+1}π_e, its optimal π*_{e_{k+1}} attains max_{π_e} min_i (M_{k+1}π_e), so V(ψ(k+1)) equals the *value of the next stage game* — i.e., the backup is consistent with Littman's minimax-Q whenever the next-stage matrix game is solved exactly. In Algorithm 2 the same quantity is used at the current stage: step iv defines V(ψ(k)) = min_i (M_k π_{e_k}).
  - *Off-policy subtlety (not discussed by authors)*: during training the evader acts ε-greedily (step v), so the behavior policy differs from π_{e_k}; no importance sampling or off-policy convergence argument is given.
- **Zero-sum? teams?** — the per-stage learned game is two-player zero-sum (E maximizer vs. pursuer *group* as a single minimizer with 2 team actions); the underlying (N+1)-player dynamic game is non-zero-sum and is reduced by the team abstraction. **Convergence guarantees: none.** No theorem, no proof, no assumption set under which Algorithm 2 converges; the word "converged" appears only in the operational sense ("after the weights have converged to a specified tolerance", p. 11). This is the single most important proof gap: minimax Q-learning with linear function approximation is not guaranteed to converge in general, and the paper does not claim otherwise — it cites [28] for "iteratively approximate".

### 5.7 Reward function (Section 5.2, p. 10, eqs. (18)–(21))

1. **Target-heading reward (eq. (18))**: H_q(ψ(k), e_j, x_e, x_T) := −(d/dt) ψ_T(x_e(t), x_T)|_{t=kΔt} (= cos θ; positive when heading toward the target). The paper stresses it is a *derivative* (limit w.r.t. the sampling period), making the reward independent of Δt and of absolute state values (p. 10).
2. **State-transition reward (eq. (19))**: R_s(ψ(k), T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j)) := (T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j))_3 − (ψ(k))_3 — the *increase* in the third squashed ratio ψ_s/ψ_T, i.e., positively rewarding an increase of time-to-capture (when heading to the target) relative to time-to-target.
3. **Terminal reward (eq. (20))**: g(ψ, e_j) := +1 if ψ_4 = 0 (target reached); −1 if ψ_1 = ψ_2 = ψ_3 = 0 (all safety ratios collapsed ⇒ capture); 0 otherwise. In practice the terminal reward may be determined from the position-space state "to avoid error due to finite precision" (p. 10) — an admission that the ψ-conditions are only asymptotically attainable since S maps into the open interval (0,1).
4. **Total reward (eq. (21))**: R_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j, x_e, x_T) = H_q(ψ(k), e_j, x_e, x_T) + R_s(ψ(k), T_q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j)) + g(ψ(k), e_j). Both positive and negative reinforcement are present; ranges of the three parts are kept comparable to minimize feature interference in the linear weight update (p. 10).

### 5.8 Algorithm 2 (p. 9, "Linear approximation of the Q-function using min–max Q-learning") — the full discrete game/learning setup

- **Input**: A_e, A_p, R_q and T_q (game dynamics); N_tr (maximum training episodes); α (learning rate); γ (discount factor); tol (convergence tolerance); δα (decay in learning rate); β (exploration probability); δβ (decay in exploration).
- **Steps** (verified against 200-dpi render):
  1. Initialize each element of w_q from a uniform distribution on (0,1);
  2. n = 0;
  3. **while** n ≤ N_tr **or** ‖δw_q‖ > tol **do** (condition *as printed*; see caveat below);
     a. pick random initial conditions x_e^0, x_T, x_i^0 ∀i ∈ I; b. k = 0;
     c. while the game episode is not over: (i) compute ψ(k); (ii) fill M_k: (M_k)_{i,j} = Q(ψ(k), p_i, e_j) for all i ∈ {1..N_p}, j ∈ {1..N_e}; (iii) find mixed strategies π_{e_k}, π_{p_k} (by the LPs (6)–(7)); (iv) V(ψ(k)) = min_i (M_k π_{e_k}); (v) with probability β choose ẽ for E uniformly at random from A_e, otherwise sample ẽ from π_{e_k}; (vi) choose p̄ for P based on π_{p_k}; (vii) Δ = R_q(ψ(k), p̄, ẽ) + γV(ψ(k+1)) − Q(ψ(k), p̄, ẽ); (viii) δw_q = α·Δ·ζ(ψ(k), p̄, ẽ); (ix) w_q := w_q + δw_q; (x) one-step state update; (xi) k := k+1;
     d. α := α·δα; e. β := β − δβ; f. n := n+1;
  4. **Output**: w_q.
- **Caveats**: (i) the while condition as printed uses "or", under which the loop would never terminate when the tolerance is met but n ≤ N_tr; the intended semantics is evidently a conjunction ("n ≤ N_tr and ‖δw_q‖ > tol") — proof gap/typo on p. 9, step 3. (ii) In step (vii), V(ψ(k+1)) requires evaluating the next stage game (M_{k+1}, π_{e_{k+1}}) per eq. (17). (iii) The pursuer's training action is sampled from π_{p_k}, i.e., the minimizer's equilibrium mixture over {p1, p2}, so the evader trains against both relay and all-active pursuit.

Validates the claim that for a slower evader outnumbered by faster pursuers all three matrix-based methods perform similarly and success is intrinsically low ("There exist initial conditions for the players such that the evader has no chance of success, which can be determined by analyzing the times to capture and intercept", p. 12).

Rows do not sum to 100% for M-1 (≈9.6% inconclusive): the text (p. 12) states the number of games inconclusive within the simulation time limit is *highest* for the learned strategy, i.e., the evader "managed to avoid capture or at least delay it until the end of the simulation time" — a survival behavior not observed with M-2/M-3 (whose rows sum to 100%). The authors' claim that M-1 is "better than the other two methods" holds for capture avoidance (2.92% vs 9.55%/7.36%) but **not** for raw target-reaching (M-3 reaches 92.64% vs 87.51% for M-1) — the advantage is the capture/delay profile.
- **Figures**: Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 — game evolutions comparing M-1 and M-2 for N = 5 (two initial-condition sets); qualitative validation that learned policies produce comparable or quicker target-reaching trajectories. Fig. 6/7 (N = 1, M-1) — pursuer initially on the evader–target line of sight; the evader uses action e4 (normal to LOS), the pursuer turns into a tail chase, and the evader reaches the target within tolerance (snapshots at t = 0, 0.5, 0.6, 0.9, 1.5, 2.2); validates the purpose of action e4. Fig. 8 (N = 4) — evader starts inside the pursuers' convex hull: evader initial position [4,4]^T, pursuers at [5,4]^T, [3,4]^T, [4,4.3]^T, [4,3.7]^T; the learned strategy performs a "swerve-like maneuver" not produced by the other payoff formulations; per the caption, in cases (a) M-1 and (c) M-3 the evader reaches the target without capture (M-2 fails); validates that learning composes maneuvers that are "difficult to explicitly generate otherwise" (p. 13). Fig. 9 — time (seconds) to solve one single-stage matrix game versus N (x-axis up to 60): M-1 (2×4 LP) stays roughly flat (≈0.03–0.07 s across N; values read from the plot), while M-2 (N×(N+1) LP) grows to roughly 0.23 s by N ≈ 45–60; grid [0,10]²; validates the constant-size matrix claim's computational benefit.
- **Sensitivity finding (p. 12)**: the learned method is sensitive to Δt relative to ℓ — when the ratio ℓ/(v_e Δt) used during training is kept constant, performance is consistent; changing the ratio makes the learned strategy execute a different move sequence for the same initial conditions. This identifies ℓ/(v_eΔt) as the true dimensionless training parameter.
- **Metrics**: % captured, % target reached, inconclusive count; per-stage payoff-construction + LP solve time. **Baselines**: M-2, M-3 (both non-learning, same LP machinery). **No external baseline** (no comparison to geometric multi-pursuer strategies of [8]–[17] or to deep-RL methods of [30], [31], [33]).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (p. 13, Concluding remarks):**
- "there are dependencies of the Q-learning solution on the variables ℓ and ε that are used during training" (transfer across ℓ, ε is not guaranteed).
- The learning-based strategy is "equally successful" as the weighted-sum (non-learning) methods in performance — the learning advantage is maneuver composition, not raw success rate.
- Future work: deep (non-linear) Q-function approximation with the same algorithm structure; identified challenges: (a) instability of a single-network bootstrapped Q approximator (addressable with double networks), (b) multi-agent Q-learning convergence concerns (mitigations suggested: reward clipping, input scaling, or embedding pursuers' play in the evader's environment); reward shaping that *guarantees* successful evasion "if such evasion is feasible" remains open.
- Sensitivity to Δt vs ℓ ratio (p. 12, quoted in Section 6 above).

**Critical observations (with page/eq.):**
1. **No convergence guarantee** for Algorithm 2: minimax Q-learning with linear function approximation has no proven convergence here; the fixed point (17) is asserted as the approximation target via [28], and termination is by weight-settling tolerance. For a T1-theory reader this is the paper's main weakness (pp. 8–9).
2. **Algorithm 2 stopping condition typo (p. 9, step 3)**: "while n ≤ N_tr or ‖δw_q‖ > tol" — as printed, "or" prevents termination upon convergence; conjunction intended. Reproducibility hazard.
3. **LP (6) dimension typo (p. 5)**: 1 printed as ∈ R^N but a·1 ≥ M_k^T π_{p_k} requires R^{N+1}.
4. **Root-existence of eq. (5) never analyzed**: when the discriminant is negative (geometry/speed dependent) "minimum non-negative real solution" is undefined; only the verbal convention "if capture is not possible, we set the value to T_f" (p. 4) and the min{φ_a, T̄_f} clamp in Algorithm 1 handle it. The feasibility characterization (who wins from a given configuration) is deferred to "analyzing the times to capture and intercept" (p. 12) without a stated criterion.
5. **Terminal conditions in ψ-space are asymptotic**: S maps into the open interval (0,1), so ψ_1 = ψ_2 = ψ_3 = 0 and ψ_4 = 0 in eq. (20) are not exactly attainable; the authors themselves switch to position-space checks "to avoid error due to finite precision" (p. 10).
6. **Backup/behavior-policy mismatch**: step (v) of Algorithm 2 explores ε-greedily while (17) backs up the equilibrium value of the next stage game; off-policy correctness is not discussed (p. 9).
7. **Pursuer model is thin**: only 2 team abstractions (relay vs. all-active, non-cooperative); no cooperative pursuit policies (e.g., [16] robust multiple-pursuer policies) are included in training or testing; evaluation always uses relay pursuit, so robustness to p2 is trained but never quantified in Tables 2–3.
8. **Performance profile nuance**: Table 3 (p. 8) shows M-1's advantage is survival/delay (≈9.6% inconclusive games, lowest capture rate) while M-3 reaches the target more often (92.64% vs 87.51%); the text's "better than the other two methods" (p. 12) should be read against this decomposition.
9. **Slow-evader regime is a practical failure mode**: with v_e = 0.9 and N = 5, ≈89.5% capture for all methods (Table 2); the method provides no advantage exactly where the reach-avoid problem is hardest.
10. **Single integrator only** ("for the sake of simplicity", p. 2); extensions to double integrators or bounded curvature are asserted (e.g., improving φ_a "by taking into account the dynamic models of the players", p. 4) but not delivered.

## 8. Citation Value

**Role in a paper's literature**: cite P078 in the reinforcement-learning-for-pursuit-evasion thread and in the matrix-game/abstraction thread: it is the clearest published instance of *minimax Q-learning used not to learn a control policy directly but to learn the entries of a per-stage payoff matrix* inside a zero-sum matrix-game reduction, with a deliberately player-count-invariant feature state (time-to-capture/intercept ratios over time-to-target) that enables cross-team-size transfer (train N=3, test N=4–5, timing to N=60). It is also the journal companion bridging the authors' ACC 2017 matrix-game formulation [20] and their TCYB 2021 reach-avoid feedback-strategy work [17]. Use it as the representative citation for: (i) feature-based state/action abstraction as an alternative to deep networks in multi-pursuer evasion; (ii) LP-solved stage games with learned payoffs; (iii) the observation that learned payoffs buy robustness/swerve-type composed maneuvers and computational constancy rather than higher raw success rates; and (iv) as an honest example of reinforcement-learning work without convergence guarantees, useful when motivating theory-grounded alternatives (barrier certificates, HJI reachability, Apollonius-based guarantees).

**Suggested citation sentences**: "Selvakumar and Bakolas [P078] reduce the multi-pursuer target-seeking evasion problem to a sequence of two-player zero-sum matrix games whose 2×4 payoff matrices are populated by min–max Q-learning in a four-dimensional time-to-capture-ratio state space, yielding an evasion policy that transfers across team sizes and pursuer speeds without retraining." Or, for a methods-contrast sentence: "Learning-based payoffs in the spirit of [P078] achieve near-constant per-stage solution times and composed evasion maneuvers, but come without convergence guarantees and with sensitivity to the training ratio ℓ/(v_eΔt)."
