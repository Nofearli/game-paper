## 2. Research Background

Border defense games — equivalently target defense [3], [4] or perimeter defense [5] — ask a team of defenders to protect a target region/boundary from an infiltrating attacker, and classical differential-game treatments (from Isaacs [1] onward, including AC-based dominance regions [8] and polynomial-time decomposition methods for perimeter defense [9]–[16]) deliver exact Nash solutions only under full-state feedback and simplified dynamics [Sec. I, p. 785]. Under partial information, the pre-detection search subproblem is stochastic: neither side knows the opponent's initial position, so the full-state-feedback machinery of differential games does not directly apply [17]–[19]. Meanwhile, multi-agent RL (MADDPG, MAPPO) handles partial observability and can discover cooperative behavior without a system model, but existing RL target-defense work [20] learns search and pursuit end-to-end, re-learning pursuit strategies that are already analytically known — wasting samples. The paper sits exactly at this interface: keep RL for the search phase (no analytic solution), and replace the pursuit phase by the closed-form game value computed at the detection instant.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem (Sec. II, pp. 785–786)**: In Ω = [0,1]², n_d single-integrator defenders (speeds v_Di, sensing radius ρ_s^(i), capture radius ρ_c^(i), speed ratio ν_i = v_Di/v_A > 1) protect the bottom edge T = {y = 0} against one attacker. Initial positions are drawn uniformly and independently from disjoint knowledge (separate spawn regions Ω_A, Ω_D) — bilateral information asymmetry. The game ends when the attacker reaches T or is captured; payoff J = max{0, y_A(t_f)} (attacker's distance from the target at termination), defender = maximizer (10). The attacker's Phase-I policy is fixed to direct descent u_direct = [0, −1]⊤ (7), reducing the problem to one-sided optimization over defender policies (11).

**Gap, as claimed by authors [Sec. I, p. 785]**:
- [authors' claim] "Analytical methods face fundamental limitations under partial information [17]–[19]... the stochastic nature of the search problem under uncertainty about the attacker's state makes it challenging to directly apply differential game techniques developed for full-state feedback settings." — Consistent with the cited partial-information line of work (Shishika et al. ICRA 2021 [17]; Maity & Pourghorban [18]; Bopardikar et al. T-RO 2008 [19]), but it is an asserted inapplicability, not a proven impossibility; the paper does not formalize why no analytic search solution exists.
- [authors' claim] "standard decomposition into 1v1 or 2v1 subgames may overlook emergent cooperative behaviors" — a modeling stance; not verified against specific cited papers.
- [authors' claim] "existing RL approaches learn both search and pursuit behaviors end-to-end, without leveraging available analytical solutions [20]. This results in sample inefficiency" — verified structurally by the paper's own baseline construction: the end-to-end MAPPO baseline with standard reward (34)–(35) must explore the pursuit phase, and Sec. V shows this slows convergence; the claim about [20] (Wang et al., Neurocomputing 2020, cooperative PE via RL) is consistent with that paper's scope.
- [authors' claim] MADDPG/MAPPO "address partial observability and discover cooperative strategies without complete system models" — generic; consistent with [21].

**Residual gap the paper leaves**: the learned search policy is optimal only against the fixed direct-descent attacker (7); the Phase-I "game" is deliberately demoted to a stochastic optimization (11), so adversarial pre-detection attacker behavior is out of scope (see Sec. 7).

## 4. Core Contributions

Stated at [p. 785, end of Sec. I] ("Our contributions are threefold") and verified against the technical sections:

1. **Modeling**: A two-phase border-defense formulation with heterogeneous, per-defender sensing/capture radii and bilateral initial-position uncertainty, transitioning from partial to perfect information at the random sensing time t_s (eqs. (1)–(11), Definitions 1–2, pp. 785–787) — the pre-detection phase is a stochastic optimization (14) coupling sensing probability and pursuit configuration quality (Remark 1).
2. **Theory**: Closed-form Nash equilibrium payoff of the post-detection pursuit phase via Apollonius Circle geometry — Lemma 1 (dominance region), Theorem 1 for one defender (eq. (16)), and Proposition 1 for multiple defenders as the minimum y-coordinate over the intersection of ACs (eq. (17)–(18)), computable as a small convex program (19)–(20) [pp. 787–788].
3. **Algorithm**: GT-assisted early termination (Definition 4, Algorithm 1, p. 788) — at the sensing instant the episode is terminated and the analytic game value J*(x(t_s)) is assigned as terminal team reward — embedded in a Dec-POMDP solved by MAPPO with shared reward, plus two comparable reward/termination structures (32)–(35) enabling a controlled GT-assisted vs. end-to-end comparison [pp. 788–789].
4. **Experiment**: MAPPO/BenchMARL/VMAS evaluation across 1v1 and 3v1 homogeneous teams showing 10% (1v1) and 18.9% (3v1) final-reward gains, faster convergence, and better sensing-time configurations (sensing rate 67.1% vs. 45.2%; median Nash payoff 0.768 vs. 0.701 over 1000 evaluation episodes) [pp. 789–790].

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

### 5.1 Setup: dynamics, information structure, payoff, termination (p. 785–786)

- **Dynamics, eq. (1)**: ẋ_k = v_k u_k, k ∈ {D_1, …, D_{n_d}, A}, single integrators, u_k ∈ U ≜ {u ∈ R² | ‖u‖ ≤ 1}; state x(t) = [x_D1⊤, …, x_Dnd⊤, x_A⊤]⊤ ∈ R^{2(n_d+1)}. Necessary condition for capture: ν_i = v_Di/v_A > 1 ∀i.
- **Initial condition, eqs. (2)–(3)**: x_A(0) ∼ Uniform(Ω_A); {x_Di(0)} ∼ Uniform(Ω_D), separate predefined regions — this is the source of the partial-information Phase I.
- **Sensing time, eq. (4)**: t_s = inf{t ≥ 0 | ∃i ∈ I : ‖x_A − x_Di‖ ≤ ρ_s^(i)}. Assumption (p. 786): after sensing, full state "trivially" becomes available to all agents for t ≥ t_s (broadcast upon detection).
- **Policies, eqs. (5)–(7)**: Phase-I defender policy γ_Di : O_i → U where O_i contains only defender positions (defender positions are shared over communication; only the attacker is unobserved); Phase-II policy γ_Di : R^{2(n_d+1)} → U. Attacker: γ_A = u_direct = [0, −1]⊤ if t < t_s; γ*_A(x(t)) if t ≥ t_s. **Justification of direct descent (p. 786)**: the attacker has no sensing and no knowledge of defenders, so any deviation from the shortest path to T "would only increase the time to reach the target without providing any informational benefit." Note this is an assumption about attacker rationality/ignorance, not a game-theoretic equilibrium argument — Phase I is not played as a game.
- **Terminal manifold, eqs. (8a)–(8b)**: S = S_A ∪ S_D with S_A = {x | y_A = 0} (breach) and S_D = {x | ∃i ∈ I : ‖x_A − x_Di‖ ≤ ρ_c^(i)} (capture); t_f = inf{t ≥ 0 | x(t) ∈ S}.
- **Payoff, eq. (9)**: J = max{0, y_A(t_f)} — attacker's distance from the target at termination (zero-sum: defenders maximize, attacker minimizes).
- **Full game, eq. (10)**: J* = max_{γ_D} min_{γ_A} E[J]; reduced one-sided problem, eq. (11): J* = max_{γ_D} E[J | γ_A] with γ_A fixed by (7).

### 5.2 Phase decomposition and the Phase-I objective (p. 786–787)

- **Definition 1 (Phase I – Stochastic Search)**: the interval [0, t_s) during which no defender has sensed the attacker (cf. (4)); defender policies depend only on all defenders' positions (5). **Definition 2 (Phase II – Deterministic Pursuit)**: begins at t_s with the boundary condition ∃i ∈ I : ‖x_A(t_s) − x_Di(t_s)‖ = ρ_s^(i) (12) (note: equality = sensing on the circle boundary, whereas the termination set (33) uses ≤); from t_s the observation is the full state (13).
- **Phase-I objective, eq. (14)**: γ*_D^I = argmax_{γ_D} E[I_{t_s<t_f} · J*(x(t_s))], where I_{t_s<t_f} is the indicator that sensing occurs before game termination, and the expectation is over initial conditions and policy stochasticity. Cases where sensing occurs but breach is unavoidable "are handled by J*(x(t_s)) = 0" (p. 787).
- **Remark 1 (Search–Pursuit Trade-off)**: the optimal Phase-I policy must jointly maximize the sensing probability P[t_s < t_f] and the quality of the sensing configuration x(t_s); a myopic coverage-maximizing search can sense often but from bad configurations, yielding suboptimal overall performance. This remark is the conceptual bridge that makes the value function of Phase II the correct shaping signal for Phase I.

### 5.3 Lemma 1 (Dominance Region) + Apollonius Circle geometry (p. 787)

- **AC definition**: for a defender–attacker pair with ν = v_D/v_A > 1, A(x_A, x_D, ν) is the locus {p : ‖p − x_A‖/‖p − x_D‖ = 1/ν}, with center and radius
  - **eq. (15a)**: c = (ν²x_A − x_D)/(ν² − 1),
  - **eq. (15b)**: r = ν‖x_A − x_D‖/|ν² − 1|.
  (Both verified numerically: the AC is the Apollonius circle of the two foci x_A, x_D with ratio 1/ν; derivable by squaring ν‖p − x_A‖ = ‖p − x_D‖ and completing the square.)
- **Lemma 1 (Dominance Region [1], [8])**: the closed disk D(x_A, x_Di, ν_i) = {p : ‖p − c_i‖ ≤ r_i} is the attacker's dominance region — the set of points the attacker can reach no later than the defender. Proof approach: cited, not reproduced (Isaacs [1]; Dorothy et al., "One Apollonius circle is enough for many pursuit-evasion games," Automatica 2024 [8]). Analytic, global for the pair. Physical meaning: everything inside the AC is attacker-reachable at least as fast as that defender.
- **Remark 2 (Effect of Nonzero Capture Radius)**: Theorem 1 assumes point capture (ρ_c = 0). With ρ_c > 0 the defender can intercept once ‖x_A − x_Di‖ ≤ ρ_c, i.e., before the point-capture meeting point, so (16) is a **conservative approximation** of the true Phase-II payoff (defender-side pessimism).

### 5.4 Theorem 1 (Nash Equilibrium Payoff, single defender) — p. 787, eq. (16)

- **Statement**: for the Phase-II pursuit game starting at configuration x(t_s), the Nash equilibrium payoff is **J*(x(t_s)) = max{0, c_y − r}**, where c_y is the y-coordinate of the AC center and r its radius.
- **Proof approach (as printed)**: two-sentence derivation from Lemma 1 — the attacker's optimal strategy is to move toward the lowest point of the dominance region, p* = [c_x, c_y − r]⊤, while the defender moves to intercept at p*; the payoff is clamped at 0 when c_y − r < 0 (attacker reaches T before interception). Local-to-the-pair, analytic, exact for point capture and the line target T.
- **Decisive step**: the identification of the game value with the minimum-y point of the attacker's dominance disk, J = min_{p∈D} p_y = c_y − r. Attacker-side guarantee: the attacker can reach p* no later than the defender (Lemma 1), so it can secure payoff ≥ c_y − r (equivalently, the defender cannot force payoff above c_y − r). **Proof gap**: the converse defender-side guarantee — that the defender can prevent the attacker from achieving y_A < c_y − r (i.e., that heading to p* is equilibrium play and the value is exactly c_y − r, not merely a lower bound for the attacker) — is not proven in the paper; it is inherited implicitly from the classical line-target reach-avoid results [1], [8]. Also no uniqueness/strategy-pair argument is given.
- **Physical meaning**: at detection, the defender team's expected final payoff is exactly how low the attacker's guaranteed "shadow" under the AC reaches; c_y − r ≥ 0 means capturable before the line, c_y − r < 0 means breach is unavoidable and J* = 0.
- **Extensibility**: Remark 2 (ρ_c > 0 conservative); the same geometric object is reused for the multi-defender value (Sec. 5.5) and as the RL terminal reward (Sec. 5.6).

### 5.5 Definition 3 + Proposition 1 (multi-defender Nash payoff) — p. 787–788

- **Definition 3 (Multi-Defender Dominance Region), eq. (17)**: with n_c defenders having capture capability, index set I_c, positions {x_Di} and speed ratios {ν_i}, the attacker's dominance region is the intersection R_A = ⋂_{i∈I_c} D(x_A, x_Di, ν_i). (Only capture-capable defenders enter; sensor-only defenders influence Phase II solely through information.) Note R_A is always nonempty since x_A ∈ D_i for all i (‖x_A − x_A‖ = 0 ≤ r_i).
- **Proposition 1 (Multi-Defender Nash Equilibrium), eq. (18)**: J*(x(t_s)) = min_{p ∈ cl(R_A)} p_y — the Nash payoff is the lowest y-coordinate in the intersection of the ACs (Fig. 3: two-defender example; the optimal interception point, red star, is at (0.561, 0.33)). **No proof is given** — the proposition is asserted by direct analogy with Theorem 1. **Proof gap**: for n_c ≥ 2 the claim that the value of the multiplayer line-target pursuit game equals min-y over the dominance intersection is nontrivial — it presumes (i) the attacker's best guaranteed outcome is its best point in R_A (attacker-side, plausible since R_A is where it is never outrun), and (ii) the defender team can hold the attacker to that level (defender-side coordination guarantee), neither of which is constructed; equilibrium *strategies* for Phase II with multiple defenders are never stated, only the scalar value. The clamp at 0 is also absent from (18) (present in (16)); the text (p. 787) says breach cases are "handled by J*(x(t_s)) = 0" — a presentational inconsistency between (16) and (18).
- **Convex program, eqs. (19)–(20)**: min_{p_x, p_y} p_y s.t. ‖p − c_i‖² ≤ r_i², ∀i ∈ I_c — a linear objective over an intersection of disks.
- **Remark 3 (Computational Complexity)**: single-defender case is closed-form; multi-defender case is numerical but **convex** ("minimizing a linear objective over an intersection of convex sets"), hence efficiently solvable by standard convex optimization. Correct as stated (the constraint set is an intersection of balls; cl(R_A) closed). Fig. 4 visualizes the induced value function J* over a third defender's position (contour plot) — the level-set landscape that the Phase-I policy is implicitly optimizing.

### 5.6 GT-assisted reward mechanism and early-termination logic (p. 788)

- **Definition 4 (GT-Assisted Termination)**: upon sensing at t_s with configuration x(t_s): (1) compute the Nash equilibrium payoff J*(x(t_s)) using eq. (18); (2) **terminate the episode immediately**; (3) assign terminal reward R = J*(x(t_s)) **to all defenders**.
- **Algorithm 1 (GT-Assisted Episode Termination, p. 788)**: input sensing configuration x(t_s) and speed ratios {ν_i}; extract x_A and {x_Di}; for each defender with capture capability compute c_i ← (ν_i²x_A − x_Di)/(ν_i² − 1) and r_i ← ν_i‖x_A − x_Di‖/|ν_i² − 1| (i.e., (15a)–(15b) per defender); if single defender then J* ← max{0, c_y − r} (16), else J* ← SolveConvexOptimization({c_i, r_i}) (the program (19)–(20)); return R = J*.
- **Termination condition for GT training, eq. (33)**: S_term^GT = {s_t ∈ S | ∃i ∈ I : ‖x_A(t) − x_Di(t)‖ ≤ ρ_s^(i)} — note ∃i ∈ I (any sensor, including sensor-only defenders), not I_c.
- **GT reward, eq. (32)**: R_t^i = 0 if t < t_s; J*(x(t_s)) if t = t_s, ∀i ∈ I (shared team reward).
- **Undiscounted payoff equivalence, eqs. (21)–(22)**: R^GT(x(t_s)) = E[R^full(x(t_s)) | optimal play] (21); by Theorem 1, R^GT(x(t_s)) = J*(x(t_s)) (22), "which equals y_A(t_f*) under optimal play by both sides, where t_f* is the optimal termination time." Status: (21)–(22) are stated, not proven — their truth reduces to Theorem 1/Proposition 1 being correct. **Discounting caveat (in-paper)**: the undiscounted terminal payoffs coincide, but discounted returns differ because the GT reward is banked at t_s < t_f; under discounting the GT formulation "induces a modified learning objective that places additional value on earlier sensing" — the authors adopt this as a feature aligned with efficient search. For suboptimal pursuit play, the GT reward "provides an upper bound, preventing reward exploitation through poor pursuit execution" — i.e., J* ≥ what full simulation would actually yield if pursuit were executed imperfectly, so the trained search policy is evaluated against an idealized (optimistic-for-the-defender) pursuit outcome; this is exactly where deployment-time performance can fall short of training-time reward if the real pursuit controller is not the analytic equilibrium one.
- **Proposition 2 (Computational Efficiency), eq. (23)**: eliminating pursuit simulation saves, per episode, an estimated number of steps **T_p ≈ [(ρ_s − ρ_c)/(v_D − v_A)] / Δt**, "where the numerator represents the approximate time required to close the distance from sensing to capture at the maximum relative speed, and the denominator converts this time into discrete simulation steps." Status: a heuristic one-dimensional estimate (head-on closing speed v_D − v_A), not a theorem; with Table-I numbers ((ρ_s − ρ_c)/(v_D − v_A) = 0.23/(2.33 v_A) ≈ 0.0987/v_A time units) the savings are modest per episode but compound over millions of frames, and the main claimed benefit is exploratory (no need to explore pursuit strategies), which the paper argues qualitatively rather than quantitatively.
- **Impact on learned behavior (p. 788)**: because training optimizes (14) with the analytic Phase-II value, the policy must learn to position defenders "not just for sensing, but for configurations that enable effective pursuit — despite never experiencing pursuit during training."

### 5.7 MARL formulation (Sec. IV, pp. 788–789)

- **Dec-POMDP, eq. (24)**: ⟨I, S, {A_i}, {O_i}, T, {R_i}, γ⟩ with S = Ω^{n_d+1} ⊂ R^{2(n_d+1)}, phase-dependent O_i (Sec. 5.1), transition governed by (1) and (7), shared R_i, discount γ ∈ [0, 1) (numeric value UNREPORTED). Learned policy π_θi : O_i → Δ(A_i) corresponds to γ_Di of Sec. II; team objective eq. (25): J(θ) = E_{π_θ}[Σ_{t=0}^{T_f} γ^t R_t | s_0 ∼ ρ_0] with ρ_0 from (2)–(3). The authors explicitly note (p. 789) that learned policies are not guaranteed to converge to a global optimum and are not directly interpretable, but the GT mechanism guarantees the pursuit outcome is always evaluated optimally.
- **Action space, eqs. (26)–(27)**: a_t^i ∈ [−1, 1] ⊂ R (scalar heading), mapped to u_Di(t) = [cos(a_t^i π), sin(a_t^i π)]⊤ so ‖u‖ = 1.
- **Observations, eqs. (28)–(31)**: Phase I: o_t^i = [x_D1⊤, …, x_Dnd⊤]⊤ ∈ R^{2n_d} (own + teammates via communication; attacker unobserved). Phase II: o_t^i = s_t ∈ Ω^{n_d+1}; pursuers with ρ_s^(i) = 0 also gain the full state once any sensor detects the attacker.
- **Standard (baseline) reward, eqs. (34)–(35)**: R_t^i = 0 if t < T_f; max{0, y_A(T_f)} if t = T_f; termination S_term^std = {s_t | y_A(t) ≤ 0 or ∃i ∈ I_c : ‖x_A(t) − x_Di(t)‖ ≤ ρ_c^(i)}, where I_c = {i ∈ I | ρ_c^(i) > 0}.
- **Shared reward rationale (p. 790)**: identical rewards for all defenders encourage coordination; individual rewards "risk incentivizing greedy behaviors" — especially in heterogeneous teams where the sensor's value is realized only through the pursuer's capture.
- **MAPPO [21] via BenchMARL [22], CTDE**: Gaussian policy a_t^i ∼ N(μ_θi(o_t^i), σ_θi(o_t^i)) with actions clamped to [−1, 1] (36); centralized critic V_φ(s_t) ≈ E[Σ_{k=t}^{T_f} γ^{k−t} R_k | s_t] (37); clipped surrogate L^CLIP(θ_i) = E_t[min(r_t^i(θ_i) Â_t^i, clip(r_t^i(θ_i), 1−ε, 1+ε) Â_t^i)] (38) with ratio r_t^i = π_θi(a_t^i|o_t^i)/π_θold_i(a_t^i|o_t^i); GAE [23]: Â_t^i = Σ_{l=0}^∞ (γλ)^l δ_{t+l} (39), δ_t = R_t + γV_φ(s_{t+1}) − V_φ(s_t).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (Sec. VI, p. 791)**: (i) extension to multiple simultaneous attackers, which "would require handling partial sensing transitions as not all attackers may be detected at the same time"; (ii) scalability of the Apollonius-circle-intersection convex optimization to larger teams; (iii) incorporating game-theoretic insight into the search phase itself; plus the in-paper caveats: policies are not globally optimal nor interpretable (p. 789), and (16) is conservative for ρ_c > 0 (Remark 2).

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. **Theorem 1 proof gap** (p. 787): only the attacker-side guarantee (attacker can reach p* = [c_x, c_y − r]⊤ no later than the defender, via Lemma 1) is given; the defender-side matching bound is not proven, so "Nash equilibrium payoff" is asserted rather than established in-paper.
2. **Proposition 1 unproven** (p. 788, eq. (18)): the multiplayer value claim transfers Theorem 1 by analogy; no Phase-II equilibrium strategies are constructed for n_c ≥ 2, and no proof that min-y over R_A is the game value. Additionally (18) omits the max{0, ·} clamp that (16) has (clamp only described in text, p. 787).
3. **Fixed attacker** (eq. (7), p. 786): the direct-descent policy is justified by attacker ignorance ("no sensing capability and no knowledge of defender positions"), making Phase I a one-sided stochastic optimization (11), (14) — not a game. An evasive Phase-I attacker (e.g., sensing-avoiding or feinting) breaks the premise; the learned search policy's robustness to such attackers is untested.
4. **Discounted objective mismatch** (pp. 788, eqs. (21)–(22)): GT-trained policies optimize a discounted objective in which reward is banked at t_s, which differs from the true game objective; the authors reframe this as intentional early-sensing bias, but it means the trained policy solves a different problem than (10).
5. **Optimism at deployment** (p. 788): for suboptimal pursuit play the GT reward is an upper bound on realized payoff; if the deployed system lacks the analytic pursuit controller, training-time reward overstates field performance.
6. **Proposition 2 is heuristic** (p. 788, eq. (23)): T_p estimate assumes a 1-D head-on closing at v_D − v_A; no measured wall-clock or step-count savings are reported.
7. **Heterogeneity motivated but not evaluated** (Fig. 1, p. 786 vs. Sec. V): the environment illustration and shared-reward discussion feature heterogeneous roles (sensor-only D1, sensor+capture D2, capture-only D3), yet all quantitative experiments are homogeneous teams; the abstract's "different team sizes" claim covers only 1v1 vs. 3v1.
8. **Statistical rigor** (pp. 790–791): 3 seeds, no confidence intervals or significance tests printed; "lower variance" (Fig. 6) is qualitative.
9. **Information broadcast assumption** (p. 786): full state "trivially" available to all agents after sensing — communication cost/delay is abstracted away (consistent with [18]-style assumptions but a modeling simplification).

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: this is the canonical recent example of *hybrid analytic-game/RL architectures* for adversarial search-and-pursuit under partial observability: the Apollonius-circle Nash value of the post-detection subgame is computed in closed form (or via a tiny convex program) and substituted as the terminal reward at the sensing instant, so MARL (MAPPO) spends all its samples on the genuinely hard, solution-free part — the stochastic search — while provably-optimal continuation is guaranteed for free. It complements the purely analytic line (Isaacs [1]; Dorothy et al. [8]; Von Moll et al. [2]) and the purely learning line (Wang et al. [20]; MAPPO [21]) in this library, and is directly relevant to any work on reward shaping from game values, early episode termination, search–pursuit decoupling, or heterogeneous sensor/pursuer teams. **Suggested citation sentence**: "Das et al. compute the Apollonius-circle Nash payoff of the post-detection pursuit subgame and inject it as an early-termination terminal reward for MAPPO, improving final rewards by 10–18.9% and sensing rates from 45.2% to 67.1% in border-defense search without ever training the pursuit phase." When citing this work, cite this ACC version (P017), not the preprint duplicate (P016).
