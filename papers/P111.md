## 2. Research Background
Spacecraft orbital games model strategic interactions among multiple spacecraft in a 3-D orbital environment, with applications the authors list as satellite constellation management, space debris mitigation, and formation control (p. 11404, refs. [1]–[5]). The paper sits in the target–attacker–defender (TAD) branch of multiplayer pursuit–evasion games: a nonmaneuverable high-value target (e.g., a space station) is threatened by an attacker spacecraft, while a defender spacecraft tries to intercept the attacker before it reaches the target (p. 11405). Existing spacecraft TAD/game work is largely analytical (two coupled pursuit–evasion games [27], multiplayer double-integrator TAD [28], linear–quadratic duration-adaptive strategies [26]), which the authors argue suffers from strong nonlinearities, physical limitations, and simplifying dynamics assumptions in complex environments (p. 11405, ref. [29]). Meanwhile RL for orbital pursuit–evasion exists (impulsive orbital PE with PRD-MADDPG [18], elliptical-orbit PE [19]), but RL training in such settings is hampered by sparse terminal rewards and training instability. The paper therefore proposes a learning-based solution to a constrained 3-D impulsive-maneuver TAD game on a geosynchronous orbit, built on multiagent TD3 (MATD3, ref. [6]) with three add-ons: a Hamilton regularization loss, an adaptive curriculum on the maneuver interval, and a sun-elevation-angle adaptation mechanism.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
- [Authors' claim, p. 11405] "there exist seldom presented works focusing on the scenario of spacecraft TAD game" — i.e., few RL works address the spacecraft TAD scenario specifically. Verified against the paper's own related-work paragraph: cited TAD works are analytical (two coupled PE games [27], multiplayer double integrator [28], LQ duration-adaptive [26]); cited RL orbital works [18], [19] are pursuit–evasion (two-player), not TAD. The gap claim is plausible within the cited literature.
- [Authors' claim, p. 11405] Analytical differential-game solutions for TAD "are confronted with implementation difficulties in complex environments due to inherent strong nonlinearities, physical limitations, and simplifying assumptions in terms of spacecraft system dynamics [29]". This is an asserted motivation (attributed to [29]); it is not demonstrated quantitatively in this paper — the only analytical comparison is against a discrete LQ Nash baseline in Section IV-D.
- [Authors' claim, p. 11405] "the available RL approaches often suffer from training instability and convergence issues, particularly in complex environments with sparse reward distributions [18]" — used to justify the Hamilton regularization + curriculum + reward decomposition. Verified as a design motivation only; no theoretical convergence analysis is offered.
- Gap statement synthesized by the authors (p. 11405): practical spacecraft TAD requires 3-D modeling, and maneuvers are discrete velocity increments (impulse sequences); impulsive constraints make the game hard for classical differential-game and heuristic methods. The paper fills this with a 3-D state-transition-matrix model + MARL optimization. Note: the "3-D + impulsive + constrained TAD" combination, not any single ingredient, is the novelty claim; the individual algorithms (TD3, MATD3, curriculum, Rodrigues rotation, Lambert correction) are all standard tools recombined.

## 4. Core Contributions
Stated as threefold (p. 11406), with my classification:
1. **Algorithm (theory-flavored)**: HMATD3 — MATD3 with a "Hamiltonian regularization" loss added to each value network's TD loss (eqs. (18)–(27)), claimed to stabilize training and improve exploration/convergence. This is a heuristic loss-shaping contribution; no convergence or optimality proof is given.
2. **Training methodology**: a performance-driven adaptive curriculum that adjusts the maneuver interval Δt online via a staircase map of a sliding-window truncated rate (eqs. (28)–(30)), combined with a dual-component reward architecture (process-oriented + terminal-condition rewards, eqs. (31)–(35)) to fight sparse-reward local optima.
3. **Constraint-handling mechanism**: a sun-elevation-angle adaptation method that rotates the intended intercept geometry and computes a corrective impulse via the CW state-transition matrices (Rodrigues rotation + Δv_adj = −Φ_rv^{-1}(Φ_rr r_P − r'_P), p. 11412, eq. (38)), applied to the defender only.
4. **Modeling + experiments (supporting)**: a constrained impulsive TAD game model in LVLH frame on GEO with CW dynamics discretized by STM (eqs. (1)–(12)); numerical simulations including MATD3-vs-HMATD3 ablation, 7-algorithm MARL comparison (MARLlib [37]), a discrete LQ Nash differential-game baseline [24], maneuverability sweep (1000-run Monte Carlo per grid point, Fig. 12), and observation-noise robustness sweep (Fig. 13).

### 5.1 Dynamics model (impulsive maneuver model)
- **CW equations (p. 11406, eq. (1))**: in the LVLH frame centered at the nonmaneuverable target,
  ẍ(t) = 2n₀ẏ(t) + 3n₀²x(t) + uₓ(t); ÿ(t) = −2n₀ẋ(t) + u_y(t); z̈(t) = −n₀²z(t) + u_z(t),
  with n₀ = √(μ/a_o³), μ the gravitational constant, a_o the reference orbit radius. (Standard CW form; the −n₀² z term appears with the opposite sign convention compared to some texts, but as printed it is z̈ = −n₀²z + u_z.)
- **Assumption 1 (p. 11406)**: (1) relative distances ≪ orbit radius so CW applies; (2) attack/defense spacecraft maneuver by instantaneous impulsive velocity changes and attitude adjustment is instantaneous (radar always faces the opponent); (3) both maneuverable spacecraft use the same fixed maneuver interval Δt during the game. (Note: Assumption 1(3) holds within an episode; the curriculum of eq. (30) varies Δt across episodes — see §7.)
- **Discretized state transition (p. 11406, eq. (2))**: with state x_l(t_i) = [r_l^T(t_i), v_l^T(t_i)]^T, l ∈ {A, D}:
  x_l(t_{i+1}) = Φ(Δt) x_l(t_i) + Φ_v(Δt) Δv_l(t_i),
  where Φ = [[Φ_rr, Φ_rv],[Φ_vr, Φ_vv]], Φ_v(Δt) = [Φ_rv; Φ_vv] (a 6×3 stack).
- **STM blocks (p. 11407, unnumbered, verified from rendered page)**: with s ≡ sin(nΔt), c ≡ cos(nΔt):
  Φ_rr = [[4−3c, 0, 0], [6(s−nΔt), 1, 0], [0, 0, c]];
  Φ_rv = [[s/n, 2(1−c)/n, 0], [−2(1−c)/n, (4s−3nΔt)/n, 0], [0, 0, s/n]];
  Φ_vr = [[3n s, 0, 0], [6n(c−1), 0, 0], [0, 0, −n s]];
  Φ_vv = [[c, 2s, 0], [−2s, 4c−3, 0], [0, 0, c]].
  These are the standard CW/HSSTM matrices (as in e.g. Vallado/Carter); I verified consistency by differentiating Φ_rr (d/dt[6(s−nΔt)] = 6n(c−1) matches Φ_vr, etc.). No typos found.
- **Impulse model (p. 11407, eq. (3))**: v_l(t_i⁺) = v_l(t_i⁻) + Δv_l(t_i), l ∈ {A, D}.
- **Coasting phase (p. 11407, eq. (4))**: between impulses, r_l(t_{i+1}⁻) = Φ_rr(Δt) r_l(t_i⁺) + Φ_rv(Δt) v_l(t_i⁺), v_l(t_{i+1}⁻) = Φ_vr(Δt) r_l(t_i⁺) + Φ_vv(Δt) v_l(t_i⁺). So each episode is an impulsive-and-coast sequence; the control is the impulse sequence u_A = {Δv_A(t_1),…,Δv_A(t_{N−1})}, u_D likewise (p. 11408).

### 5.2 Game model, constraints, termination (winning conditions)
- **Terminal sets (p. 11407, eq. (5))**: C_A = {r_A(t) | ‖r_A(t)‖ < d_A} (attacker within kill range d_A of the target at origin); C_D = {r_A(t), r_D(t) | φ(r_A(t), r_D(t)) < 0} with φ(r_A(t), r_D(t)) = ‖r_A(t) − r_D(t)‖ − d_D (defender within intercept range d_D of attacker). (The printed φ uses (r_A, r_D); the text-extraction artifact suggesting φ(r_A, r_A) was checked against the rendered page — the paper is correct.)
- **Objective functions (p. 11408, eqs. (6)–(9))**: J_A = f_A(x_A, x_D, u_A, u_D, params), J_D = f_D(x_A, x_D, u_A, u_D, params), each minimized over its own control sequence (eqs. (8)–(9)), with params = [Δv^A_max, Δv^D_max, Δv^A_total, Δv^D_total, d_A, d_D, θ_radar] (p. 11408). Both objectives are stacked into a general-sum-looking model, but the realized rewards (§5.5) are exactly antisymmetric except for constraint punishments.
- **Terminal conditions (p. 11407–11408, eqs. (10)–(11) + list)**:
  - Successful approach (attacker wins): {r_A(t_i),…,r_A(t_{i+N_A−1})} ⊆ C_A with N_AΔt ≥ T_{dA} (a dwell-time requirement) and condition D not simultaneously holding.
  - Successful interception (defender wins): {r_A(t_i),…,r_A(t_{i+N_D−1}), r_D(t_i),…,r_D(t_{i+N_D−1})} ⊆ C_D with N_DΔt ≥ T_{dD}.
  - Timeout: t_i > t_max ⇒ both sides fail. N_max = t_max/Δt (p. 11408).
- **Constraints (p. 11407–11408, eq. (12))**: x_l(t_{i+1}) = Φ(Δt)x_l(t_i) + Φ_v(Δt)Δv_l(t_i) for l ∈ {A,D}; t_{i+1} = t_i + Δt; ‖Δv_A(t_i)‖ ≤ Δv^A_max, ‖Δv_D(t_i)‖ ≤ Δv^D_max, i ∈ {1,…,N}; Σ_{i=1}^{N−1}‖Δv_A(t_i)‖ ≤ Δv^A_total, Σ_{i=1}^{N−1}‖Δv_D(t_i)‖ ≤ Δv^D_total; plus the sun-elevation constraint θ > θ_radar (see §5.6) and maximum relative distance d_max. Also listed: photo-sensitive radars with perception radius r_s and cone half-angle θ_radar (p. 11407).

### 5.3 MDP and information structure
- **MDP (p. 11408, eqs. (13)–(14))**: M = (S, A, R, P, γ) with S = {x_A, x_D}, A = {Δv_A, Δv_D}, R = [R_A(x, t_i), R_D(x, t_i)], P = P(x(t_{i+1})|x(t_i), Δv_A, Δv_D) (deterministic given CW + impulses), γ = 0.99.
- **Cumulative objectives (p. 11408, eq. (15))**: J_A = argmax_{π_A} Σ_{i=0}^{N} γ^i R_A(x_A(t_i), t_i), J_D = argmax_{π_D} Σ_{i=0}^{N} γ^i R_D(x_D(t_i), t_i), N = t_f/Δt.
- **Partial observability (p. 11408, eqs. (16)–(17))**: each spacecraft observes only its own state, the relative state of the opponent, and the sun direction:
  o_A(t_i) = [x_A(t_i), x_D(t_i) − x_A(t_i), r̂_{DA}(t_i), s(t_i)], o_D(t_i) = [x_D(t_i), x_A(t_i) − x_D(t_i), r̂_{AD}(t_i), s(t_i)],
  r̂_{DA} = (r_D − r_A)/‖r_D − r_A‖, r̂_{AD} = (r_A − r_D)/‖r_A − r_D‖ (eq. (17)); s(t_i) is the sun direction in the LVLH frame. Each observation is 6+6+3+3 = 18-dimensional, matching the policy input size 18 in Table III; the centralized critic input 42 = 18 + 18 + 6 (both observations + joint 3-D impulse pair) — consistent with MATD3's double centralized critics [6].

### 5.4 HMATD3 update rules (the paper's central algorithmic object)
- **TD target (p. 11409, eq. (18))**: y_l(t_i) = R_l(t_i) + γ min_{j∈{1,2}} Q_{j,θ'_l}(x(t_{i+1}), u'(t_{i+1})) — clipped double-Q, standard TD3.
- **TD loss (p. 11409, eq. (19))**: L^{TD}_{l,j} = E[(Q_{j,θ_l}(x(t_i), u(t_i)) − y_l(t_i))²].
- **Hamilton regularization (p. 11409, eqs. (20)–(26))** — applied to each value network separately:
  - Joint observation s(t_i) = [o_D(t_i), o_A(t_i)], joint action u(t_i); observation derivative ṡ(t_i) = [ȯ_D(t_i), ȯ_A(t_i)] (eq. (20)).
  - For o_l = [x_{lT}, x_{l̄l}, r̂_{l̄l}, s_sun], the derivative is (eq. (21), verified from rendered page): ȯ_l = [−ẋ_{lT}, ẋ_{l̄T} − ẋ_{lT}, r̂̇_{l̄l}, ṡ_sun]. Note the leading minus sign on the own-state derivative as printed; since o_l's first block is x_{lT} itself, d/dt should be +ẋ_{lT} — this is a printed sign inconsistency (or an unstated sign convention); the second block (relative state) derivative ẋ_{l̄T} − ẋ_{lT} is consistent. Proof gap flag: the paper does not explain the minus sign.
  - ẋ_{lT}(t_i) = (x^{cw}_{lT}(t_{i+1}) − x_{lT}(t_i))/Δt by one-step CW propagation via eq. (2) (eq. (22)); r̂̇ and ṡ_sun similarly by finite differencing (not explicitly spelled out).
  - Gradient g_{l,j}(t_i) = ∇_s Q_{j,θ_l}(s(t_i), u(t_i)), each element clipped to [−g_max, g_max].
  - **Hamilton residual (p. 11409, eq. (23))**: h_{l,j}(t_i) = R_l(t_i) + g_{l,j}(t_i)^T ṡ(t_i). This is the continuous-time identity dQ/dt = −R (i.e., the residual of the stationary Hamilton–Jacobi/Bellman relation R + ∇Q·ṡ ≈ 0) evaluated on critic samples; the paper calls it "Hamiltonian regularization" but never derives it from the HJB equation of the game nor states in what sense driving h → 0 is consistent with the TD objective.
  - **Normalized Hamilton loss**: residual clipped to [−c_h, c_h]; batch scale σ^{batch}_{l,j} = max(Std(clip(h_{l,j}, −c_h, c_h)), ε_h) (eq. (24)); EMA scale σ̄_{l,j} ← β_σ σ̄_{l,j} + (1−β_σ) σ^{batch}_{l,j} (eq. (25)); L^H_{l,j} = clip(E[(h_{l,j}/σ̄_{l,j})²], 0, c_ℓ) (eq. (26)).
  - **Total value loss (p. 11409, eq. (27))**: L^Q_{l,j} = L^{TD}_{l,j} + λ_H L^H_{l,j}, l ∈ {A, D}, j ∈ {1, 2}.
- **Training schedule (p. 11409, printed)**: value networks updated every gradient step; policy and target networks updated with delayed frequency d = 2; soft target update rate τ = 0.005; policy noise std σ = 0.3. Action mapping from policy output a_l(t_i) to impulse (p. 11411, eq. (36)): Δv_l(t_i) = (tanh(a_l(t_i)) + 1)/2 · Δv^l_max — note this maps the tanh range [−1,1] onto [0, Δv^l_max] (i.e., a one-sided impulse bound; combined with the direction being free in R³ this is coherent since the vector components are each scaled).
- **Algorithm 1 (p. 11410)**: standard episode loop (reset; per step propagate states with CW; per maneuver point observe, select Δv_A, Δv_D from policy networks, execute, store (x, Δv_A, Δv_D, R_A, R_D, x′) in replay buffer; sample batch; update Q- and policy networks "using the TD3 algorithm"; soft-update targets). Caveat: the pseudocode itself does not show the Hamilton-loss step of eqs. (23)–(27) — the regularization exists only in the text/equations, so Algorithm 1 as printed is generic MATD3.
- Hyperparameters of the Hamilton term: λ_H, g_max, c_h, ε_h, c_ℓ, β_σ are named but their numerical values are **UNREPORTED** (not in Tables I–III).

### 5.5 Adaptive curriculum learning (p. 11409–11410)
- z_k ∈ {0,1} indicates whether episode k was truncated (timeout or forced termination); sliding-window truncated rate (p. 11410, eq. (28)): r_tr(k) = (1/W) Σ_{i=k−W+1}^{k} z_i, W = 100. If fewer than W episodes finished, Δt stays at Δt₀.
- **Staircase map (p. 11410, eq. (29))**: α(r_tr) = 0.30 if r_tr < 0.08; 0.45 if 0.08 ≤ r_tr < 0.16; 0.60 if 0.16 ≤ r_tr < 0.24; 0.75 if 0.24 ≤ r_tr < 0.32; 0.90 if 0.32 ≤ r_tr < 0.40; 1.00 if r_tr ≥ 0.40.
- **Interval update (p. 11410, eq. (30))**: Δt = clip(⌊Δt₀·α(r_tr)⌋, Δt_min, Δt_max), with Δt_max = Δt₀ and Δt_min = 10 s.
- Interpretation as printed: when the recent truncated rate is low, Δt decreases (finer temporal resolution, harder decisions); when truncation is high, Δt is restored larger to avoid unstable training regions. Curriculum progression is thus performance-driven rather than manually scheduled. With Δt₀ = 300 s (Table I), the curriculum spans 90 s–300 s (α = 0.30 ⇒ ⌊90⌋ s), i.e., 12–40 maneuver decisions in the 10800 s episode horizon.

### 5.6 Reward design (p. 11410–11411)
- **Process-oriented rewards (p. 11410, eq. (31))**: R_A(t_i) = log(μ_AD·‖r_A(t_i) − r_D(t_i)‖), R_D(t_i) = −log(μ_AD·‖r_A(t_i) − r_D(t_i)‖) — exactly antisymmetric; attacker (as evader w.r.t. the defender) is rewarded for distance from the defender, defender (as pursuer) for proximity. μ_AD is a scaling factor.
- **Terminal-condition rewards (p. 11410–11411, eq. (32))**: R_A(t_f) = −log(μ_AT·‖r_A(t_i)‖), R_D(t_f) = log(μ_AT·‖r_A(t_i)‖). Note the printed argument is r_A(t_i) inside a quantity labeled R(t_f) — evidently a typo for r_A(t_f) (the terminal attacker-target distance); the intended semantics is: attacker rewarded for small final distance to target, defender for large. μ_AT is a scaling factor.
- **Punishment (p. 11411, eq. (33))**: R_punish = −50 if ‖r(t_i)‖ > d_max; −50 if t_i > t_max; given to both spacecraft. The prose says "if the relative distance between the attack spacecraft and defense spacecraft exceeds the maximum distance dmax" while the constraint list (Section II-B-2, item 3) defines d_max as a bound on distances of the two maneuverable spacecraft to the target — a wording inconsistency.
- **Assembled piecewise rewards (p. 11411, eqs. (34)–(35))**: R_A(x_A(t_i), t_i) = R_A(t_i) if t_i < t_f; R_A(t_f) if t_i = t_f; R_punish if t_i > t_max or ‖r(t_i)‖ > d_max or D-set holds. R_D symmetric with "A sets up" as the punishment trigger. So each agent receives the opponent's winning event as punishment, and constraint violations/timeout punish both.
- **Insensitivity argument (p. 11411, prose)**: because μ_AD, μ_AT appear inside the log, they contribute only additive constants, so Adam-updated gradients are unaffected — the authors' (correct, back-of-envelope) argument that training is insensitive to these scales.

### 5.7 Constraint handling (p. 11411)
- t_max: episode length capped at N_max = t_max/Δt steps; violation ⇒ termination + R_punish (eq. (33)).
- Single-impulse bound: enforced structurally by the tanh map (eq. (36)).
- Total Δv budget: accumulate ‖Δv_l(t_i)‖ per maneuver point; if the running sum exceeds Δv^l_total, the episode is terminated and a punishment reward is added to that spacecraft (p. 11411).

### 5.8 Sun-elevation-angle adaptation mechanism (p. 11411–11412)
- **Sun-elevation angles (p. 11411, eq. (37))**: θ_A = arccos((r_D − r_A)·s / (‖r_D − r_A‖‖s‖)), θ_D = arccos((r_A − r_D)·s / (‖r_A − r_D‖‖s‖)) — the angle at each spacecraft between the opponent line of sight and the sun direction; the operational requirement is θ > θ_radar (photo-sensitive radar needs sun illumination; cone half-angle θ_radar).
- **Adaptation geometry (p. 11412, unnumbered)**: with x_A = [r_A^T, v_A^T]^T, x_D = [r_D^T, v_D^T]^T, the paper defines "the relative position vectors as PE = r_A − r_D and EP = r_A − r_D" (both identical as printed — notational redundancy; the E/P evader/pursuer naming suggests one of them was meant to be r_D − r_A). Sun position vector PS; θ_SPE = arccos(PE·PS/(‖PE‖‖PS‖)). If θ_SPE < θ_radar: target angle θ* = θ_SBE, rotation amount θ_PEB = θ_SBE − θ_SPE, candidate rotations θ₁ = θ_PEB, θ₂ = −θ_PEB about axis k = EP × (−PS)/‖EP‖, applied to v = EP via **Rodrigues' rotation formula [36]**: Eb₁ = v cos θ₁ + (k × v) sin θ₁ + (k·v)(1 − cos θ₁)k, Eb₂ likewise with θ₂. Both candidate sun angles θ_Eb1S = arccos(Eb₁·b₁S/(‖Eb₁‖‖b₁S‖)), θ_Eb2S similarly are computed and the rotated vector with the larger sun-elevation angle is selected: Eb = Eb₁ if θ_Eb1S > θ_Eb2S else Eb₂. Adjusted defender position r'_P = r_P + Eb.
- **Impulse correction (p. 11412, unnumbered + eq. (38))**: Δv_adj = −Φ_rv^{-1}(Φ_rr r_P − r'_P) (inverting the CW position row of eq. (2) so that the one-step-coasted position equals the adjusted one — algebraically Φ_rv Δv = r'_P − Φ_rr r_P); the final maneuver is Δv̄_l = μ_v·Δv_adj,l + Δv_l (eq. (38)) with scaling μ_v = 0.2 (printed) so the correction does not overwhelm the learned maneuver. Applied to the **defense spacecraft only** in the experiments. No feasibility check (e.g., whether ‖Δv_adj‖ respects Δv_max or whether the rotated geometry is dynamically reachable within Δt) is given.

### 5.9 Quoted differential-game baseline (p. 11414–11415, Section IV-D)
- Compact state x̃(t_i) = [x_A^T(t_i) − x_D^T(t_i), x_A^T(t_i)]^T; dynamics x̃(t_{i+1}) = Ãx̃(t_i) + B̃_A u_A(t_i) + B̃_D u_D(t_i) with Ã = diag{A_d, A_d}, B̃_A = [B_d^T, B_d^T]^T, B̃_D = [−B_d^T, 0]^T, cited from [24].
- Cost J_l = x̃^T(t_N)Q_l x̃(t_N) + Σ_{i=0}^{N}(x̃^T(t_i)Q_l x̃(t_i) + u_l^T(t)R_{ul}u_l(t)), l ∈ {A, D}.
- Nash controls u*_A(t_i) = K₁(t_i)x̃(t_i), u*_D(t_i) = K₂(t_i)x̃(t_i) with K₁ ≜ −R^{-1}B_A^T Ã^T P_A(t_i), K₂ ≜ −R^{-1}B_D^T Ã^T P_D(t_i); coupled Riccati recursion P_A(t_i) = Q_A + K₁^T R K₁ + (Ã + B_AK₁ − B_DK₂)^T P_A(t_{i+1})(Ã + B_AK₁ − B_DK₂), P_D(t_i) = Q_D + K₂^T R K₂ + (Ã + B_AK₁ − B_DK₂)^T P_D(t_{i+1})(Ã + B_AK₁ − B_DK₂), solved backward. (Printed with a single R for both players; the gain form −R^{-1}B^T Ã^T P is quoted as-is from the baseline and is not re-derived — it does not match the standard discrete-time LQR gain (R + B^T P B)^{-1}B^T P Ã, so the baseline's exact optimality should not be assumed without checking [24].)

### 5.10 Analytical claims made without proof (summary)
- No winning-condition characterization (no barrier surface, no Apollonius-type region, no capturability result) is derived for the impulsive CW-TAD game — the paper is purely constructive/learning-based.
- The only theoretical-flavored statements are: (i) the HJB-residual interpretation implicit in eq. (23); (ii) the log-reward scale-invariance argument for μ_AD, μ_AT under Adam (p. 11411); (iii) the claim that Hamilton regularization "encourages exploration … at the beginning of training" (p. 11406) — asserted, not analyzed.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated / implicit:**
- No theoretical guarantees for HMATD3: the Hamilton regularization (eqs. (23)–(27)) is motivated heuristically; no convergence, stability, or equilibrium-quality analysis is provided (whole Section III).
- The comparison to the differential-game baseline is one-sided: only the attacker uses the LQ Nash strategy and the RL defender is not retrained against it (p. 11415); no claim of RL strategic superiority over Nash play is actually supported.
- The sun-elevation adaptation is applied to the defender only (p. 11412); attacker-side constraint handling is not demonstrated.
- Observation noise degrades interception to failure at moderate noise (κ = 4, σ = 5, Fig. 14); no noise-robust training (e.g., domain randomization) is attempted.

**Critical observations (mine, with page/eq.):**
1. Eq. (21) prints ȯ_l = [−ẋ_{lT}, …] with a leading minus on the own-state derivative although o_l contains x_{lT} itself (p. 11409) — either a typo or an undeclared sign convention; reproducibility of the Hamilton residual depends on it.
2. Eq. (32) labels the terminal reward R_A(t_f) but evaluates ‖r_A(t_i)‖ (p. 11410–11411) — evidently r_A(t_f) intended.
3. p. 11412 defines PE = r_A − r_D and EP = r_A − r_D identically; the E/P (evader/pursuer) notation suggests one vector was meant to be reversed; as printed the rotation-axis formula k = EP × (−PS)/‖EP‖ is unaffected, but the prose is not self-consistent.
4. Tension between Assumption 1(3) ("maneuvers with the same fixed interval Δt", p. 11406) and the curriculum, which changes Δt across episodes between 10 s and 300 s (eq. (30)); also, a smaller Δt with the same t_max multiplies the number of decisions N_max = t_max/Δt, so the difficulty knob changes both decision frequency and the reward-horizon length.
5. Reward semantics: process and terminal rewards are exactly antisymmetric (eqs. (31)–(32)), so the game is effectively zero-sum except the symmetric −50 punishments (eq. (33)); the general-sum formulation (eqs. (8)–(9)) is not exploited (no Nash/Stackelberg analysis of the learned policies).
6. The action map (eq. (36)) scales each impulse component by (tanh+1)/2 ∈ [0, 1], so an all-negative policy output saturates at zero impulse; one-sided [0, Δv_max] scaling is unusual (the common choice is (tanh+1)/2·Δv_max mapping to [0, Δv_max] per component — as printed; directional freedom makes it workable but the paper does not discuss the induced action-distribution bias).
7. Key game parameters (d_A, d_D, T_{dA}, T_{dD}, Δv_total budgets, θ_radar, r_s) and all Hamilton-term hyperparameters are UNREPORTED — the experiments are not exactly reproducible from the paper.
8. Reference-list defects (as printed): ref. [35] "F. Scott, H. Herke, and M. David, Addressing function approximation error in actor-critic methods" does not match the well-known TD3 paper (Fujimoto, van Hoof, Meger, ICML 2018); ref. [36] (Rodrigues) carries a garbled title and an anachronistic arXiv number; ref. [11] (Isaacs 1965, Wiley) carries DOI 10.1007/s10551-009-0237-3, which is not a plausible Isaacs DOI; in-text [21] (p. 11405, "sequential intention sharing enabled MCTS") corresponds to ref. [20]. None of these affect the results but they signal weak bibliography curation.
9. Single-seed training curves (Figs. 5, 6, 10) with no variance reporting; Monte Carlo sweeps (Figs. 12, 13) evaluate fixed trained policies but the training itself is not repeated across seeds.
10. The truncated-rate curriculum (eq. (29)) conflates "episode hard" with "timeout"; since both sides' failures raise r_tr, the mechanism cannot distinguish which agent is failing — curriculum difficulty is global, not per-agent.

## 8. Citation Value
Role in the literature: this is the bridge paper between the classical TAD differential-game corpus (Garcia/Casbeer/von Moll-style TAD, Coon–Panagou multiplayer TAD, Li–Liang–Dang orbital TAD) and the MARL-for-orbital-games line (PRD-MADDPG [18], elliptical-orbit RL [19]); within the game-paper skill it is the primary T1 reference for (i) CW-equation impulsive TAD modeling with STM discretization, (ii) constrained multiagent RL reward/constraint engineering (process+terminal reward decomposition, tanh impulse mapping, total-Δv budget termination), and (iii) auxiliary mechanisms (adaptive interval curriculum; sun-elevation-angle handling via Rodrigues rotation + STM inversion), and it supplies the standard GEO parameter set (Table I) and the Monte-Carlo evaluation protocol (maneuverability and noise sweeps) used for impulsive orbital games. Its weakness — absence of any analytical solution concept (no barrier, no capturability, no equilibrium characterization) — makes it a contrast citation rather than a theory source. Suggested citation sentence: "For impulsive, constraint-laden spacecraft TAD scenarios where analytic differential-game solutions are difficult to deploy, Yuan et al. [P111] combine a CW state-transition-matrix game model with a Hamilton-regularized MATD3 and adaptive curriculum learning, demonstrating converged attack/defense strategies on GEO with Monte-Carlo robustness sweeps over maneuverability and observation noise."
