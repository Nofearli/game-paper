## 2. Research Background
The target–attacker–defender (TAD) differential game (introduced by Boyell [3,4] and matured by the Pachter/Garcia/Casbeer line [8,13,6,14]) involves an attacker pursuing a slower, defended target while a defender tries to intercept the attacker. For the classical version with unrestricted headings and simple motion, closed-form optimal point-capture guidance laws exist for both teams, and a barrier surface $B$ partitions initial configurations into a region of escape $R_e$ (defender team wins) and a region of capture $R_c$ (attacker wins) [6,13,14]. Traditional attacker guidance — pure pursuit or proportional navigation — and even the optimal attacker law are defeated by the cooperative defense whenever the target starts in $R_e$; i.e., attacker success has a hard limit set by $B$. The authors propose to break this limit with deep reinforcement learning: the attacker is given the *additional objective of avoiding the defender*, is trained by TD3 [22] against the optimal point-capture defense, and thereby learns maneuvers that exploit the defender's turn-rate limitations in close-proximity states. The work is positioned as the first artificial-intelligence study of the TAD variant in which the attacker actively evades the defender while pursuing the target.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
**Problem** (Sec. I, p. 366; Sec. III, p. 369): in the planar TAD game with simple motion, equal attacker/defender speed (normalized to 1) and target speed ratio $\alpha = V_T/V_A < 1$, design an attacking guidance policy $\pi(s) \mapsto \Delta\chi$ that captures the target while avoiding the defender, when the target and defender play the optimal point-capture strategies of [6] (Eqs. (4)–(6) in $R_e$, Eqs. (8)–(13) in $R_c$), all agents being turn-rate constrained. The game is framed as an MDP; the defender's position must enter the attacker's observation, reward, and termination conditions so that "avoid the defender" is learnable.

**Gap as claimed by the authors**:
1. [authors' claim, p. 366] Guidance laws exist for TAD variants (faster defender [23], constrained-maneuverable defender [24], nonzero capture radius [25]), "but the case where the attacker actively avoids the defender in pursuit of the target has not yet been studied using artificial intelligence."
2. [authors' claim, p. 368] Prior RL applications to pursuit/evasion — Q-learning in multi-agent pursuit–evasion [33], a learned TAD defender policy against a PN attacker [27], PPO for angle-only interception [34], and DDPG policies for all three TAD agents [35] — "did not involve training an attacker to avoid a defender in capturing its target, and the research where the optimal point capture solutions to the TAD differential game were considered only sought to match their performance" (referring to [35], which trained in alternating batches to *match* the guidance laws of [1,8]).
3. [authors' claim, p. 366] Existing optimal results "guarantee that the attacking agent is intercepted by the defender in a defined set of cases" — i.e., the barrier limits attacker success; the paper aims to expand the winning set beyond that boundary.

**Verified from cited text (as far as the printed reference entries and this paper's reproduction of them allow)**: [13] (Pachter, Garcia, Casbeer, *Dynamic Games and Applications* 9(1):165–216, 2019) is indeed the unconstrained-heading Active Target Defense Differential Game solution whose reduced-space barrier and optimal headings this paper restates (Eqs. (2)–(6)); [6] (Garcia, Casbeer, Pachter, *DGA* 9(3):652–670, 2019) is the pursuit-in-presence-of-a-defender paper supplying the $R_c$ capture Game-of-Degree aimpoint construction (Eqs. (7)–(13)); [14] is the ACC 2018 optimal target-capture companion. The claim that these works leave the defender-avoiding attacker unstudied is consistent with their titles/scopes. Caveat: the claim that *no* AI work treats a defender-avoiding attacker is the authors' literature assessment; this archive did not re-survey the ML literature of 2019–2021 to falsify it. Note also the mismatch in problem classes: [6,13,14] solve the unconstrained-heading game, while this paper trains under turn-rate constraints (Eqs. (23)–(24), after [24]) — so the learned policy does not "beat" the unconstrained-game barrier in the same game; it beats the *turn-rate-limited implementation* of the optimal defense, a distinction the paper only partially acknowledges (p. 373: "the escape/capture surface B is still present in the turn rate constrained version of the TAD game").

## 4. Core Contributions
1. **Modeling (MDP formulation)**: the TAD engagement framed as an MDP whose 9-dimensional state includes the defender's full pose (Eqs. (19)–(20)), with a one-dimensional continuous action $\Delta\chi$ (Eq. (21)), explicit-Euler simple-motion transitions (Eq. (22)), turn-rate bounds (Eqs. (23)–(24)), and terminal sets $\Gamma_{Win}, \Gamma_{Loss}$ (Eqs. (25)–(26)) (Sec. III, pp. 369–370).
2. **Reward design (algorithm enabler)**: a composite shaped reward (Eqs. (27)–(29)) — terminal win bonus + attacker–target Gaussian attraction − defender-proximity Gaussian penalty − heading-alignment penalty $\kappa\delta$ − constant drain $\iota$ — that makes defender avoidance learnable without a binary-only signal (Sec. III, p. 370, following the Gaussian-shaping practice of [45]).
3. **Algorithm (training)**: defender-aware attacker trained with TD3 [22] (clipped double Q-learning, delayed policy updates, target policy smoothing) on a 100-game grid of target initializations, against a defender/target team that replays the optimal point-capture laws of [6] every step under turn-rate saturation (Sec. III pp. 369–370; hyperparameters Table A1, p. 375).
4. **Experiment (performance evidence)**: trained policy wins 97% of the 100 training setups and 88.54% of a ~10,000-case high-resolution test set, versus 26% / 23.70% for the point-capture attacker guidance law; wins in $R_e$ (97.29% train / 86.95% test) are "beyond the previous method's capabilities" (Sec. IV, pp. 371–374, Table 2); case studies (Figs. 8–13) show the learned exploit: force the defender to commit, then out-turn it.
5. **No theory**: the paper proves nothing new; all differential-game content (barrier, regions, optimal headings, Apollonius aimpoint) is restated from [6,13]. The contribution is entirely the MDP/reward/DRL pipeline and its empirical evaluation.

### 5.1 Dynamics, information structure, termination (pp. 366–367, 369–370)
- **Kinematics (p. 366, Eq. (1))**: planar simple motion, velocities normalized by $V_A$: $\dot x_T = \alpha\cos\phi$, $x_T(0)=x_{T0}$; $\dot y_T = \alpha\sin\phi$, $y_T(0)=y_{T0}$; $\dot x_A = \cos\chi$, $x_A(0)=x_{A0}$; $\dot y_A = \sin\chi$; $\dot x_D = \cos\psi$, $x_D(0)=x_{D0}$; $\dot y_D = \sin\psi$. Here $\phi,\chi,\psi$ are target/attacker/defender headings and $\alpha = V_T/V_A < 1$; $V_A = V_D$ (equal-speed assumption, p. 366). The paper asserts 2-D sufficiency "as altitude changes in aerial engagements would be relatively small [28]" (p. 367).
- **State/action/transition (p. 369, Eqs. (19)–(22))**: $s = (x_T, y_T, \phi,\; x_A, y_A, \chi,\; x_D, y_D, \psi) \in S$, $S = \{s \mid s\in\mathbb{R}^9,\ 0 \le \phi,\chi,\psi < 2\pi\}$; action $a = \Delta\chi$ (single real value, the attacker's heading increment); transition by explicit Euler with step $\Delta t$ (Eq. (22)): $x_T' = x_T + \alpha\Delta t\cos\phi$, $y_T' = y_T + \alpha\Delta t\sin\phi$, $x_A' = x_A + \Delta t\cos\chi$, $y_A' = y_A + \Delta t\sin\chi$, $x_D' = x_D + \Delta t\cos\psi$, $y_D' = y_D + \Delta t\sin\psi$. **Information structure**: full perfect observation — the attacker's policy input is the entire 9-vector (p. 370: "When testing the learned policy, the state vector is the only input needed").
- **Turn-rate constraints (p. 369, Eqs. (23)–(24))**: $\Delta\chi_{Max} = \Delta\psi_{Max} = 1.0/d_{MinTurn}$ (attacker, defender) and $\Delta\phi_{Max} = \alpha/d_{MinTurn}$ (target), with $d_{MinTurn}=2.0$. **Unclear step (flag)**: as printed, these are pure reciprocals with no $\Delta t$ factor; whether the bound is per time step (then effective turn rate $= 5$ rad/s for $d_{MinTurn}=2$, $\Delta t = 0.1$, contradicting the kinematic turn radius $r = v/\omega = 2.0$) or a per-unit-time rate to be multiplied by $\Delta t$ (then consistent: $0.5\ \text{rad/s}$) is never stated; the tanh actor output is "multiplied ... with the attacker's maximum turn rate value" (p. 371), leaving the per-step increment convention ambiguous. This matters when reproducing the experiments.
- **Termination (pp. 369–370, Eqs. (25)–(26))**: $\Gamma = \{s \mid \sqrt{(x_A-x_T)^2+(y_A-y_T)^2} < d_{Capture}\ \text{or}\ \sqrt{(x_A-x_D)^2+(y_A-y_D)^2} < d_{Capture}\}$; $\Gamma_{Win} = \{s \mid d_{AT} < d_{Capture}\}$, $\Gamma_{Loss} = \{s \mid d_{AD} < d_{Capture}\} - \Gamma_{Win}$, $\Gamma = \Gamma_{Win}\cup\Gamma_{Loss}$. Capture radius $d_{Capture}=0.2$ (Table 1, p. 370). **Tie-breaking favors the attacker**: "The simulation environment checked for a violation of the capture radius $d_{Capture}$ between the attacker and the target before the defender for the terminal game state" (p. 371, spanning to p. 373) — at three-agent convergence the attacker wins ties; the paper attributes boundary-case losses to the $\Gamma_{Loss}$ outcomes of small pursuit errors (p. 373).

### 5.2 Reproduced barrier and winning regions (p. 367, Eqs. (2)–(3), (7); attributed to [6])
- **Barrier (p. 367, Eq. (2))**: in the reduced state space (transformation $y_A = 0$, $y_D = 0$, $x_D = -x_A$, p. 367),
$$B = \Big\{(x_A, x_T, y_T)\ \big|\ x_A > 0,\ x_T > 0,\ x_A^2 + \frac{y_T^2}{1-\alpha^2} - \frac{x_T^2}{\alpha^2} = 0\Big\}.$$
  (Signs verified against the rendered page.) Physical meaning: surface in the reduced 3-space separating target-survival from target-capture configurations; it depends only on attacker/defender positions (through $x_A$) and $\alpha$. *Check*: for $A_0=(7,0)$, $D_0=(-7,0)$, $\alpha = 0.55$ (Fig. 1, p. 367), the zero set in $(x_T,y_T)$ is $x_T^2 = \alpha^2 x_A^2 + \frac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha^2}y_T^2$, giving $x_T = 3.85$ at $y_T=0$ and $x_T \approx 7.6$ at $y_T = \pm 10$ — matches the plotted curve.
- **Region of escape (p. 367, Eq. (3))**: $R_e = \{(x_A,x_T,y_T)\mid x_A\ge 0, x_T\ge 0, x_A^2 + \frac{y_T^2}{1-\alpha^2} - \frac{x_T^2}{\alpha^2} > 0\}\ \tilde\times\ \cup\ \{(x_A,x_T,y_T)\mid x_A\ge 0, x_T\le 0\}$ — i.e., the $>$-side of $B$ plus the whole half-space $x_T \le 0$ (defender's side). *Typo*: a stray "$\times$" is printed before "$\cup$" (verified on the page image); the intended set is the union.
- **Region of capture (p. 368, Eq. (7))**: $R_c = \{(x_A,x_T,y_T)\mid x_A>0, x_T>0, x_A^2 + \frac{y_T^2}{1-\alpha^2} - \frac{x_T^2}{\alpha^2} < 0\}$ — the $<$-side of $B$. Games initializing in $R_e$ follow the Active Target Defense Differential Game solution [13]; games in $R_c$ follow the "TAD capture Game of Degree [6]" (p. 368).

### 5.3 Reproduced optimal play in $R_e$ (p. 367, Eqs. (4)–(6); from [13,6])
- **Quartic for the aimpoint ordinate (p. 367, Eq. (4))**: "The following quadratic Eq. (4), has two real solutions, $y_1$ and $y_2$, where $y_1 \le y_T \le y_2$" (sic — it is a quartic in $y$; "quadratic" is a misnomer, the paper means quadratic in structure/quartic in degree):
$$(1-\alpha^2)y^4 - 2(1-\alpha^2)y_T y^3 + \big[(1-\alpha^2)y_T^2 + x_A^2 - \alpha^2 x_T^2\big]y^2 - 2x_A^2 y_T y + x_A^2 y_T^2 = 0.$$
  Decisive structural fact: the bracket $[(1-\alpha^2)y_T^2 + x_A^2 - \alpha^2 x_T^2] = (1-\alpha^2)\big[x_A^2 + \frac{y_T^2}{1-\alpha^2} - \frac{x_T^2}{\alpha^2}\big]$ is exactly $(1-\alpha^2)\times$ (the Eq. (2) barrier expression), so the quartic degenerates precisely on $B$ — internal consistency between Eqs. (2) and (4).
- **Optimal headings (p. 367, Eq. (5))**: with $y = y_1$ if $x_T \le 0$ and $y = y_2$ if $x_T > 0$,
$$\cos\phi^* = \pm\frac{x_T}{\sqrt{x_T^2+(y_T-y)^2}},\quad \sin\phi^* = \pm\frac{y_T-y}{\sqrt{x_T^2+(y_T-y)^2}}\ \ (x_T \ne 0);$$
$$\cos\chi^* = -\frac{x_A}{\sqrt{x_A^2+y^2}},\quad \sin\chi^* = \frac{y}{\sqrt{x_A^2+y^2}};\qquad \cos\psi^* = \frac{x_A}{\sqrt{x_A^2+y^2}},\quad \sin\psi^* = \frac{y}{\sqrt{x_A^2+y^2}}.$$
  Interpretation: attacker and defender head to the interception point $(0, y)$ on the $y$-axis of the reduced frame (opposite $x$-signs, same $y$-component), and the target heads to the same point — all three fly direct lines to one aimpoint. **Unclear step (flag)**: the $\pm$ on the target's components is printed but never resolved; the geometrically consistent choice is the sign combination pointing from $(x_T,y_T)$ toward $(0,y)$, i.e. $\cos\phi^* = -x_T/\sqrt{\cdot}$, $\sin\phi^* = (y-y_T)/\sqrt{\cdot}$; the printed $+$ branch would point away from the aimpoint.
- **Special case $x_T = 0$ (p. 367, Eq. (6))**: $\displaystyle \phi^* = \frac{\sqrt{x_T^2 + (1-\alpha^2)y_T^2}}{\alpha y_T} + \pi/2$ **as printed** (verified at 500 dpi: no inverse-trig wrapper). **Proof gap / typo**: a dimensionless ratio (for $x_T=0$: $\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}/\alpha = 1.52$ at $\alpha = 0.55$) is added to an angle; the formula is dimensionally inconsistent as an angle in radians, and an $\arccos$ reading is impossible since the argument exceeds 1. The correct form in the source [13] evidently differs (missing inverse-trig or different bracketing); anyone re-implementing Eq. (6) must recover it from [13].
- Worked illustration: optimal play in $R_e$ for $T_0=(2.30, 4.87)$, $A_0=(7.0,0.0)$, $D_0=(-7.0,0.0)$, $\alpha=0.55$ (Fig. 2, p. 368).

### 5.4 Reproduced capture Game of Degree in $R_c$ (p. 368, Eqs. (8)–(13); from [6])
- **Apollonius circle (attacker vs target) (p. 368, Eqs. (8)–(9))**: center and radius
$$x_c = \frac{1}{1-\alpha^2}x_T - \frac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha^2}x_A,\qquad y_c = \frac{1}{1-\alpha^2}y_T;\qquad l = \Big(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha^2}\Big)\sqrt{(x_A-x_T)^2 + (y_A-y_T)^2}.$$
  (Standard Apollonius geometry for speeds $1$ vs $\alpha$; "simplifies to" checks: $\alpha^2[(x-x_A)^2+(y-y_A)^2] = (x-x_T)^2+(y-y_T)^2$ yields exactly this center/radius.)
- **Aimpoint (p. 368, Eqs. (10)–(11))**: $\quad I^* = (l\cos\theta^* + x_c,\; l\sin\theta^* + y_c)$, with $\theta^* = \arccos \mathrm{Re}(v^*)$, where $v^*$ is "the solution to Eq. (12) that minimizes Eq. (13)". **Unclear step (flag)**: the map $v \mapsto \theta$ is never stated; implicitly $v = e^{i\theta}$ on the unit circle (which is why taking the real part recovers $\cos\theta^*$), and which root of the quartic is admissible/which of the minimizing candidates is chosen is unspecified.
- **Complex quartic (p. 368, Eq. (12))**:
$$\Big[x_c y_c + \frac{i}{2}(x_c^2 - x_A^2 - y_c^2)\Big]v^4 + l(y_c + i x_c)v^3 + l(y_c - i x_c)v + x_c y_c - \frac{i}{2}(x_c^2 - x_A^2 - y_c^2) = 0$$
  (no $v^2$ term; conjugate-symmetric coefficients — roots come in conjugate pairs; solved numerically with the NumPy roots solver, p. 370).
- **Selection criterion (p. 368, Eq. (13))**:
$$M(v) = \sqrt{(l\cos v + x_c + x_A)^2 + (l\sin v + y_c)^2}\; -\; \sqrt{(l\cos v + x_c - x_A)^2 + (l\sin v + y_c)^2},$$
  i.e. $M(v) = d_D(I^*(v)) - d_A(I^*(v))$ in the reduced frame ($D = (-x_A, 0)$, $A = (x_A,0)$). Minimizing $M$ "us[es] this strategy, the defender is as close as possible to the attacker when the target is captured, allowing the defender to have the highest potential of intercepting the attacker when a suboptimal attacking guidance method is being used" (p. 368). So in $R_c$ the defense team's game-of-degree role is a punishment-posture selection: since capture cannot be prevented, the target/defender headings (Eq. (5)-style lines to $I^*$) place the defender optimally against any attacker deviation. In this paper's training environment the *target and defender* run these laws (p. 369: "The headings of the target and defender, $\phi$ and $\psi$, respectively, are determined by the optimal point capture solutions from [6], with Eqs. (4–6) being used when the target is in $R_e$ and Eqs. (8–13) when the target is in $R_c$"), re-evaluated each step after the reduced-space transform; the attacker alone is the learning agent. Worked illustration with a *different* configuration from training: $T_0=(10.0, 5.55)$, $A_0=(4.5,0.0)$, $D_0=(-4.5,0.0)$, $\alpha = 0.35$ (Fig. 3, p. 368).

### 5.5 Paper's own result: defender-aware MDP reward design (p. 370, Eqs. (27)–(29)) — the core contribution
- **Total reward (p. 370, Eq. (27))**: $r(s) = 10$ if $s\in\Gamma_{Win}$, else $r_{shaping}(s)$ — note the shaping term is also delivered at loss states and at every non-terminal step.
- **Shaping reward (p. 370, Eq. (28), simplified Eq. (29))**:
$$r_{shaping}(s) = \beta_{AT} e^{-\left(\sqrt{(x_A-x_T)^2+(y_A-y_T)^2}\right)^2/2\sigma_{AT}^2} - \Big(\beta_{AD} e^{-\left(\sqrt{(x_A-x_D)^2+(y_A-y_D)^2}\right)^2/2\sigma_{AD}^2} + \kappa\delta + \iota\Big)$$
  (Eq. (28)), simplified to $r_{shaping}(s) = \beta_{AT} e^{-[(x_A-x_T)^2+(y_A-y_T)^2/2\sigma_{AT}^2]} - (\beta_{AD} e^{-[(x_A-x_D)^2+(y_A-y_D)^2/2\sigma_{AD}^2]} + \kappa\delta + \iota)$ (Eq. (29)). Components as stated (p. 370): the target Gaussian rewards decreasing attacker–target distance; the defender Gaussian (subtracted inside the parenthesis) penalizes proximity to the defender; $\delta$ = difference between the attacker's heading and the attacker→target angle, penalized through $-\kappa\delta$ to smooth the path; $-\iota$ is a fixed per-step drain preventing reward accumulation far from the target. **How the defender enters the policy — explicit summary**: (i) observation $(x_D, y_D, \psi)$ inside $s$ (Eq. (19)); (ii) the $-\beta_{AD}e^{-d_{AD}^2/2\sigma_{AD}^2}$ penalty term (Eqs. (28)–(29)); (iii) the absorbing loss condition $d_{AD} < d_{Capture}$ (Eq. (26)); (iv) indirectly, the defender's executed heading is the optimal law of [6] under turn-rate saturation, so the TD3 policy can (and does, see Sec. 6) learn to bait saturation. **Typographical flag on Eq. (29)**: the "simplified" exponent prints the $2\sigma^2$ under the $y$-term only, whereas Eq. (28) shows the intended $e^{-d^2/2\sigma^2}$; Eq. (29) should be read as $e^{-[(x_A-x_T)^2+(y_A-y_T)^2]/(2\sigma_{AT}^2)}$.
- **Hyperparameters (p. 370 example for Fig. 4)**: $\beta_{AT} = 1.0$, $\sigma_{AT} = 10$, $\beta_{AD} = 0.9$, $\sigma_{AD} = 2.0$, $\iota = 0.3$ (with $\delta = 0$ assumed; $\kappa$ not listed for the example). **Final training values (p. 375, Table A1)**: $\beta_{AT} = 20.6\mathrm{e}{-2} = 0.206$, $\sigma_{AT} = 20.0$, $\beta_{AD} = 15.0\mathrm{e}{-2} = 0.15$, $\sigma_{AD} = 0.2$, $\kappa = 3.7\mathrm{e}{-2} = 0.037$, $\iota = 13.6\mathrm{e}{-2} = 0.136$. The two sets disagree (notably $\sigma_{AD}$: 2.0 vs 0.2 — the final defense penalty is 100× sharper), and the Fig. 4 text "the attacker is located at $(-7.0, 0.0)$" is a mislabel: $(-7,0)$ is the *defender's* position in every scenario (Table 1); Fig. 4's surface is the reward over attacker positions on the $x$–$y$ plane with the target fixed at $(2.30, 4.87)$ and the defender fixed at $(-7,0)$ (verified on the figure: reward minimum $\approx -0.6$ centered exactly on the blue defender marker).
- **Learning machinery (pp. 369–370, Eqs. (14)–(18), (30)–(31))**: standard background, not results — Q-policy $\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)$ (Eq. (14)) with TD update $Q'(s,a) = Q(s,a) + \eta[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)]$ (Eq. (15)); deterministic policy parameterization $\pi_\rho(s) = \rho^\top s$ (Eq. (16)) and gradient $\nabla_\rho J(\rho) = \frac{1}{N}\sum \nabla_a Q(s,a)|_{a=\pi_\rho(s)}\nabla_\rho\pi_\rho(s)$ (Eq. (17)); Gaussian shaping template $r_{Gaussian}(s) = \beta e^{-d(s,s_g)^2/2\sigma^2}$ attributed to [45] (Eq. (18)); TD3 improvements over DDPG (clipped double Q [41], delayed policy updates, target policy smoothing) described qualitatively (p. 369); ReLU hidden activation (Eq. (30)) and tanh actor output (Eq. (31)) scaled by the max turn rate (p. 371).

### 5.6 Extensibility of the analytic results
The reproduced TAD block (Eqs. (1)–(13)) inherits the full generality of [6,13] within its assumptions (2-D, simple motion, $V_A = V_D$, point capture, unrestricted headings for the *derivation*). The MDP/reward block is dimension-agnostic in form: any change of $\alpha$, $d_{Capture}$, $d_{MinTurn}$, initial geometry or velocity set merely "would lead to a different positioning of the escape/capture surface $B$, and different agent behavior from the learned policy" (p. 370) — i.e., the policy must be retrained per problem family; the reward template, not the policy, is the transferable artifact.

## 6. Simulation and Experiments
- **Platform**: Python simulation environment; optimal point-capture solutions computed with the NumPy polynomial roots solver; PyTorch actor/critic networks; TD3 implementation forked from the authors' repository (github.com/sfujim/TD3, "the TD3 algorithm itself was not altered", Acknowledgments p. 375). Training on a CUDA-enabled GeForce GTX 1050 Ti, Intel Core i7 CPU, 32 GB RAM; total 5 h (p. 371).
- **Scenario (Sec. III, pp. 369–370)**: attacker trained on 100 games — a $10\times10$ grid of equally spaced target initial positions with $x_{T0}, y_{T0}\in[-10,10]$; fixed $A_0 = (7.0, 0.0)$, $D_0 = (-7.0, 0.0)$ for every engagement ("Training was performed with fixed relations of the attacker to defender with the target initial condition changing to isolate the problem and allow for fixed relation scaling"; the phrase "fixed unit distance" on p. 370 is inconsistent with the actual 14-unit $A$–$D$ separation). Starting headings of all three agents set from the optimal solutions of [6] ("ensuring that the optimal defensive strategy was initially attainable"); at each step the defensive team's controls are recomputed from the optimal laws and all states advance by Eq. ## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated**:
1. Single, fixed problem family: $\alpha, d_{Capture}, d_{MinTurn}, \Delta t, A_0, D_0$ held fixed and admitted to be "chosen arbitrarily"; changing any value repositions $B$ and changes learned behavior (p. 370) — no cross-parameter generalization is claimed or tested.
2. Boundary cases remain hard: initializations near $B$ (all three agents converge) and near-head-on approaches in $R_e$ (failure band around $y=0$, Fig. 7) are where the policy loses (pp. 371, 373).
3. Reward-hacking artifact: the anomaly at $(6.0, 0.0)$ — game prolongation to accumulate shaping reward (pp. 373–374) — and the loss of coverage with longer training (p. 371) show the shaped reward is not aligned everywhere; hyperparameters were "manually tuned" (p. 370).
4. No sampling-optimality guarantee: "Given an infinite amount of sampling ... reinforcement learning techniques will converge to an optimal policy ... In practice, guaranteeing such sampling of the state space is a complex issue [46]" (p. 370).

**Critical observations (this archive, with page/eq. anchors)**:
5. *Adversary is a fixed script*: the defender/target replay the unconstrained-heading optimal laws of [6] under turn-rate saturation; the learned attacker is a best response to that specific opponent, not a game-theoretic strategy for the turn-rate-constrained TAD game. No robustness to other defender laws (PN, heuristic, or re-optimized constrained defense [5,24]) is tested, so "expands the number of cases where the target is captured" (p. 366) holds only within this environment.
6. *Barrier claim needs qualification*: wins "outside of the optimal point capture guidance laws' limited region" (p. 366) partly follow from imposing turn-rate limits on the defender that the barrier derivation never assumed; the paper itself notes $B$ "is still present" in the constrained game (p. 373) but does not recompute what the correct barrier would be for the turn-rate-constrained game.
7. *Turn-rate convention ambiguity*: Eqs. (23)–(24) omit $\Delta t$; per-step vs per-unit-time readings differ by 10× in effective turn rate and change whether the simulated kinematics match $d_{MinTurn}=2.0$ (Sec. 5.1 flag).
8. *Printed-equation defects to fix before reuse*: Eq. (3) stray "$\times$" before "$\cup$" (p. 367); Eq. (4) called "quadratic" but is quartic (p. 367); Eq. (5) unresolved "$\pm$" on the target heading (p. 367); Eq. (6) dimensionally impossible as printed — likely a dropped inverse-trig wrapper (p. 367); Eq. (29) exponent typography (p. 370); "attacker is located at $(-7.0,0.0)$" should read "defender" (p. 370); "achieved bt" → "by" and "would resulted in" (p. 366); "1e + 3" test cases vs 2370/10,000 (pp. 371, 373); "fixed unit distance" vs actual $A$–$D$ separation 14 (p. 370).
9. *Unstated environment details*: no episode horizon, no replay-buffer size, single run/seed, no variance estimates for the headline 97%/88.54% numbers; attacker-favoring tie-break in the terminal check (pp. 371→373) inflates wins at three-agent convergence.
10. *Reward design weaknesses visible in their own data*: with final $\sigma_{AT}=20$ over a $[-10,10]^2$ space the attraction term is nearly flat (explains the prolongation anomaly), and the loss-state reward is just the shaping value (max defense penalty $-\beta_{AD} = -0.15$), i.e., losing is penalized mostly through the foregone +10 and the per-step $-\iota$; no shaping-theory invariance analysis (cf. [43]) is offered.
11. *Perfect full-state observation*: the policy consumes exact 9-D state including defender heading; no sensing/latency model, and the 2-D, equal-speed, point-capture abstractions inherit all classical TAD caveats (p. 367 argues 2-D sufficiency via [28]).

## 8. Citation Value
Role in the literature: this is the bridge paper between the analytical TAD canon ([6,8,13,14] — barrier, Apollonius aimpoints, cooperative defense) and learning-based attack guidance; it is the earliest-known-to-this-archive demonstration that a reward-shaped TD3 attacker, observing the defender's full state and punished for defender proximity, can convert the defender's turn-rate constraint into additional capture wins on both sides of the classical escape/capture barrier (26% → 88% overall in the studied family). Its simulation protocol (optimal defense as the environment, grid-based $T_0$ sweeps, win-coverage tables split by $R_e/R_c$, minimum $d_{AD}$ maps) is a reusable evaluation template for defender-aware attackers, and its failure modes (boundary convergence, near-head-on mirroring, reward-prolongation anomaly) are documented starting points for follow-up work.
Suggested citation sentences: "English and Wilhelm [P026] trained a TD3 agent, whose reward penalizes proximity to the defender through a Gaussian term and whose observation includes the defender's state, and showed that the learned attacker captures the target in a large fraction of configurations that the optimal point-capture attacker guidance law cannot win, by exploiting the defender's turn-rate limits in close proximity." Or, for gap statements: "Defender-aware attacking guidance for the TAD game has been addressed with deep reinforcement learning [P026], but only against a fixed optimal point-capture defense in a single parameterized scenario, leaving equilibrium-quality and cross-scenario robustness open."
