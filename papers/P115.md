## 2. Research Background
Game theory is the standard paradigm for modeling strategic interactions among selfish players since von Neumann and Nash [refs. 1–3 of the paper], with applications in economics, biology, finance, computer science, and politics, and a documented role in national defense (U.S. security-control agencies). The survey focuses on **adversarial games**, where players deliberately compete and inflict loss on each other, motivated by poker, chess, evader pursuit, drug interdiction, coast-guard patrolling, cybersecurity, and national defense. The authors list five main features of adversarial settings: (1) hardness of efficient/fast algorithm design under limited computing resources and samples; (2) **imperfect information** (private information hidden from others, e.g., poker); (3) **large models** — large action spaces/infosets, e.g., road-network security games with action spaces of order 10^18 [their ref. 12]; (4) **incomplete information** (an agent does not know which game is being played — number of players, strategy sets, payoffs); and (5) possible **dynamic traits** (time-varying games, e.g., seasonal poacher behavior). They carefully distinguish imperfect from incomplete information, note that not all adversarial games are informationally deficient (Go is perfect and complete, solved by AlphaGo/AlphaZero), and mention bounded rationality as an additional feature.

(These summaries are the authors'; each entry's content summary is [authors' claim] unless checked against the cited survey.)
- **Scope choice [authors' claim]**: the survey systematizes **three main game models** — (i) zero-sum normal-form and extensive-form games, (ii) Stackelberg (security) games, (iii) zero-sum differential games (DGs) — which "are not mutually exclusive" (SGs and DGs can be zero-sum). Bayesian, Markov/stochastic, signaling, behavioral, and evolutionary games are explicitly **excluded** as each "is of independent interest."
- **Taxonomy (Section 3 of the paper)**:
  - *Zero-sum games (ZSGs)* (Sec. 3.1): (1) bilinear games; (2) saddle point problems (SPPs)/min-max optimization; (3) multi-player zero-sum games (MP-ZSGs); (4) team games (TGs); (5) imperfect-information ZSGs (II-ZSGs).
  - *Stackelberg games* (Sec. 3.2): (1) GSGs; (2) SSGs (homogeneous/heterogeneous resources, schedule size); (3) continuous SGs; (4) incomplete-information SGs.
  - *Zero-sum DGs* (Sec. 3.3): (1) linear-quadratic (LQ) DGs; (2) nonlinear DGs; (3) Stackelberg DGs; (4) stochastic DGs; (5) terminal-time/state-constrained DGs.
- **Trends identified**: convergence of game theory with machine learning (GANs, MARL, PSRO/meta-solvers, meta-learning auto-curricula); dominance of regret-based methods for imperfect-information games (Cepheus, DeepStack, Libratus, Pluribus lineage); bilevel/MILP machinery for security games; HJI/viscosity-solution machinery for DGs; growing interest in last-iterate convergence, bandit feedback, and online/time-varying games.
- **Open problems**: eight explicit future directions (see Section 7 below).
- **Complexity facts [verified as printed; attribution to cited papers is the authors']**: computing NE is PPAD-complete for κ-player (κ≥3) zero-sum and even two-player nonzero-sum games [29–31]; (C)CE is polynomial-time in all NFGs [32,55]; TME is FNP-hard and additively inapproximable [37,107,108]; GSG with one follower is polynomial, with multiple followers NP-hard [43]; zero-sum DG NE is "computationally intractable in general" [46].

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (MOST IMPORTANT — exhaustive catalog)

### 5.1 Game models and equilibrium concepts (Sec. 2)
- **NFG (Sec. 2.1)**: tuple (N, A, u), N:=[n]; A=A_1×⋯×A_n; u_i:A_i→R; mixed strategy π_i∈Δ(A_i); expected utility E_{a∼π}[u_i(a)]; social welfare SW(a):=Σ_i u_i(a); constant-sum if Σ_i u_i(a)=c_s, zero-sum if c_s=0. Continuous action sets (closed, convex) → continuous games.
- **II-EFG (Sec. 2.1)**: tuple (N, H, Z, A, P, μ, u, I) with histories H, terminal nodes Z, chance player c, player function P, infoset partition I_i of H_i with identical action sets per infoset; perfect recall formally defined via prefix-order histories; normal-form plan a_i∈Ξ_i=×_{I_{i,j}∈I_i}A(I_{i,j}); behavioral strategy π_i; reach probability p^π(h); expected payoff u_i(π)=Σ_{h∈Z} p^π(h)u_i(h). Best response BR(π_{−i}):=argmax_{π'_i} u_i(π'_i,π_{−i}). **Exploitability** e(π_i):=u_i(π*_i,π*_{−i})−u_i(π_i,BR(π_i)); multiplayer total exploitability/NashConv e(π):=Σ_{i∈[n]}[u_i(BR(π_{−i}),π_{−i})−u_i(π_i,π_{−i})] [their ref. 26], average exploitability e(π)/|N|.
- **Def. 1, ε-NE (eq. (1))**: u_i(π*_i,π*_{−i}) ⩾ u_i(π_i,π*_{−i}) − ε, ∀π_i, i∈N; NE at ε=0. Existence in mixed strategies for finite games [Nash, their ref. 2].
- **Def. 2, ε-CE (eq. (2))**: distribution μ over ×_i A_i with E_{a∼μ}[u_i(a_i,a_{−i})] ⩾ E_{a∼μ}[u_i(φ_i(a_i),a_{−i})] − ε for every swap function φ_i [Aumann, ref. 32].
- **Def. 3, ε-CCE (eq. (3))**: same without conditioning on the advised action: E_{a∼μ}[u_i(a_i,a_{−i})] ⩾ E_{a∼μ}[u_i(a'_i,a_{−i})] − ε ∀a'_i [Hannan, ref. 33]. **Assessment**: NE, CE, CCE payoff-equivalent in two-player zero-sum games via the minimax theorem [ref. 34]; CE/CCE extensions to EFGs [refs. 35,36] exist but are "less investigated."
- **Zero-sum single-team single-adversary TG (Sec. 2.1)**: team T={1,…,n−1}, u_i=u_j ∀i,j∈T, u_n=−u_T=−Σ_{i∈T}u_i. Realization plan r_i:S_i→[0,1] with r_i(∅)=1, Σ_{a∈A(I_{i,j})} r_i(s_i,a)=r_i(s_i), r_i(s'_i)⩾0 (eq. (4)). **Def. 4, TME (eq. (5))** [von Stengel–Koller, ref. 38; Celli–Gatti, ref. 37]: argmax_{r_1,…,r_{n−1}} min_{r_n} Σ_{s=×_{i∈N}s_i, s∈S} U_T(s)·Π_{i=1}^n r_i(s_i), with team utility U_T(s):=Σ_{l∈Z'}u_T(l)c(l) if some terminal is achieved, 0 otherwise. Assessment: TME generally unique, is an NE maximizing team utility; ε-TME analogously defined.
- **GSG (Sec. 2.2)**: leader commits x∈Δ(A_l); p followers respond with pure strategies (rational followers need only pure strategies [ref. 43]); type probabilities ϖ_k∈[0,1]; utility pair (R^k, C^k) per follower; expected utilities U_l(x,q)=Σ_{i∈A_l}Σ_{j∈A_f}Σ_{k∈F} ϖ_k x_i q^k_j R^k_{ij} (eq. (6)); U^k_f(x,q^k)=Σ_{i∈A_l}Σ_{j∈A_f} x_i q^k_j C^k_{ij} (eq. (7)).
- **SSG (Sec. 2.2)**: m<n security resources covering n targets; leader's pure strategies = all subsets of ≤m targets; coverage probability c_j=Σ_{i∈A_l, j∈i} x_i; defender utilities D^k_c(j) (covered) / D^k_u(j) (uncovered), attacker utilities A^k_c(j)/A^k_u(j), with orderings D^k_c(j)⩾D^k_u(j) and A^k_u(j)⩾A^k_c(j); expected utilities (eqs. (8)–(9)): U_d(x,q)=Σ_{j∈A_f}Σ_{k∈F} ϖ_k q^k_j[c_j D^k_c(j)+(1−c_j)D^k_u(j)], U^k_a(x,q^k)=Σ_{j∈A_f} q^k_j[c_j A^k_c(j)+(1−c_j)A^k_u(j)].
- **Def. 5, SSE (Sec. 2.2)**: profile (x*, {q^{k*}}_{k∈F}) with (1) leader optimality (x*)^⊤R^k q^{k*} ⩾ x^⊤R^k ℛ^k(x) ∀x∈Δ(A_l), ∀k∈F (ℛ^k(x) = follower k's best-response set — calligraphic operator verified from rendered p. 7); (2) follower best response (x*)^⊤C^k q^{k*} ⩾ (x*)^⊤C^k q^k ∀feasible q^k; (3) follower tie-breaking in favor of the leader (x*)^⊤R^k q^{k*} ⩾ (x*)^⊤R^k ℛ^k(x*). **Assessment**: SSE always exists in all SGs [refs. 42,44]; tie-breaking is justified by ε-perturbation of the leader's commitment [ref. 45]; the weak Stackelberg equilibrium (WSE, tie-breaking favoring followers) does **not** always exist [ref. 46].
- **TP-ZS-DG (Sec. 2.3)**: dynamics ż(t)=f(t,z(t),u(t),v(t)), t∈[t_0,T], z(t_0)=z_0, u∈U⊆R^{m_1}, v∈V⊆R^{m_2} (eq. (10)); essential cost J(u(·),v(·))=∫_{t_0}^T f_0(t,z,u,v)dt + φ(z(T)) (eq. (11)); player 1 minimizes / player 2 maximizes: min_{u∈U} max_{v∈V} J (eq. (12)); optimal cost = **value function** ψ(t,z); solution = NE, also called minimax/saddle-point equilibrium. Multi-player extension ż=f(t,z,u_1,…,u_N), J_i=∫f_i+φ_i, zero-sum if ΣJ_i≡0; stochastic DGs via SDEs with Brownian motion [refs. 53,54,49]; standard assumptions: compactness of U,V, Lipschitz continuity of f, f_0, φ [ref. 50].

### 5.3 ZSG research landscape (Sec. 3.1)
- **Bilinear games [56]**: payoffs x^⊤Ay, x^⊤By; strategy sets X:={x∈R^m: R_1x=r_1, x∈R^m_+}, Y analogous; zero-sum when B=−A; **rank of game = rank(A+B)**. Special cases: bimatrix games [57–59], imitation games (B=I) [60], Colonel Blotto [61]; multi-player polymatrix games reduce to bilinear [62,56]. Representative complexity results quoted: NE in two-player nonzero-sum with rank ⩾2 is PPAD-hard [67,68]; 1/n_s^c-approximate NE is PPAD-hard even for imitation games for any c>0 (n_s = number of moves) yet a polynomial-time approximate-NE algorithm exists [60]; tree polymatrix with 20 actions per player is PPAD-hard [65]; polynomial-time 1/3-approximate NE for bimatrix games [64] called "state-of-the-art"; efficient/simple Blotto algorithms [69–71] and extensions (dynamic [72], generalized/lottery [73], multi-player [71,74]). Hidden bilinear games [75]: player inputs pass through a smooth hidden layer before the bilinear game.
- **SPPs [76]**: min_{x∈X} max_{y∈Y} f(x,y), X,Y closed convex. Non-bilinear form f(x)+Θ(x,y)−h(y) [77,78]; bilinear SPP when Θ(x,y)=x^⊤Cy [79–81]. Variants surveyed: Frank–Wolfe/projection-free [82,83]; nonconvex–nonconcave [84,85]; linear last-iterate convergence [86]; adversarial bandits with delays [87]; periodic zero-sum bimatrix with continuous strategies [88]; compositional SPPs [89]; decentralized [90]; functional-form games [91]; hidden general SPPs [92]; min-max Stackelberg games with dependent feasible sets [93].
- **MP-ZSGs**: NE approximation PPAD-complete [29–31]; multiplayer symmetric zero-sum games may have only asymmetric equilibria [94]. Polymatrix (network matrix) games: generalized minimax theorem ⇒ convexity of equilibria, polynomial-time tractability, no-regret convergence [95]; last-iterate results [96]; O(1/T) time-average convergence by alternating gradient descent [97]; time-average failure but Poincaré recurrence for continuous-time dynamics in periodic polymatrix games / replicator dynamics [98,99]; team/alliance formation as social dilemma [100]; also general-sum multi-player [101–103] and ML-based studies [104].
- **TGs**: one-team (adversarial) [105] vs two-team [106]; TME computation FNP-hard and additively inapproximable [107,108]; efficient TME algorithms for perfect-information zero-sum NFGs [105]; two-team perfect-information normal-form NE-finding is **CLS-hard** [106]; two-network zero-sum games [109–111]; imperfect-information team games: one-team [37,112,113], two-member team [114], team correlated equilibrium in two-team games [115].
- **II-ZSGs**: heads-up limit Texas Hold'em > 10^14 infosets essentially solved by **Cepheus** [117]; no-limit > 10^161 infosets; **DeepStack** [118] and **Libratus** [119] first to beat professionals. Techniques: pruning (regret-based pruning, dynamic thresholding, best-response pruning) [124], abstraction (information/action, bucketing infosets) [125], search (depth-limited solving) [123,126]; poker as sequential Bayesian extensive game [127]; NFG↔EFG bridge via kernelized multiplicative weights with last-iterate convergence, lower size dependence, constant regret [128]; bandit feedback [129–131]; multi-player: **Pluribus**, first multi-player poker agent beating top humans in six-player no-limit [132,133], plus [130,134–137]; RL/DNN approaches [118,131,138–149]; pointer to AI-in-games survey [150]; related: general-sum imperfect-information with full/bandit feedback [151–153], two-player zero-sum Markov games [154], multi-player general-sum Markov games [155].
- **Bridge to deep learning [authors' claim, refs. 156,157]**: zero-sum game theory helps model/resolve deep-learning problems, e.g., polymatrix games in semi-supervised learning (graph transduction).

### 5.4 SG research landscape (Sec. 3.2)
- **GSGs**: one follower → polynomial; multiple followers → NP-hard [43]; methods based on LP/MILP (cutting plane, enumerative, hybrid [158,159]); GSG/SSG as **bilevel optimization** [158–160]; applications: economics [161], smart grid [162,163], wireless networks [164], dynamic inspection [165], IIoT [166]; extensions: multi-leader [167–171], bounded rationality [172], general-sum [173].
- **SSGs**: homogeneous vs heterogeneous resources; **schedule** = number of targets a resource can simultaneously cover [174], size 1 [175] and >1 [176]; Table 5 complexity; applications: wildlife protection [177], airport passenger screening [178], crime prevention [179], cybersecurity [180], information security [181], border patrol [182,183]; extensions: multi-defender [184,185], Bayesian generalizations [186], bounded rationality [187], ambiguous information [188].
- **Continuous SGs**: leader/follower costs f_1, f_2 on Ω=X×Y; bilevel program min_{x∈X}{f_1(x,y): y∈argmin_{y∈Y}f_2(x,y)} (eq. (13)); **Stackelberg equilibrium strategy** [189]: sup_{y∈BR(x*)} f_1(x*,y) ⩽ sup_{y∈BR(x)} f_1(x,y), ∀x (printed "∀x∈X_1"; likely typo for X — verified from render); BR(x)={y∈Y: f_2(x,y)⩽f_2(x,y'), ∀y'∈Y}. Hierarchical Stackelberg v/s Stackelberg games: first general existence result without single-valuedness of the follower equilibrium [190]; NE↔SE connections and Stackelberg gradient dynamics [189]; min-max Stackelberg games with **dependent strategy sets** (constraints g(x,y)⩾0) where the minimax theorem [34] fails [93,191].
- **Incomplete-information SGs**: leader lacks follower information [10]; partial observability of attacker behavior [192]; single-leader-multiple-follower SSG with misperception and deception via **hypergame** framework, strategic/cognitive stability by hyper-NE [193]; **deception result [194]**: almost any (fake) Stackelberg equilibrium can be induced by the follower if and only if the leader procures at least their maximin utility at that equilibrium.
- **SGs for AI/ML [195–200]**: SSGs for AI security; incomplete-information SSGs for incentive mechanisms in distributed ML; GSG-based robust/MARL.

### 5.5 DG research landscape (Sec. 3.3)
- **LQ DGs [201,202]**: analytically/numerically solvable; pursuit–evasion applications [203,204]. Quoted advances: singular LQ DG not solvable by Isaacs MinMax or Bellman–Isaacs, handled via interception DG with regularized cost and dual representation [205]; LQG asset-defending DG with inaccessible attacker/defender state [206]; two-player LQG pursuit–evasion with partial information and selected observations (observation cost + exposure cost) [207]; two-player mean-field LQ stochastic DGs on infinite horizon via coupled generalized algebraic Riccati equations, open- and closed-loop saddle points [208]; two-defender-two-attacker LQ DG defending a stationary target [209]; NN-based synchronous iteration learning for multi-player zero-sum DGs [210].
- **Nonlinear DGs**: iterative ADP [211]; single-NN policy-iteration critic learning [212]; time delays [213–215]; fractional-order systems [216]; distribution/random-initial-condition dependence, Wasserstein space [217]; multi-player zero-sum with uncertain dynamics via new iterative ADP [218]; efficient iterative LQ approximations for multi-player nonlinear general-sum DGs [219]; pursuit–evasion [220,221] and **reach-avoid** [222,223] multi-player DGs.
- **Stackelberg DGs**: LQ Stackelberg DG with mixed deterministic/stochastic controls [224]; counter-terrorism [225]; two classes of state-constrained Stackelberg DGs with Hamilton–Jacobi equations established [226].
- **Stochastic DGs [227,228]**: two-person zero-sum stochastic LQ DGs, open-loop saddle point and lower/upper values [229]; ergodic payoff without non-degeneracy [230]; stochastic LQ Stackelberg DGs with asymmetric information [231], jump-diffusion [232], without Riccati solvability [233], with model uncertainty [234]; nonlinear Stackelberg stochastic DG with asymmetric noisy observation [235].
- **Terminal time/state constraints**: fixed terminal time, unconstrained state [236]; state-constrained fixed terminal [237]; free terminal time without state constraints [238], with state constraints but zero running cost [239,240]; nonzero state constraint and underdetermined terminal time [226]; infinite horizon [208,241]; also continuous/impulse controls [241], mean-field DGs [242,243], risk-sensitive zero-sum DGs [228].

### 5.6 Algorithms (Sec. 4)
- **Regret notions (eqs. (15)–(16))** [249]: external regret R^E_i:=max_{a_i∈A_i} Σ_{t=1}^T [u_i(a_i,π^t_{−i})−u_i(π^t)]; internal regret R^I_i:=max_{a'_i,a_i∈A_i} Σ_{t=1}^T 1_{a^t_i=a^A_i}[u_i(a'_i,a^t_{−i})−u_i(a^t)]; weighted variants [250]; swap regret [102]; dynamic/static NE-based regrets [21,251–254].
- **Optimistic FTRL (eq. (17))**: x^{t+1}=argmax_{x∈X}{α⟨x, m^t+Σ_{τ=1}^t g^τ⟩ − R(x)}; **OMD (eq. (18))**: x^{t+1}=argmax_{x∈X}{α⟨x,m^t⟩−D_R(x,x̂^t)}, x̂^{t+1}=argmax_{x̂∈X}{α⟨x̂,g^t⟩−D_R(x̂,x̂^t)}; X closed convex, α>0 stepsize, g^t subgradient, m^t prediction (often m^t=g^t), R strongly convex, Bregman divergence D_R(x,y):=R(x)−R(y)−⟨∇R(y),x−y⟩. OGDA [86] and OMWU/optimistic hedge [255] are special cases/variants; also OptDA [256], greedy weights [250].
- **CFR [257]** (eqs. (19)–(25)): counterfactual reach probability p^π_{−i}(J) (player i's contribution to reaching J treated as 1); counterfactual value at history ν^π_i(h):=Σ_{z∈Z,h<z} p^π_{−i}(h)p^π(h,z)u_i(z) (19); infoset value ν^π_i(J):=Σ_{h∈J}ν^π_i(h) (20); action value ν^π_i(J,a):=Σ_{h∈J}[p^π_{−i}(h)Σ_{z∈Z}p^π(h·a,z)u_i(z)] (21); instantaneous regret r^t_i(J,a):=ν^{π^t}_i(J,a)−ν^{π^t}_i(J) (22); cumulative R^T_i(J,a):=Σ_t r^t_i(J,a) (23); **RM update (24)**: π^{T+1}_i(J,a)=R^{T,+}_i(J,a)/ζ^T_i(J,a) if ζ^T_i(J,a)>0, else uniform 1/|A(J)|, with ζ^T_i=Σ_a R^{T,+}_i(J,a); average strategy ¯π^T_i(J,a)=Σ_t p^{π^t}_i(J)π^t_i(J,a)/Σ_t p^{π^t}_i(J) (25) — **CFR converges to NE via the average strategy** [authors' claim, standard]. Variants: CFR+ [245,258], DCFR [259], LCFR [260], ECFR [261], AutoCFR [262]; survey refs. [18,116,263].
- **AI methods [104]**: PSRO [26,264] (unifies fictitious play and double oracle); NFSP [138]; Deep CFR [260]; single Deep CFR [265]; **UDEF** [147] (unified PSRO+CFR framework, superior to both on Leduc poker); **PoG** [144] (integrates guided search, self-play learning, game-theoretic reasoning; beats Slumbot in heads-up no-limit Hold'em); **NAC** [148] (meta-learning of objectives and meta-solutions; comparable/superior to PSRO on Games of Skill, differentiable Lotto, non-transitive Mixture Games, Iterated Matching Pennies, Kuhn poker). Feedback loop: game-theoretic reasoning (minimax-optimal strategy computation) is a key ingredient inside PoG, and exploitability minimization is crucial inside NAC — i.e., game theory also benefits the AI methods.
- **Last-iterate caveat**: CFR-type methods guarantee convergence only of the empirical distribution of play (time-average) and "generally fail" for day-to-day (last-iterate) play [266,267], though last-iterate convergence holds for two-player zero-sum games [96]; importance motivated by GANs [299] and economics [255]; further refs. [86,96,268–270].
- **Stackelberg solvers (Sec. 4.2)**: GSGs/SSGs as bilevel LP or MILP, transformable/relaxable to LP [158]; NP-hardness in general [159]. (1) **Multiple LP approach** [43] — widest use for polynomial cases (single follower), improved by merging LPs into a single MILP [271] and extended to SSGs [174] (efficient for schedule size 1, and size 2 with homogeneous resources — per Table 5); (2) **Benders decomposition** [272] — master problem (relaxed constraints) + subproblem + separation (dual) problem; improvements via Farkas' lemma [273] and normalized cuts [274] → normalized Benders decomposition [159]; (3) **Branch and cut** [275,276] — cutting planes + branch and bound, cuts added at nodes as needed; (4) **Cut and branch** — cuts only at root; with variables in R in the master problem and stabilization found superior to some extent [159]; (5) **Gradient descent ascent** [277] — sequential descent/ascent for continuous SGs; max-oracle GDA [93] replaces the ascent step by an approximate best response; AI approaches [278], ML-for-SSG survey [279].
- **HJI approach (Sec. 4.3, eqs. (26)–(29))**: Hamiltonian H(t,x,ω)=min_{u∈U} max_{v∈V}{⟨f(t,x,u,v),ω⟩+f_0(t,x,u,v)} (26); **HJI equation** ∂_tψ(t,z)+H(t,z,∂_zψ(t,z))=0, ψ(T,z)=φ(z) (27) with ∂_t, ∂_z subgradients; function class Ψ (continuous in t, Lipschitz on bounded sets in z); characterization [from their ref. 214]: if ψ∈Ψ is coinvariantly differentiable at each point, satisfies (27) and ∂_tψ, ∂_zψ∈Ψ, then ψ is the value function of DG (10)–(11); optimal controls u*(t,z)∈argmin_u max_v χ, v*(t,z)∈argmax_v min_u χ with χ(t,z,u,v):=⟨f(t,z,u,v),∂_zψ(t,z)⟩+f_0(t,z,u,v) (28)–(29). AI note: two-level value-iteration-based integral RL for multi-player nonlinear DGs using partial dynamics [281].

### 5.7 Application exemplars with full mathematics (Sec. 5)
- **Example 1, Radar jamming [288,289,290]**: two-player zero-sum game (radar vs jammer; utility = probability of detection; Swerling Type II targets; physical constraints on jammer power, spatial extent, radar threshold/reference window). Signal models: radar s(t)=Σ_{m=1}^M s_m(t−(m−1)T), s_m(t)=a(t)Σ_{k=1}^K rect((t−kT_c)/T_c)exp(ȷ2πf^m_k t) (30) with carrier frequency f^m_k∈F:={f̄_1,…,f̄_N}; jammer u(t)=Σ_{m=1}^M u_m(t−(m−1)T), u_m(t)=rect(t/T_J)v_m(t)exp(ȷ2πf_m t) (31). Actions = carrier-frequency selection. Modeled as an **II-EFG**, solved to NE by **CFR** [289].
- **Example 2, Border patrols [182,183,291]**: Carabineros de Chile night-shift SSG; precinct pairing, vantage locations, weekly joint-detail deployment (Fig. 5, case of 3 pairings/7 precincts/10 locations). MILP formulation (eqs. (32)–(36)): max_{x,s^k,q^k,r^k} Σ_{k=1}^n ϖ^k r^k s.t. x^⊤**1**=1, x⩾0; (q^k)^⊤**1**=1, q^k∈{0,1}^{|A_f|}; r^k ⩽ Σ_{i=1}^{|A_l|} R^k_{ij}x_i + M(1−q^k_j); 0 ⩽ s^k − Σ_i C^k_{ij}x_i ⩽ M(1−q^k_j) (∀k∈F, ∀j∈[|A_f|]); M big-M making constraints redundant when q^k_j=0. Solution concept: SSE, computable by Sec. 4.2 algorithms; also a two-stage approximate sampling method [182]. (Notation inconsistency as printed: ϖ^k superscript here vs ϖ_k subscript in eq. (6).)
- **Example 3, Pursuit–evasion [204,292]**: one pursuer (speed v_P) defends a coastline (x-axis) against two collaborating slower evaders (common speed v<v_P) in the open half-plane y>0; state z=(x_P,y_P,x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)∈R^6; controls are heading angles ψ,φ_1,φ_2∈[−π,π); dynamics (37): ẋ_P=cosψ, ẏ_P=sinψ, ẋ_1=v_r cosφ_1, ẏ_1=v_r sinφ_1, ẋ_2=v_r cosφ_2, ẏ_2=v_r sinφ_2, with v_r:=v/v_P; terminal cost (38)–(39): J(u(t),v(t),z_0)=Φ(z(t'),z(t_f)), Φ=z-sum of pursuer heights y_P(t')+y_P(t_f) at the two capture instants t',t_f; zero-sum DG, NE = optimal instantaneous headings; solved via the **Apollonius circle and the HJI equation** [292].
- **Example 4, GANs [293,294]**: generator G vs discriminator D; max_𝒟 min_𝒢 V(𝒢,𝒟) (40), V(G,D):=E_{p_data(x)}log D(x)+E_{p_g(x)}log(1−D(x)) (41); a two-player zero-sum game, solution concept NE computable by any efficient two-player zero-sum method.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated future directions (Sec. 6, listed in full):**
1. **Efficient algorithm design** — efficient, fast, optimal algorithms under limited computing/storage/memory remain the overarching direction (equilibrium computation [250], real-time strategy making [295], exploiting suboptimal opponents [296], attack resiliency [297]; large action spaces/infosets with limited computation, storage, communication [298]).
2. **Last-iterate convergence** — no-regret learning guarantees only time-average convergence in general; last-iterate fails generally [266,267], restricted positive cases exist (two-player zero-sum [96]); important for GANs [299] and economics [255].
3. **Imperfect information** — far from fully explored: computationally light algorithms, domain-free algorithms, large numbers of players [126,301].
4. **Incomplete information** — parameter, action-outcome, and world-state uncertainty; main models are Bayesian and interval models [25,302,303].
5. **Bounded rationality** — behavior models (level-/QR-type models), prospect-theory-inspired models, quantal response models [304–306].
6. **Dynamic environments** — online/time-varying games with time-varying, even adversarial, utility functions without distributional assumptions [21,251–254].
7. **Hybrid games** — realistic games with both continuous and discrete physical dynamics; combining game theory with control dynamics is important and challenging [307,308].
8. **AI in games** — integration of RL, neural networks, meta-learning [146,309–311]; implementation of real-time strategies from offline or streaming data [150].
Also acknowledged: the literature "is too vast to cover in its entirety" (Sec. 7), and the excluded model families (Bayesian, Markov/stochastic, signaling, behavioral, evolutionary games).

**Critical observations (this archive, with page/section citations):**

## 8. Citation Value
- **Role**: ideal Introduction/Related-Work anchor when a paper needs a single citation covering the whole adversarial-games landscape — from discrete zero-sum/Stackelberg security games to continuous-time zero-sum differential games — and for motivating why game-theoretic equilibrium computation connects to AI (CFR/PSRO/PoG/NAC, GANs). For differential-game papers (pursuit–evasion, reach–avoid, target defense), it serves as the "state of the art in the broader adversarial decision-making" citation and situates the DG contribution relative to SSG/normal-form lines; it also backs claims that zero-sum DGs are the continuous-time limit of adversarial sequential games (Table 4 framing).
