## 2. Research Background

Perimeter defense asks a mobile defender to intercept intruders before they reach a protected region; it was first posed for one vehicle vs. one intruder by Isaacs [2] and has since been studied mostly as pursuit-evasion / reach-avoid games ([3], [4]), typically solved via Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman-Isaacs equations [5], [6] or via matching-based receding-horizon and perimeter-constrained defender schemes [7], [8]. A parallel literature treats intruder interception as a dynamic vehicle routing (DVR) problem [9]–[13], where average-case (stochastic-input) performance is quantified via competitive analysis [14]–[16], and as the Moving-Target TSP on a line [17]–[19]. The authors' own prior work analyzed circular and rectangular perimeter defense with stochastically generated inputs [20], [21]. All of these lines essentially characterize **average-case** behavior or small-team optimal play; the worst case, where intruders may coordinate their arrival times to overwhelm the defense, had not been characterized. This paper imports the competitive-analysis methodology of online algorithms [1], [22] into multi-intruder perimeter defense to obtain worst-case guarantees over the parameter space (perimeter size $\rho$, intruder speed $v$). Application motivation given: defending a perimeter from missiles or locusts, and surveillance/traffic information gathering (p. 1).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem** (Section II, p. 2): the environment is the line segment $\bar{E} = [-1, 1]$; the protected region (perimeter) is $[-\rho, \rho]$ with $0 < \rho < 1$; the surrounding environment is $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \{y \in \mathbb{R} : \rho \le |y| \le 1\}$. Intruders arrive over time at the endpoints $-1$ or $+1$ and move with fixed speed $v < 1$ toward the nearest of $-\rho$ or $\rho$ (i.e., straight inward, fixed direction — they cannot evade or reverse). A single defender, a first-order integrator with maximum speed $1$, must capture intruders (become coincident with them) before they reach the perimeter. An intruder that reaches $-\rho$ or $\rho$ uncaptured is **lost**. Input instance $\mathcal{I} = \{t, n(t), \{-1,1\}^{n(t)}\}_{t=0}^{T}$ — arrival times, multiplicities, and endpoints. An online algorithm is a map $A: \bar{E} \times \mathcal{F} \to [-1,1]$ ($\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{E})$ = finite subsets of $\mathcal{E}$) yielding $\dot{x}(t) = A(x(t), Q(t, \mathcal{I}))$, where $Q(t,\mathcal{I})$ is the set of live intruder positions at $t$; an **optimal offline** algorithm knows the entire instance in advance (non-causal). Inputs are assumed **non-adaptive**: arrivals do not depend on the defender's motion (p. 2).

**Performance metric** (Definition 1, p. 2): for algorithm $A$ on instance $\mathcal{I}$, $c_A(\mathcal{I}) = n_{OPT}(\mathcal{I}) / n_A(\mathcal{I}) \ge 1$, where $n_A(\mathcal{I})$ counts captured intruders and $OPT$ maximizes captures; the environment-level ratios are $c_A(\mathcal{E}) = \sup_{\mathcal{I}} c_A(\mathcal{I})$ and $c(\mathcal{E}) = \inf_A c_A(\mathcal{E})$. $A$ is $c$-competitive if $c_A(\mathcal{E}) \le c$. Note this is a **maximization** problem (count of captures), the mirror image of the usual online-TSP minimization cost.

**Gap**: [Authors' claim, p. 1]: "While perimeter defense problems have been well-studied, most prior work has focused on determining an optimal strategy for a small number of intruders or assuming that the input instance is generated by some stochastic process… they essentially ignore the worst-case where intruders may coordinate their actions to overwhelm the defense." [Verified from cited text as cited in-paper]: reach-avoid formulations [3], [4] design control policies for fixed player sets; HJB-Isaacs approaches [5], [6] are "suitable for low dimensional state spaces and in simple environments"; receding-horizon matching [7] and perimeter-constrained defenders [8] are strategy constructions without worst-case ratio guarantees; DVR reviews [13] and stochastic DVR [10] characterize average-case behavior; MTTSP work [19] gives an $O(n^2)$ minimum-time algorithm to capture $n$ intruders on a line but for a known, finite input. The authors' earlier works [20], [21] are explicitly stochastic ("dynamic boundary guarding against radially incoming targets", "translating targets"); they state the "key distinction of the current work from these past works is the characterization of competitiveness of the algorithms for worst case inputs" (p. 1). No cited prior work establishes impossibility thresholds or competitive ratios for online perimeter defense on a line, so the gap claim is **substantiated within the paper's citation set**.

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Fundamental limits (impossibility), theory** (Section III): a most-difficult regime $v > \frac{1-\rho}{2\rho}$ in which no algorithm — online *or* offline — is $c$-competitive for any constant $c$ and cannot even capture all intruders (Theorem III.1); a slightly easier regime $v \ge \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$ in which $c(\mathcal{E}) \ge 2$ for every algorithm (Theorem III.2).
2. **Negative result for a natural algorithm, theory**: FCFS is not $c$-competitive for any constant $c$ whenever $\frac{2}{v+1} + \rho > \frac{1-\rho}{v} + \epsilon$ (Lemma 2) — a natural greedy is ruled out even in regimes where other algorithms are competitive.
3. **Three online algorithms with proven ratios, modeling/algorithm** (Section IV): Sweeping (1-competitive for $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{3+\rho}$, Theorem IV.1), Compare-and-Capture / CaC (2-competitive where Lemmas 3–4 hold, Theorem IV.2), Capture-with-Patience / CAP (4-competitive for $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}$, Theorem IV.4), plus tightness lower bounds for CAP (Lemma 6). Increasingly hard parameter regimes are covered at the price of larger competitive ratio.
4. **Reduction to extreme-speed algorithms, theory**: any algorithm can be matched by one that moves at unit speed or rests (Lemma 1), so all analysis restricts to bang-bang speed.
5. **Numerical study, experiment** (Section V): Poisson-arriving intruders ($\lambda = 5$, $\rho = 0.2$, 50 runs) show the algorithms capture at least ~half of the intruders even **beyond** their proven parameter regimes.

### 5.1 Setup and reduction (Section III, p. 2)

- **Lemma 1 (Extreme speed algorithms, p. 2)**: Given an arbitrary algorithm $A$ and a non-adaptive input instance $\mathcal{I}$, there exists an extreme-speed algorithm $A': \bar{E}\times\mathcal{F} \to \{-1, 0, 1\}$ (unit speed or stationary) that captures at least as many intruders in $\mathcal{I}$ as $A$. *Assumptions*: non-adaptive inputs (arrival times independent of defender motion). *Proof approach (outline)*: define the **capture profile** of $A$'s execution as the set of pairs $(x_i, k_i)$, $1 \le i \le n$, where $k_i$ is the time of the $i$-th capture and $x_i$ its location; construct $A'$ that reproduces the same profile by moving to the next capture point $x_i$ as fast as possible and, if necessary, **waiting** at $x_i$ until time $k_i$. *Consequence*: all subsequent fundamental limits and algorithm analyses may restrict to extreme-speed (bang-bang) strategies; global statement, analytic. *Physical meaning*: speed-1-or-0 motion is WLOG for counting captures when arrivals are non-adaptive. *Extensibility*: the argument is generic and applies to any interception-count objective with unit-speed vehicles.

### 5.2 Fundamental limits (Theorems III.1, III.2, p. 2–3)

- **Theorem III.1 (p. 2)**: For any environment $\mathcal{E}$ such that $v > \frac{1-\rho}{2\rho}$: (i) there does not exist a $c$-competitive algorithm for any constant $c$; (ii) no algorithm (online or offline) can capture all intruders. *Assumptions*: non-adaptive inputs; single defender, speed 1; intruders speed $v < 1$ moving inward. *Proof approach (outline)*: construct a two-phase worst-case input — a **stream** of intruders arriving at endpoint $+1$ at times $1, 3, 5, \dots$ ("2 time units apart starting at time 1"), plus a **burst** of $c+1$ intruders arriving at endpoint $-1$ at the first time $t$ at which the online vehicle moves to $\rho$ (i.e., commits to one side / crosses toward the perimeter). Decisive intermediate fact (p. 2–3, unnumbered): for $v > \frac{1-\rho}{2\rho}$ the inward transit time $\frac{1-\rho}{v} < 2\rho$ equals less than the defender's $\rho \to -\rho$ crossing time $2\rho$, so any online algorithm "captures at most 1 stream intruder and loses the entire burst of $c+1$ intruders, however, the optimal algorithm can capture all the burst intruders and at least all but the last stream intruder." Making the stream arbitrarily long drives the ratio past any fixed $c$. *Local/global*: global in the input space (a single adversarial instance per $c$); analytic. *Physical meaning*: when intruders cross faster than the defender can sweep the perimeter once, no defender — even with clairvoyance — guarantees a bounded capture fraction, so the problem is fundamentally hopeless in this regime. *Extensibility*: the stream-plus-burst template is reusable for any two-sided perimeter geometry. *Proof gap*: only the outline is printed; the formal construction (e.g., the precise synchronization between the vehicle's first arrival at $\rho$ and the burst release) is in [24].

- **Theorem III.2 (p. 3)**: For the environment $\mathcal{E}$ such that $v \ge \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$, $c(\mathcal{E}) \ge 2$ — no algorithm is better than 2-competitive. *Proof approach (outline)*: five different two-intruder input instances, intruders $a$ and $b$ arriving at **opposite endpoints**; no single online algorithm captures both intruders in all five instances, while for each instance some algorithm in a fixed collection does — an adversary yao-style argument over the defender's first commitment. *Decisive intermediate fact*: $\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho} \le v$ means an intruder reaches the perimeter in time $\le 1+\rho$, no more than the defender's end-to-end travel, so committing to one endpoint necessarily forfeits a simultaneous arrival at the other. *Local/global*: global lower bound on $c(\mathcal{E})$; analytic. *Physical meaning*: 2 is an information-theoretic barrier for one-speed-1 defender vs. speed-$\ge\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$ intruders — the "at best 2-competitive" threshold of the abstract. *Extensibility*: the five-instance gadget applies to any two-approach-corridor defense. *Proof gap*: the five instances are not enumerated in the conference version (deferred to [24]).

- **Lemma 2 (FCFS negative result, p. 3)**: For any $\mathcal{E}$ where $\frac{2}{v+1} + \rho > \frac{1-\rho}{v} + \epsilon$ for some small $\epsilon > 0$, FCFS (the algorithm sending the vehicle at speed 1 toward the earliest-arriving intrader that is neither lost nor captured) is not $c$-competitive for any constant $c$. *Proof approach (outline)*: worst-case instance $\mathcal{I}(c)$: first intruder released at time 0 at $+1$; $c+1$ intruders released at time $\epsilon$ at $-1$. Decisive computation (unnumbered, p. 3): under the hypothesis, FCFS$(\mathcal{I}(c)) = 1$ while OPT$(\mathcal{I}(c)) = c+1$, so the ratio $c+1$ is unbounded as $c \to \infty$. The timing comparison is $\frac{2}{v+1} + \rho$ (the defender, starting at the origin, intercepts the first intruder at time $\frac{1}{1+v}$ at position $\frac{1}{1+v}$ and then needs the remaining $\frac{1}{1+v}+\rho$ to reach $-\rho$) versus $\epsilon + \frac{1-\rho}{v}$ (burst arrival at $-\rho$) — the reconstruction above is consistent with the printed inequality; the paper itself states only the conclusion. *Scope*: "This result generalizes to any variation of FCFS which captures the first arriving intruder, if possible, before any later arriving intruder" (p. 3). *Physical meaning*: greedy earliest-first service is fatally exploitable by a synchronized opposite-end burst. *Proof gap*: the algebra connecting the printed hypothesis to FCFS$(\mathcal{I}(c)) = 1$ is not shown (deferred to [24]).

### 5.3 Sweeping algorithm (Section IV-A, p. 3)

- **Algorithm**: at time 0 move at unit speed toward $+1$; reverse direction **only** upon reaching an endpoint $\pm 1$; open-loop (ignores intruder information). A variant that stops moving into a side with no intruders achieves the same guarantee (p. 3).
- **Theorem IV.1 (p. 3)**: For environment $\mathcal{E}$, Sweep is 1-competitive if $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{3+\rho}$; if not, it is not $c$-competitive for any constant $c$. *Assumptions*: single vehicle, unit speed; non-adaptive inputs. *Proof (full, printed)*: any intruder $i$ (WLOG arriving at $+1$) takes $\frac{1-\rho}{v}$ to travel from $+1$ to $\rho$. Worst case for Sweep: the vehicle left $+1$ **just before** intruder $i$ arrived; it then needs $3 + \rho$ time to get back to $\rho$ moving toward $i$ (traverse from $+1$ to $-1$: 2, plus $-1 \to \rho$: $1-\rho$, plus the return distance $\rho$ — the paper states "it will take $3+\rho$ time to get to $\rho$"). Decisive inequality (unnumbered): $3 + \rho \le \frac{1-\rho}{v} \iff v \le \frac{1-\rho}{3+\rho}$, in which case the vehicle reaches $\rho$ first and captures every intruder — 1-competitiveness. Converse: for $v > \frac{1-\rho}{3+\rho}$ an instance with intruders arriving at $+1$ just after the vehicle departs $+1$ loses all of them while OPT captures them, so the ratio is unbounded. *The variant*: the upper-bound argument carries over; the lower bound needs decoy intruders at $-1$ to force the stopping-variant to head toward $-1$, and the bound "still holds by increasing the number of intruders which arrive at 1" (p. 3). *Local/global*: global; analytic and tight (both directions proved). *Physical meaning*: a pure patrol guarantees zero losses only when intruders are slow enough that the 4-unit round-trip patrol period outpaces their ingress. *Extensibility*: directly transferable to any convex perimeter with a patrol cycle of known period.

### 5.4 Compare-and-Capture (CaC) algorithm (Section IV-B, p. 3–4)

- **State sets (p. 3)**: epoch $k$ = time interval during which the vehicle moves from $x_k \in \{\rho, -\rho\}$ toward $x_{k+1}$, capturing intruders en route; $k_S$ = start of epoch $k$. $S^k_{\text{same}}$ = intruders on the same side as the vehicle at $k_S$; $S^k_{\text{opp}}$ = intruders on the opposite side at distance between $\rho + 2\rho v$ and $\rho + 2v\rho + \frac{2v(1-\rho)}{1+v}$ from the origin at $k_S$; if the vehicle is at $x_k = \rho$, then $S^k_{\text{opp}} \subset \left[-\left(\rho + 2\rho v + \tfrac{2v(1-\rho)}{1+v}\right),\, -(\rho + 2\rho v)\right]$ (verified against the rendered page 3).
- **Algorithm 1 (p. 4)**: initial phase — vehicle waits at the center for $\frac{1-\rho-3\rho v}{v}$ time units, i.e., until the first intruder is $3\rho v + \rho$ from the origin; if intruders in $[\rho + 3\rho v,\, 1]$ outnumber those in $[-1,\, -(\rho + 3\rho v)]$ move to $x_1 = \rho$, else $x_1 = -\rho$; first epoch starts upon reaching $x_1$. Per epoch $k \ge 1$: if $|S^k_{\text{same}}| \le |S^k_{\text{opp}}|$ — capture all intruders in $S^k_{\text{opp}}$ and move to $x_{k+1} = -x_k$ (switch sides); otherwise — move away from the origin for at most $\frac{1-\rho}{1+v}$ time to capture $S^k_{\text{same}}$ and return to $x_{k+1} = x_k$ (p. 3, verified). The opposite-side excursion lasts at most $2\rho + \frac{4v(1-\rho)}{(1+v)^2}$ time (p. 3, verified against the 300-dpi render: the fraction $\tfrac{4v(1-\rho)}{(1+v)^2}$ is a separate addend, not a common denominator). CaC is **not open-loop but memoryless**: actions depend only on the present state of vehicle and intruders (p. 3).
- **Lemma 3 (no incidental losses, p. 4)**: In every epoch $k$, any intruder outside $S^k_{\text{same}} \cup S^k_{\text{opp}}$ is not lost if
  $$\frac{\rho v}{1-\rho} + \frac{v^2}{(1+v)^2} \le \frac{1}{4}.$$
  *Proof approach (outline)*: such an intruder is at least $\rho + 2\rho v$ from the origin at the end of epoch $k$. *Proof gap*: the derivation of the $\frac14$ condition from that positional bound is not printed (deferred to [24]); note the hypothesis couples $\rho, v$ nonlinearly and is one of the two curves of the CaC parameter region in Fig. 2.
- **Lemma 4 (well-definedness, p. 4)**: In every epoch $k$ of CaC, $S^k_{\text{opp}}$ is well defined if
  $$\rho + 2\rho v + \frac{2v(1-\rho)}{1+v} \le 1.$$
  *Proof*: "Omitted for brevity" (p. 4) — the outer radius of the $S^k_{\text{opp}}$ annulus must not exceed the endpoint, so the band lies inside the environment. *Proof gap*: none printed at all; the reading above (band inside $[-1,1]$) is the natural interpretation.
- **Theorem IV.2 (2-competitiveness of CaC, p. 4)**: CaC captures at least half of all intruders and is 2-competitive in parameter regimes for which Lemma 3 and Lemma 4 both hold, i.e.,
  $$\frac{\rho v}{1-\rho} + \frac{v^2}{(1+v)^2} \le \frac{1}{4} \quad\text{and}\quad \rho + 2\rho v + \frac{2v(1-\rho)}{1+v} \le 1.$$
  *Proof (printed, counting argument)*: Lemma 4 makes Algorithm 1 well-defined in every epoch; Lemma 3 ensures every intruder belongs to $S^k_{\text{same}}$ or $S^k_{\text{opp}}$ for some epoch $k$ (an intruder lost in epoch $k$ would have had to be outside both sets). Because of the comparison (line 9 of Algorithm 1 — switch only when the opposite side holds at least as many intruders), "in every epoch, the number of intruders captured is at least the number of intruders lost"; an intruder not lost in epoch $k$ belongs to $S^{k+1}_{\text{same}}$ or $S^{k+1}_{\text{opp}}$ in the next epoch. Summing over epochs: captures $\ge$ losses, hence $n_{\text{CaC}} \ge \frac{1}{2} n_{\text{total}} \ge \frac{1}{2} n_{OPT}$... more precisely $n_{OPT} \le n_{\text{total}}$ and captures $\ge$ losses gives captures $\ge n_{\text{total}}/2$, so $c_A(\mathcal{I}) \le 2$ for every instance. *Local/global*: global guarantee on $\mathcal{E}$; analytic. *Physical meaning*: a myopic greedy comparison — go where there are more intruders you can still catch — suffices for a factor-2 worst case whenever the annulus timing is consistent. *Proof gap*: the epoch-by-epoch accounting that partitions *all* intruders into per-epoch same/opp classes (including intruders that arrive mid-epoch) is only sketched; the full charging is in [24].
- **Interpretation of the regime**: Lemma 3's condition is the binding constraint at small $\rho$ (e.g., $\rho = 0.2$ gives $v \lesssim 0.4$) and Lemma 4's at larger $\rho$; Section V-A notes the CaC feasibility curve lies **entirely below** the Theorem III.2 lower-bound curve $\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$, so a gap remains open between "2-competitive achievable" and "c(E) ≥ 2 unavoidable" (p. 5).

### 5.5 Capture-with-Patience (CAP) algorithm (Section IV-C, p. 4–5)

- **Key quantity (p. 4)**: $z = \frac{1-\rho}{v}$ = time for any intruder originating at $\pm 1$ to reach the corresponding $\pm\rho$. CAP requires $z \ge 6\rho$, equivalently
  $$v \le \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho},$$
  which ensures incoming intruders take at least $6\rho$ time to reach the perimeter while the vehicle needs $2\rho$ to cross between $-\rho$ and $\rho$ (p. 4).
- **Algorithm 2 (p. 5)**: time is re-zeroed at the first arrival and sliced into intervals of length $2\rho$; the $i$-th interval is $[2(i-1)\rho, 2i\rho]$, $i \ge 1$. $S^i_{\text{opp}}$ / $S^i_{\text{same}}$: intruders arriving in the $i$-th interval on the opposite / same side of the vehicle. Initial case: vehicle stays at the origin until $2\rho$; at $2\rho$, if $|S^1_{\text{opp}}| > |S^1_{\text{same}}|$ (left of origin = opposite, right = same in this special case) move to $x_2 = -\rho$, else $x_2 = \rho$; then stay until time $z$. Steady state (after $z$), evaluated at instants $z + 2j\rho$, $j \ge 0$: stationed at $\rho$ (WLOG), the vehicle knows $S^{j+1}_{\text{same}}$ intruders lie in $[\rho, \rho+2\rho v]$, $S^{j+2}_{\text{same}}$ in $[\rho+2\rho v, \rho+4\rho v]$, $S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}$ in $[\rho+4\rho v, \rho+6\rho v]$ (Fig. 1, p. 4 — "breakdown of the environment into regions of length $2\rho v$"), and since $z \ge 6\rho$ all of $S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}$ has already arrived by $2j\rho + z$ (and analogously for the opposite-side intervals). Decision rule (line 9 of Algorithm 2, verified): if
  $$|S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}| > |S^{j+1}_{\text{same}}| + |S^{j+2}_{\text{same}}| + |S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}|,$$
  cross to $-\rho$, arriving at $2(j+1)\rho + z$ "just in time" to capture all of $S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}$; otherwise stay and capture $S^{j+1}_{\text{same}}$, re-evaluating at $2(j+1)\rho + z$. Essence: switch sides only when the payoff across the line outweighs the three sacrificed same-side intervals.
- **Lemma 5 (no wasted round trips, p. 5)**: CAP never moves the vehicle from $\rho$ to $-\rho$ and back to $\rho$ (or vice versa) without capturing at least one interval of intruders. *Proof (printed)*: to move at $2j\rho + z$ it must hold that $|S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}| > |S^{j+1}_{\text{same}}| + |S^{j+2}_{\text{same}}| + |S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}|$, hence $|S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}| > |S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}|$; an immediate return without capturing $S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}$ would require $|S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}| > |S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}| + |S^{j+3}_{\text{opp}}| + |S^{j+4}_{\text{opp}}|$, which contradicts the previous inequality. Decisive chain: $|S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}| > |S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}|$ and $|S^{j+3}_{\text{same}}| \not> |S^{j+2}_{\text{opp}}| + \cdots$. *Local/global*: structural property used by the accounting; analytic.
- **Corollary IV.3 (p. 5)**: For any $i \ge 1$, CAP captures intruders from one of $S^i_{\text{same}}$ or $S^{i+1}_{\text{opp}}$. *Proof*: "Omitted for brevity" (p. 5). *Proof gap*: fully omitted; it is the per-interval analog of Theorem IV.2's "captured ≥ lost" bookkeeping and is what makes the global charge bounded by 3 lost per captured interval.
- **Theorem IV.4 (4-competitiveness, p. 5)**: CAP is 4-competitive for any environment $\mathcal{E}$ with $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}$. *Proof approach (outline)*: an **accounting/charging analysis** — "charge" lost intruders to captured ones ("captured intruders pay for the lost intruders"); CAP switches sides only when the payoff on the other side is large enough to pay for the lost intruders on the original side, so each captured intruder is charged at most 3 losses plus itself, giving $n_{\text{CAP}} \ge \frac{1}{4} n_{\text{total}} \ge \frac{1}{4} n_{OPT}$. *Local/global*: global; analytic. *Physical meaning*: patience (accepting a one-side-only harvest, switching rarely) degrades the guarantee from 2 to 4 but extends operation to intruders up to 3× faster ($\frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}$ vs. the CaC regime at small $\rho$). *Proof gap*: the charging invariant (which captured interval pays for which losses, including the initial transient before $z$) is only outlined; full proof in [24].
- **Lemma 6 (tightness of CAP, p. 5)**: CAP is **no better than 3-competitive** for $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}$, and **no better than 4-competitive** for $v \le \min\{\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}\}$. *Proof approach (outline)*: two steady streams, one per endpoint — at $+1$ one intruder at times $6i\rho$, $0 \le i \le K$; at $-1$ one intruder at times $3\rho + \frac{\rho}{v} + i 2\rho$, $0 \le i \le K$; CAP stays at $\rho$ and captures the less dense stream while another algorithm stays at $-\rho$ and captures the dense stream — a 3-competitive lower bound valid throughout the CAP regime; for $v \le \min\{\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}\}$ an algorithm exists that captures **all** intruders, so CAP's ratio is exactly 4 there (4-competitive lower bound, i.e., the Theorem IV.4 constant is tight). *Local/global*: instance-level lower bounds; analytic. *Physical meaning*: CAP is provably suboptimal by factors 3–4 in parts of its own regime — the price of memorylessness/patience. *Proof gap*: stream-timing details deferred to [24].

### 5.6 Model, information structure, winning conditions (recap with locations)

- **Model**: 1-D line $\bar{E} = [-1,1]$; perimeter $[-\rho,\rho]$; intruders first-order at constant speed $v<1$ inward from $\pm 1$ toward the nearest perimeter endpoint $\pm\rho$; defender first-order integrator, $|\dot{x}| \le 1$; capture = coincidence of positions; loss = intruder reaching $\pm\rho$ (p. 2).
- **Information structure**: online — velocity command $A(x(t), Q(t,\mathcal{I}))$ uses only intruders that have already arrived; offline OPT has full $\mathcal{I}$; inputs non-adaptive (p. 2). CaC is memoryless; Sweep is open-loop; CAP is memoryless with interval-based bookkeeping.
- **Termination / winning condition**: no per-instance terminal condition is defined; performance is the **count** $n_A(\mathcal{I})$ of captured intruders over the whole instance horizon $[0, T]$, compared via the ratio $c_A(\mathcal{I}) = n_{OPT}(\mathcal{I})/n_A(\mathcal{I})$ (Definition 1, p. 2). "Winning" = finite competitive ratio on $\mathcal{E}$; the ideal (ratio 1) = capture all intruders.

### 5.7 Consolidated parameter-regime map (Section V-A, Fig. 2, p. 5)

For a given $\rho$, as $v$ grows: Sweep guaranteed 1-competitive for $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{3+\rho}$; CaC guaranteed 2-competitive for the pair of Lemma 3–4 conditions; CAP guaranteed 4-competitive for $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}$; every algorithm at best 2-competitive for $v \ge \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$; nothing possible for $v > \frac{1-\rho}{2\rho}$. Printed decision guidance: for $\rho > 0.3$ the CaC feasibility curve lies above the CAP curve, so **implement CaC** (guarantees half the intruders vs. CAP's quarter); for $\rho \le 0.3$ there exist $v$ values where any of the three algorithms may be chosen (p. 5). The gap between the CaC curve and Theorem III.2's lower-bound curve is explicitly flagged as open (p. 5).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated:**
1. Only proof outlines are given; "the detailed proofs are contained in [24]" (p. 2), and Lemma 4's and Corollary IV.3's proofs are "omitted for brevity" (pp. 4–5).
2. Open gap: "for the values of $v$ and $\rho$ that lie above the curve defined by the conditions for the CaC algorithm, either there may still exist an algorithm which is 2-competitive or it may be possible to tighten the analysis of Theorem III.2 and Lemma 3" (p. 5) — the boundary of achievable 2-competitiveness is not settled.
3. CAP is suboptimal within its own regime: no better than 3-competitive for $v \le \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}$ and exactly 4-competitive for $v \le \min\{\frac13, \frac{1-\rho}{6\rho}\}$ (Lemma 6, p. 5) — room for better 4-regime algorithms.
4. Future work (p. 6): intruders that "move outward or can actively evade the vehicle"; cooperative multi-vehicle defense in higher-dimensional environments that could lower the competitive ratios.
5. The numerical study covers a single $(\rho, \lambda)$ pair (0.2, 5); the authors themselves caution about the low-$\lambda$ artifact (p. 6) but do not sweep other $(\rho, \lambda)$.

**Critical observations (mine, with locations):**
6. The non-adaptive-input assumption (p. 2) is essential and restrictive: Lemma 1's waiting argument and all worst-case constructions break if arrivals may depend on the defender's position — yet the paper's own motivation ("intruders may coordinate their actions to overwhelm the defense", p. 1) is silent on this distinction, which is milder than full game-theoretic adaptivity but stronger than pure exogenous arrivals.
7. The competitive ratio compares **capture counts**, not capture times or weighted priorities; an offline OPT with unbounded lookahead can be arbitrarily better on instances where timing matters, and Definition 1 gives no runtime/latency guarantee (p. 2) — results do not directly transfer to DVR metrics such as makespan or flow time.
8. Several decisive steps are unverifiable from the conference text: Theorem III.1's stream-burst synchronization, Theorem III.2's five instances, Lemma 3's jump from "at least $\rho + 2\rho v$ from the origin" to the $\frac{\rho v}{1-\rho} + \frac{v^2}{(1+v)^2} \le \frac14$ condition, and Theorem IV.4's charging invariant (pp. 2–5). All must be taken from arXiv:2103.11787 [24].
9. References [22] (Borodin & El-Yaniv, *Online Computation and Competitive Analysis*) and [23] (Krumke et al., online traveling repairman) are listed but never cited in the body text (verified by text extraction) — an editorial slip, though [22] underlies the methodology.
10. The regime guidance "for $\rho > 0.3$ … implement CaC" (p. 5) is based on comparing guarantee curves, not on CAP vs. CaC simulations at the same $(v,\rho)$; Fig. 3's single-$\rho$ data cannot confirm the $\rho = 0.3$ threshold.
11. CaC's initial wait $\frac{1-\rho-3\rho v}{v}$ (Algorithm 1, p. 4) requires $1 - \rho - 3\rho v > 0$, i.e., $v < \frac{1-\rho}{3\rho}$, which is *not* listed among the stated CaC feasibility conditions (Lemmas 3–4) — presumably implied inside the regime but worth checking against [24].

## 8. Citation Value

This is the foundational conference paper of the line-environment worst-case perimeter-defense thread (followed by the randomized variant "Randomized Competitive Perimeter Defense on a Line" and the conical-environment extensions already present in this library). Its role in a related-work section is (a) as the citation that **imports competitive analysis into perimeter defense** — providing impossibility thresholds ($v > \frac{1-\rho}{2\rho}$: nothing possible; $v \ge \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$: $c(\mathcal{E}) \ge 2$) and the first algorithms with proven worst-case ratios (1-, 2-, 4-competitive) on a line segment; and (b) as the deterministic worst-case counterpoint to stochastic DVR boundary-guarding results [20], [21]. Suggested citation sentences: "Bajaj et al. [P005] introduced competitive analysis for perimeter defense on a line, proving that no algorithm is $c$-competitive when $v > \frac{1-\rho}{2\rho}$, that $c(\mathcal{E}) \ge 2$ whenever $v \ge \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$, and designing 1-, 2-, and 4-competitive sweeping, comparison-based, and patience-based algorithms for progressively harder regimes." Or, for gap-setting: "Worst-case guarantees for one-dimensional perimeter defense were initiated in [P005], which left open the exact boundary of 2-competitiveness between its CaC feasibility conditions and its $c(\mathcal{E}) \ge 2$ lower bound."
