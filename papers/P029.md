## 2. Research Background

Adversarial games (attack-defense, pursuit-evasion, herding, target-attacker-defender, racing) are the standard frameworks for designing opposing decision-making mechanisms in applications such as criminal pursuit, bird tracking, and network/national security (p. 16558) [authors' framing]. The dominant prediction model of an opponent's behavior is the Nash equilibrium (NE) "equilibrium model" [10]–[12], which assumes all players share the same decision mechanism and full information — an assumption that fails when decisions are opaque or humans are involved, because real players are boundedly rational and reasoning at unequal depths (p. 16558) [authors' claim; consistent with experimental level-k literature, refs. [13] Crawford–Iriberri Econometrica 2007, [14] Ho–Su Manage. Sci. 2012, cited as evidence that nonequilibrium models describe observed behavior better under asymmetric reasoning]. The level-k theory of behavioral game theory (each player best-responds to the belief that all opponents are level-(k−1)) has been used to model human-like bounded rationality, mainly in autonomous-driving scenarios (lane change/merge/intersections, refs. [23]–[26]) and once in pursuit-evasion ([4] Guan et al., ACC 2021) (pp. 16558–16559) [authors' claim; reference titles verified]. This paper builds a two-player attack-defense game in which the attacker has an unknown, fixed reasoning depth, and equips the defender with an online Bayesian inference scheme over the attacker's level, coupled to a receding-horizon multimodel interception strategy; the work is validated in 3-D Monte Carlo simulations and on an indoor ground-robot platform (pp. 16559, 16563–16566).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** Two players in Ω ⊂ R^n (n = 2, 3): a defender D must intercept an attacker A; the attacker must invade a protected area and avoid interception — "if the attacker approaches the protected area, the attacker wins. Conversely, if the defender intercepts the attacker, the defender wins" (p. 16560). Both plan by receding-horizon optimization with horizon H (eq. (4)). The attacker is a level-k bounded-rational decision maker with k unknown to the defender (Assumption 2, p. 16562). The defender must (i) construct prediction models of the attacker at every candidate level k ∈ K = {0, 1, …, kmax}, (ii) infer online which level the attacker is actually using, and (iii) generate an adaptive counter (interception) strategy (Sec. IV, p. 16562).

**Gap** (provenance marked):
- Equilibrium-model literature (NE-based AD games [6], [8], [9]; differential-game approaches [3], [7]) "assumes that all players have the same decision-making mechanism" and requires "sufficient depth of reasoning to reach the NE"; with opaque decisions or human players, "equilibrium models may not be able to predict or explain the observed behavior" (p. 16558) [authors' claim].
- Level-k exists for pursuit-evasion [4] and cognitive-hierarchy for CPS security [22], but these do not infer the opponent's level online [authors' claim, verified from the cited titles: [4] "Bounded-rational pursuit-evasion games"; [22] "Non-equilibrium dynamic games and cyber–physical security: A cognitive hierarchy approach"].
- The one-step-difference level-inference used in autonomous-driving work ([23]–[26]) computes k* ∈ arg min_k ‖y^t_A − ŷ^{t+1|t−1}_A[k]‖ from a single predicted point and works there because "their modes of motion are restricted to the traffic rules, trajectories at different k levels are easy to characterize" (p. 16562) [authors' claim]. "But in AD games, where players are free to move around, the decision outcomes at different levels of reasoning may be similar or identical. Hence, the one-step difference information is not sufficient to characterize the feature of a level-k player" (p. 16559) [authors' claim; supported by their own Fig. 4(a)–(b), where the predicted level-1 and level-2 trajectories coincide — p. 16564].
- [my observation] The genuinely new information-theoretic ingredient is the substitution of a single-step residual by a *statistical signature over a window of M historical receding-horizon predictions* (mean (10) + deviation (11)), i.e., the defender re-fits each level-k model's past one-step-ahead predictions against the observed trajectory, which separates levels even when their instantaneous predictions agree.

## 4. Core Contributions

Stated by the authors (p. 16559) and verified against the body:
1. **Modeling**: a nonequilibrium AD game with level-k rationality formulated in receding-horizon fashion (Secs. II–III): double-integrator dynamics (1), role-specific quadratic costs (2)–(3), receding-horizon program (4), and the recursive level-k best-response mechanism (5)–(6) with its best-response nesting (7)–(9) relating convergent infinite-level play to NE. Applicable to "adversarial games involving players with different computation and reasoning abilities" [authors' claim].
2. **Inference algorithm**: a Bayesian inference algorithm (Steps 1–4, eqs. (10)–(12)) that updates the defender's belief θ^t(k) over the attacker's reasoning level by scoring each level-k model's *historical receding-horizon prediction trajectories* (exponentially recency-weighted mean and deviation) against the attacker's actual position — explicitly replacing the one-step-difference inference of [23]–[26].
3. **Adaptive strategy**: a multimodel counter strategy (Step 5, eqs. (13a)–(13b), Algorithm 1) that blends the level-(k+1) counter strategies according to the updated belief, shown to raise the defender's win rate to 91–94% against any fixed level-k attacker (Table III) versus 27–56% for an NE defender (Table II).
4. **Experiment**: a hardware validation on a 2-D indoor AD game platform with three AGVs, 24-camera NOKOV motion capture, onboard Nvidia TX1 and MPC tracking (Figs. 6–9), demonstrating online level identification and interception under real dynamics mismatch (linear model vs Ackermann steering). A video is linked (footnote 1, p. 16566).

There are **no formal theorems, lemmas, propositions, or corollaries** in this paper; the mathematical content is the model (1)–(4), the recursive best-response characterization (5)–(9), the estimator (10)–(11), the Bayesian update (12), and the strategy (13), all stated without proofs (correctly so — none are needed beyond derivation-by-construction).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

**Equilibrium concept and Bayesian model (summary before details).** The base solution concept is *level-k best response*: a level-k player optimizes its cost against a model of the opponent playing level-(k−1), recursed down to level-0 ("opponent is stationary"). NE appears only as the k → ∞ fixed point of the best-response nesting (9) (Remark 2: "any convergent level-k strategy with infinite level of reasoning is a NE"; which NE depends on the level-0 choice). The defender's operating rule against a *believed* level-k attacker is the counter strategy u_D[k+1]; with an unknown level, the defender runs a Bayesian model-averaging scheme: prior θ^0 over K = {0, 1, …, kmax}, per-level Gaussian likelihoods built from historical receding-horizon predictions (10)–(11), posterior update (12), and a θ-weighted multimodel strategy (13a) with inertia (13b).

### 5.0 Dynamics, information structure, cost, termination (pp. 16560–16561, Table I)

- **Players/sets** (Table I, p. 16560): V = {D, A}; state x^t_j ∈ X_j ⊂ R^{2n} = [positions; velocities] along n axes; position y^t_j ∈ Y_j ⊂ R^n; action u^t_j ∈ U_j ⊂ R^n = acceleration vector; u^t_{−j}, x^t_{−j}, y^t_{−j} the opponent's counterparts; C_j : R^{2n} → R^n "dynamics constraint"; H ∈ R prediction horizon.
- **Dynamics, eq. (1)** (p. 16560): discrete-time second-order (double-integrator) model
  x^{t+1}_j = A x^t_j + B u^t_j,  y^t_j = C x^t_j, ∀j ∈ V,
  with A = [I_n, T·I_n; 0_{n×I_n}, I_n] ∈ R^{2n×2n}, B = [T²/2·I_n; T·I_n] ∈ R^{2n×n}, C = [I_n, 0·I_n]^T ∈ R^{n×2n}, T the sampling interval. [my observation] Exact zero-order double integrator — no model noise, no disturbance; all "adaptivity" enters through the belief channel, not the plant.
- **Defender cost, eq. (2)** (p. 16560): J_D(y^t_D, y^t_A, u^t_D) = ‖y^t_D − y^t_A‖²_{Q_D} (term1: close distance to intercept) + ‖u^t_D‖²_{R_D} (term2: control effort), with Q_D, R_D ∈ R^{n×n} positive definite.
- **Attacker cost, eq. (3)** (p. 16560): J_A(y^t_A, y^t_D, u^t_A) = ‖y^t_A − y_p‖²_{Q¹_A} (term1: approach the protected area position y_p) − ‖y^t_A − y^t_D‖²_{Q²_A} (term2: *maximize* distance to the defender, entering with a minus sign because J_A is minimized) + ‖u^t_A‖²_{R_A} (term3: effort); Q¹_A = ω¹_A I_n, Q²_A = ω²_A I_n, R_A positive definite. **Assumption (printed)**: ω¹_A > ω²_A — "the attacker's intention to invade the protected area is stronger than the intention to avoid the defender" (p. 16560). [my observation] y_p (target point of the protected area) is used in (3) but is *not* defined in Table I; in the simulations the protected area is a sphere of radius 3 centered at [−2, 15, 2]^T, so y_p is presumably its center — inferred, not printed.
- **Termination/winning**: attacker wins "if the attacker approaches the protected area"; defender wins by intercepting (p. 16560). Operationalized in simulations only: protected area = sphere, center [−2, 15, 2]^T, radius 3; "the defender's interception distance from the attacker is within 0.8" (p. 16563). No formal capture/target-set definitions or terminal costs are given.
- **Information structure**: Assumption 1 (p. 16561): "The defender and the attacker know each other's current position and optimization problem" — i.e., full observability of positions *and* of each other's cost/dynamics; the only unknown is the opponent's reasoning level (Assumption 2, p. 16562: "The attacker adopts a fixed level of decision-making reasoning in the AD game. The attacker and the defender do not know each other's k level.").

### 5.1 Receding-horizon game, eq. (4) (p. 16561)

For player j ∈ V with horizon H:
min_{u^{·|t}_j} Σ_{i=1}^{H} J_j(y^{t+i|t}_j, y^{t+i|t}_{−j}, u^{t+i|t}_j)  (4a)
s.t. y^{t+i}_j ∈ Y_j, u^{t+i}_j ∈ U_j, x^{t+i}_j ∈ X_j (4b), (x^{t+i}_j, u^{t+i}_j) ∈ C_j (4c),
decision variable u^{·|t}_j = {u^{t+1|t}_j, …, u^{t+H|t}_j}; abbreviated 𝒥_j(u^{·|t}_j). Rationale printed: "Continuous replanning over a longer time horizon H while executing only over a shorter horizon allows the defender to react to the attacker's dynamic behavior" (p. 16561); the defender executes the first action each decision cycle (p. 16563). Because (4a) is coupled to the opponent's future trajectory y^{·|t}_{−j}, each player must *predict* it — this is where game theory enters (p. 16561).

### 5.2 Level-k decision mechanism, eqs. (5)–(6) + level-0 rule (pp. 16561–16562)

- **Level set**: K = {0, 1, …, kmax}, finite.
- **Level-0 rule** (p. 16561): a level-0 player j "believes that his opponent is stationary": after observing y^t_{−j}, it assumes ŷ^{t+i|t}_{−j} = y^t_{−j} for i = 1, …, H, then computes u^t_j[0] from (4). "Level-0 players have no reasoning ability and cannot predict the trajectories of their opponents."
- **Level-k best response, eq. (5)** (p. 16561):
  𝒥_j(u^{t|t}_j[k]) = Σ_{i=1}^{H} J_j(y^{i|t}_j[k], ŷ^{i|t}_{−j}[k−1], u^{i|t}_j[k])  (5a)
  u^{t|t}_j[k] = arg min_{u_j∈U_j} 𝒥_j(u^{t|t}_j[k])  (5b) subject to (4b)–(4c),
  where ŷ^{·|t}_{−j}[k−1] is j's *prediction* of the opponent playing level-(k−1). [my observation] (5a) writes the prediction index as i|t where (4a) uses t+i|t — a notational shorthand/typo, harmless but noted.
- **Opponent prediction model, eq. (6)** (p. 16561): player j models the level-(k−1) opponent by
  𝒥_{−j}(û^{·|t}_{−j}[k−1]) = Σ_{i=1}^{H} J_{−j}(ŷ^{i|t}_{−j}[k−1], ŷ^{i|t}_j[k−2], û^{i|t}_{−j}[k−1])  (6a)
  û^{·|t}_{−j}[k−1] = arg min_{û_{−j}∈U_{−j}} 𝒥_{−j}(û^{·|t}_{−j}[k−1])  (6b),
  which itself requires j's own predicted level-(k−2) trajectory ŷ^{·|t}_j[k−2] (obtained by setting k → k−2 in (5)); "The level-k reasoning process is hierarchical and recursive, and the depth of reasoning continues until the level is reduced to 0" (p. 16561). Fig. 2 (p. 16561) illustrates the level-2 chain u_j[2] ← u_{−j}[1] ← u_j[0].
- **Consequence printed**: if a player believes its opponent "cannot deploy the NE but is using a level-k strategy, then the player has an incentive to respond with a level-(k+1) strategy rather than a NE to exploit his opponent's weaknesses"; under (5), "the best strategy is not necessarily a NE" (p. 16561).

### 5.3 Best-response nesting and the NE limit, eqs. (7)–(9), Remarks 1–2 (pp. 16561–16562)

Define BR(u_{−j}[k−1]) := arg min_{u_j∈U_j} J_j(u_j[k], u_{−j}[k−1]) (unnumbered, p. 16561). Then:
u_j[k] ∈ BR(u_{−j}[k−1])  (7);
u_{−j}[k+1] ∈ BR(u_j[k]),  u_j[k+2] ∈ BR(u_{−j}[k+1])  (8);
if k → +∞ and u_j[k+2] = u_j[k+1] = u_j[k], the fixed-point property u_{−j}[k+1] ∈ BR(u_j[k]), u_j[k] ∈ BR(u_{−j}[k+1]) holds  (9), and "if the policies converge to the strategy pairs at a specific k level, the two players reach a NE by applying the joint policy (u_j[k], u_{−j}[k+1])" (p. 16562).
- **Remark 1** (p. 16561): NE "requires that all players are playing the same game and have the same decision-making mechanism, which can be a too strong setting"; moreover "there may be multiple or infinite NEs in a game, and computing NE may be computationally intractable [8]".
- **Remark 2** (p. 16562): "any convergent level-k strategy with infinite level of reasoning is a NE. Furthermore, in cases where there are multiple NEs, it can not determine which equilibrium the level-k strategy will converge to, as this may depend on the choice of the level-0 strategy." [my observation] This is a characterization, not a proven convergence theorem — no conditions (e.g., contraction/monotonicity of BR, convexity of the coupled programs) are given under which the level-k iteration converges; the claim is presented as the standard behavioral-game-theory reading.

### 5.4 Baseline one-step level inference of [23]–[26] (unnumbered, p. 16562)

Construct level-k opponent models; compute the difference between the observed current position y^t_A and the first predicted point ŷ^{t+1|t−1}_A[k] from each model's prediction made at t−1; then k* ∈ arg min_{k∈{1,2,…}} ‖y^t_A − ŷ^{t+1|t−1}_A[k]‖, and the belief in k* increases. [my observation] As printed the argmin runs over k ∈ {1, 2, …} — level-0 is excluded from the printed formula, though the paper's own mechanism includes k = 0; minor inconsistency. The paper's critique: in free-motion AD games different levels' one-step predictions can coincide, so this residual is not identifying (pp. 16559, 16562; confirmed empirically in Fig. 4, p. 16564).

### 5.5 Historical-trajectory statistics, eqs. (10)–(11) with Fig. 3 (p. 16562)

**Step 3 mechanics.** The defender keeps the *sequences of past receding-horizon predictions* ŷ^{t|s}_A[k] (made at times s < t for the current time t, by each level-k model, built via (5)–(6) with the then-current observations). It samples no more than M points, 2 ≤ M ≤ H: if t ≤ M, all t−1 available predictions; if t > M, the M most recent (p. 16562). These are predictions of the *same* future instant t issued at different past times — the paper's key identifying statistic.

- **Exponentially recency-weighted mean, eq. (10)** (p. 16562):
  ȳ^t_A[k] = { Σ_{i=1}^{t−1} (2^{i−1}/(2^{t−1}−1)) ŷ^{t|i}_A[k],  2 ≤ t ≤ M ;
               Σ_{i=1}^{M}  (2^{i−1}/(2^M−1))   ŷ^{t|t−M+i−1}_A[k],  M < t }
  i.e., a prediction issued more recently gets a geometrically larger weight ("If the trajectory point is sampled from a more recent prediction sequence, it has a higher weight", p. 16562); example printed: ȳ^3_A[k] = (1/3)ŷ^{3|1}_A[k] + (2/3)ŷ^{3|2}_A[k]. [my observation — check] Weights sum to 1 since Σ_{i=1}^{m} 2^{i−1} = 2^m − 1; the index map in the second branch (i = 1 ⇒ prediction time t−M, i = M ⇒ t−1) is consistent with the recency ordering.
- **Deviation, eq. (11)** (p. 16562):
  σ^t_A[k] = { sqrt( Σ_{i=1}^{t−1} (ŷ^{t|i}_A[k] − ȳ^t_A[k])² / (t−1) ),  2 ≤ t ≤ M ;
               sqrt( Σ_{i=t−M}^{t−1} (ŷ^{t|i}_A[k] − ȳ^t_A[k])² / M ),  M < t }.
  [my observation — internal inconsistency] (11) is an *unweighted* RMS deviation around the *weighted* mean of (10); a weighted variance would be consistent with (10). Also for 2 ≤ t ≤ M it divides by t−1 (number of samples) rather than an unbiased t−2 correction — immaterial for the likelihood use but printed as such. Fig. 3 (p. 16562) visualizes the sample points ŷ^{t|s}[k] across prediction times s and levels k.

### 5.6 Bayesian belief update, eq. (12) (p. 16562)

**Step 4.** The defender models the scatter of level-k predictions as N(ȳ^t_A[k], (σ^t_A[k])²); φ^t_k(y^t_A | ȳ^t_A[k], σ^t_A[k]) is the likelihood of the attacker's actual position under level k, and
θ^{t+1}(k) = θ^t(k) φ^t_k(y^t_A | ȳ^t_A[k], σ^t_A[k]) / Σ_{k∈K} θ^t(k) φ^t_k(y^t_A | ȳ^t_A[k], σ^t_A[k])  (12),
with the normalization Σ_{k∈K} θ^t(k) = 1. **Prior** in simulations: θ^0 = [0.2, 0.5, 0.3]^T over levels {0, 1, 2} (p. 16563) — informative, not uniform.
- **Remark 3** (p. 16563): the method "makes full use of historical information rather than single-step information. This leads to the evident differences between posterior and prior beliefs, which are so-called Bayesian surprise [28]. And the surprise is believed to be a necessary trigger for Bayesian learning." [my observation] This is a heuristic justification; no analysis of likelihood overlap/identifiability (the case where level-1 and level-2 predictions coincide, admitted on p. 16564) is provided, and there is **no convergence proof** for θ^t(k) → 1{k = k_true} — convergence is only asserted via statistics (Remark 4, Fig. 5). Also note a degenerate-risk: σ^t_A[k] → 0 for a level whose historical predictions are all identical would make its Gaussian likelihood spike (unbounded likelihood ratio); no flooring/regularization of σ is printed.

### 5.7 Multimodel adaptive counter strategy, eqs. (13a)–(13b), Algorithm 1, Remark 3 (pp. 16562–16563)

**Step 5.** With belief θ^{t+1}, the defender's expected cost over counter-level strategies:
J_θ(u^{·|t}_D) = Σ_{k∈K} θ^{t+1}(k) J_D(u^{·|t}_D[k+1])  (13a)
u^{·|t}_D = (1 − a^t) u^{·|t−1}_D + a^t arg min_{u_D∈U_D} J_θ(u^{·|t}_D)  (13b),
a^t ∈ (0, 1] the strategy-update step size (a^t = 1 in all reported runs, so the inertia term is inactive); the defender "applies the first action u^{t+1|t}_D in a sequence u^{·|t}_D over one time step" (p. 16563). The counter doctrine is fixed: "If player j knows that the reasoning level of player −j is k and does not exceed the maximum level kmax, then player j adopts the counter strategy u^{·|t}_j[k+1]" (p. 16562) — i.e., the defender's candidate strategies are levels 1, …, kmax+1 = 3 for kmax = 2 (the defender's own depth may exceed the attacker's maximum).
- **Algorithm 1** (p. 16563), one defender decision cycle: Input: kmax; initial beliefs θ^t(k), k = 0,…,kmax; sample count M; current observation y^t_A. Output: θ^{t+1}(k); u^{·|t}_D. Lines: (1) build level-k attacker prediction models 𝒥_A(u^{·|t}_A[k]) by (5)–(6); (2) predict ŷ^{·|t}_A[k] for all k; (3) compute (10)–(11); (4) build N(ȳ, σ²), evaluate φ^t_k, update θ^{t+1} by (12); (5) compute (13a)–(13b) to get the multimodel strategy; (6) return θ^{t+1}(k), u^{·|t}_D.
- **proof gap (interpretive, p. 16563, eq. (13b))**: as printed, (13a) defines J_θ as a weighted sum of *already-minimized per-level counter-strategy costs* J_D(u^{·|t}_D[k+1]), yet (13b) applies "arg min_{u_D∈U_D} J_θ(u^{·|t}_D)" — the decision variable of the argmin is not well defined relative to (13a). Two readings are compatible with the text: (i) following the cited multimodel approach [26] (Li et al., ACC 2018), the defender computes each counter-trajectory u^{·|t}_D[k+1] against the level-k prediction ŷ^{·|t}_A[k] and the implemented strategy is the θ-weighted aggregate (with (13b) adding optional smoothing via a^t); or (ii) the defender re-solves (4) once against all level-k predicted attacker trajectories with a θ^{t+1}-weighted cost. The printed formulas do not disambiguate; with a^t = 1 both readings reduce to "execute the belief-optimal counter action", and the reported behavior (dashed predicted trajectories + one executed trajectory in Figs. 4, 9) is consistent with either.
- **Complexity [my observation]**: per decision instant, the defender solves kmax+1 nested prediction optimizations — each level-k model requires solving k+1 receding-horizon programs (chain down to level-0) — plus its own strategy computation; cost grows roughly quadratically in kmax. No timing figures are reported.

Claims validated: (a) one-level advantage pays — "in the case of level-k versus level-(k-1) … the defender has a high probability of winning, approaching 90%" (90% and 94%, p. 16563); (b) wrong-level assumptions degrade — level-k vs level-k (80–82%) and level-k vs level-(k−2) (75%) are lower; (c) **the headline nonequilibrium result**: an NE defender (NE computed by the IBR algorithm of [8], p. 16563–16564) *loses* to level-k attackers — 27% vs L0, 38% vs L1, 56% vs L2 — "In the case of NE versus level-k, the defender also gets a lower probability of winning" (p. 16564). [my observation] The L0-vs-L0 row at 82:18 shows the scenario geometry itself is defender-favored, which makes the NE-vs-L0 = 27:73 reversal all the more striking: playing NE against a nonstrategic opponent is worse than playing level-0. This is the paper's strongest empirical argument for nonequilibrium decision mechanisms.

### 6.3 Bayesian defender vs fixed-level attackers — Table III + Fig. 4 (pp. 16563–16564)

- **Table III** (100 MC each): Bayesian level-k vs L0 91 : 9; vs L1 94 : 6; vs L2 93 : 7. Claim validated: "the defender has a similar win rate when facing attackers of different levels. Thus, the proposed method helps the defender to generate a counter-strategy when facing an opponent with an unknown level of reasoning" (p. 16564) — i.e., the belief mechanism removes the wrong-level penalty visible in Table II.
- **Fig. 4** (p. 16564), one 3-D run: (a)–(c) trajectories — red solid = defender, black solid = attacker, dashed = defender's level-k predicted attacker trajectories, dotted = trajectory under the one-step inference of [23]–[26]; (d) Dis1 (defender–attacker distance) and Dis2 (attacker–protected-area distance), solid = proposed, dashed = [23]–[26]; (e) belief evolution. Claims validated: the proposed defender intercepts at **t = 30 s**, whereas under the [23]–[26] inference "the attacker reaches the protected area … at t = 33 s" (p. 16564) — the baseline loses this run. Belief dynamics printed: level-0 belief drops to 0 within 1–10 s (level-0 prediction "completely different from the trajectory of the attacker"); level-1 and level-2 beliefs rise together within 1–20 s because "the predicted level-1 trajectory coincides with the predicted level-2 trajectory"; within 20–30 s the level-2 belief converges to 1 because "only the predicted level-2 trajectory matches the attacker's motion trend" (p. 16564) — demonstrating exactly the identification failure mode of one-step methods that the historical-statistics approach is designed to overcome.

### 6.4 Convergence vs sample count M — Fig. 5 + Remark 4 (pp. 16564–16565)

- Protocol: 50 Monte Carlo runs per M; success criterion: "if the belief θ^{t_f}(k′) for the level-k′ attacker satisfies θ^{t_f}(k′) ≥ 0.5 at the end time of the simulation t_f, then θ^{t_f}(k′) has learned the correct level" (p. 16564); blue curve = defender win percentage, red = correct-convergence percentage, M swept 2–20 (axis read from Fig. 5).
- Printed finding: "the defender's win rate is positively correlated with the number of sample points M. Read approximately from Fig. 5: win fraction ≈ 0.70 at M = 2 rising to ≈ 0.95 by M ≈ 18–20; correct convergence ≈ 0.70 → ≈ 0.80 (values approximate — exact numbers not printed).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated:**
- Single defender vs single attacker only; "future research will focus on multiplayer game systems with limited rational players and their practical applications" (p. 16566, Conclusion).
- No theoretical convergence analysis of the Bayesian inference: "It is difficult to analyze this performance theoretically" (Remark 4, p. 16565) — convergence is demonstrated only by Monte Carlo statistics (Fig. 5), and even there the correct-convergence fraction plateaus around 0.8 (read from Fig. 5), i.e., in roughly one of five runs the belief does not settle on the true level although the defender still often wins.
- Convergence performance depends on "the duration of the game process, the initial setting of positions, and the estimated distribution of predicted trajectories" (Remark 4, p. 16565) — i.e., identifiability is scenario-dependent.

**Critical observations (mine, with locations):**
1. **Well-posedness of (13b)**: the argmin's decision variable is undefined as printed given (13a)'s already-minimized per-level costs (see §5.7 proof gap) — the exact multimodel operator inherited from [26] is not restated, so the strategy layer is not reproducible from the paper alone (p. 16563).
2. **No identifiability theory**: the paper itself shows level-1/level-2 predictions coincide early in the game (Fig. 4(a)–(b), p. 16564); nothing rules out persistent non-identification when a level-k and level-(k+1) model induce identical predicted trajectories over the whole window (e.g., a level-0-frozen opponent faced by a level-1 attacker yields nested predictions); the likelihood (12) then rides on the σ^t_A[k] scatter alone.
3. **Numerical safeguards absent**: σ^t_A[k] in (11) can approach zero (identical historical predictions), making the Gaussian likelihood in (12) nearly degenerate; no variance floor is printed (p. 16562).
4. **(11) vs (10) inconsistency**: unweighted deviation around a recency-weighted mean; divisor t−1 (not t−2) for 2 ≤ t ≤ M (p. 16562).
5. **Notation drift**: simulation weights printed as Q_A^0, Q_A^1 vs model's Q¹_A, Q²_A (p. 16563 vs eq. (3), p. 16560); y_p used in (3) but absent from Table I; (5a)/(6a) index predictions as i|t while (4a) uses t+i|t; "one-step" baseline argmin excludes k = 0 (p. 16562).
6. **Strong assumptions**: Assumption 1 gives both players the opponent's *optimization problem* (full cost knowledge — rarely true in security applications, and inconsistent in spirit with the bounded-information premise); Assumption 2 fixes the attacker's level within a game (no level switching/adaptation by the attacker, no adversarial manipulation of the belief channel — an attacker aware of the inference could masquerade at another level, unexamined).
7. **NE baseline scope**: the NE is computed by the IBR algorithm of [8] in simulation only; no analysis of whether the level-k recursion (5)–(6) itself converges for the printed quadratic games, and no theoretical comparison of level-(k+1) counter-strategy value vs NE value.
8. **Editorial**: several typos do not impede understanding — "NOVOK"/"NOKOV" (p. 16565), "AGversus" (p. 16565), "at the begging" (p. 16566); parameter table of the hardware experiments (AGV speeds, arena size, trial count) unreported.

## 8. Citation Value

**Role in the literature**: this is the bridge paper between behavioral game theory (level-k / cognitive hierarchy, refs. [13], [14], [20], [22]) and physical attack-defense/pursuit-evasion games: it supplies (a) a concrete receding-horizon (MPC-style) nonequilibrium AD game model with double-integrator players, (b) the observation that one-step trajectory-difference level inference (the autonomous-driving standard [23]–[26]) is non-identifying in free-motion games, and (c) a working alternative — Bayesian model averaging over level-k models whose likelihood is built from recency-weighted *historical* receding-horizon predictions — validated by the striking Table II result that an NE defender loses to level-0 attackers (27 : 73) while the Bayesian multimodel defender wins ≈ 93% against all levels. Use it as the bounded-rationality/online-opponent-modeling representative in any AD-game related-work paragraph, and as the natural baseline opponent-model class when proposing equilibrium-based or learning-based defenders. Suggested citation sentences: "Beyond equilibrium play, Fang and Yi [P029] show that in attack-defense games a Nash-equilibrium defender can be decisively beaten by boundedly rational level-k attackers, and that a Bayesian inference over the attacker's reasoning depth — built from recency-weighted historical receding-horizon predictions rather than one-step residuals — restores a ≈ 93% interception win rate without knowing the attacker's level"; or, for methodology surveys: "Level-k opponent models with online Bayesian identification of the opponent's reasoning depth have been demonstrated in receding-horizon attack-defense games on both Monte Carlo simulations and an AGV testbed [P029]."
