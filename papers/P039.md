## 2. Research Background

Capture-the-flag is a two-sided team game on a finite domain divided into two territories, each side protecting a flag that the opponent must capture and carry back to safety — an adversarial scenario with multiple, competing objectives (attack, defend, retreat) whose interplay makes it complex even with one player per side (p. 1451). It is a useful proxy for robotics problems where automation must coordinate with, and be legible to, human operators; the Cornell RoboFlag competition [6] had already produced results on multi-robot control [7]–[9] and human–robot interaction [10]. Prior robotic CTF and air-combat work handled the adversarial aspect by *assuming away* opponent rationality: mixed-integer linear programming for defense assuming straight-line attackers [7], linear programming against an assumed linear-feedback attacker [11], or MPC / approximate dynamic programming against predicted opponent models [12], [13]. Hamilton–Jacobi reachability under a differential-game setting had prior successes on aircraft collision avoidance [14], automated in-flight refueling [15], and autonomous quadrotor aerobatics [16], and this paper brings that machinery to CTF.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** Two players (one attacker A, one defender D) with simple motion on a rectangular domain $\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^2$; attacker must (i) enter the circular flag region $\mathcal{F}$ (radius $r_f$), (ii) then reach the rectangular return strip $\mathcal{R}$, while (iii) never letting the defender come within capture radius $r_c$. The defender is barred from $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R}$ and from leaving $\mathcal{D}$. The goal is to characterize, in the 4-D joint configuration space $(x^a,x^d)\in\mathbb{R}^4$, the **winning regions** $W_A$ (attacker) and $W_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus W_A$ (defender) and to synthesize the corresponding winning feedback strategies (p. 1452).

**Gap — [authors' claim] vs [verified]:**
- [Claim, p. 1451] "previous approaches often make various limiting assumptions upon player actions" — verified against the two CTF citations: [7] (Earl & D'Andrea) assumes "attackers proceed toward the goal in straight lines" and [11] (Chasparis & Shamma) solves "under an assumed attacker strategy in the form of a linear feedback law" (both quoted verbatim on p. 1451). Air-combat methods [12], [13] likewise "predict actions based upon expected opponent strategies" with re-planning for deviations. The claimed gap — no opponent-model-free, rational-play solution for CTF — is real for the CTF literature cited.
- [Claim, p. 1451] "This formulation allows each player to choose their control actions rationally based upon the current positions of the two players, without the use of explicit prediction models." — consistent with the HJI feedback-strategy synthesis of Sec. III-C; the strategies (8)–(9) are indeed functions of the current state only (through $\nabla\phi$).
- [Claim, p. 1453] "Although it is possible to use geometric analysis to construct some solutions to the game for simple configurations of the game regions, the numerical approach allows arbitrarily complex geometries to be addressed, including obstacles… Such sequencing is very difficult to analyze otherwise." — this is the authors' positioning of the numerical HJI approach against geometric/Apollonius-style solutions; no geometric CTF solution is cited or refuted in detail, so the comparison is programmatic rather than a verified impossibility claim.
- Residual gap the paper closes: no prior work treats the **sequential** structure of CTF (capture, then return, with defender repositioning in between) under full two-sided rationality; the paper's answer is the coupled target set $\tilde{R}_A=\mathcal{G}_F\cap R_A$ in eq. (5) (p. 1454).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Modeling**: first formulation of two-player CTF as a two-phase zero-sum differential game with simple motion, equal speed caps, capture radius, and rule-based state constraints (defender excluded from $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{D}^C$), with game rules encoded *as winning conditions for the opponent* rather than as hard state constraints (Sec. II p. 1452; Sec. III-B p. 1453).
2. **Theory (set characterization)**: characterization of the attacker's flag-return set $R_A$, flag-capture set $F_A$, and overall winning set $W_A$ as reach-avoid sets computed from a constrained HJI PDE (eqs. (3)–(5), p. 1454), including the sequential-composition device $\tilde{R}_A = \mathcal{G}_F\cap R_A$ that stitches the two phases; defender sets by complement ($F_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus F_A$, $R_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus R_A$, $W_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus W_A$, p. 1452).
3. **Algorithm**: synthesis of approximate feedback winning strategies wherever $\phi$ is differentiable, with closed-form expressions (8)–(9) for the simple-motion Hamiltonian, a phase-switching rule at the instant $x(t)\in\tilde{R}_A$, and a fixed-point (infinite-horizon) approximation (10)–(11) justified by numerical convergence (Sec. III-C, pp. 1454).
4. **Experiment/computation**: 4-D level-set computations with 2-D slices (Figs. 2–4) demonstrating (i) $W_D\supsetneq F_D\cup R_D$ — the defender exploits the capture phase to pre-block the return path — and (ii) closed-loop simulations whose outcomes match set membership in all four scenarios (Sec. IV, pp. 1454–1456).
5. **Application**: a smartphone app (Fig. 5, p. 1456) consuming offline-computed reachable sets to give online visualization and movement recommendations to human players — the automation-aided decision-making angle later expanded in the TCST 2015 journal version.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

The paper contains **no theorems, lemmas, propositions, or proofs** — it is a case-study/methods paper whose analytic content is the reach-avoid formulation and its consequences, all resting on cited theory ([14], [19], [20], [21], [23], [24]). Every analytic result is recorded below.

### 5.1 Game formulation: dynamics, information structure, constraints, termination (p. 1452)

- **State**: joint configuration $x=(x^a,x^d)\in\mathbb{R}^4$; permissible set $\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\setminus(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^4$. (Notational caveat: as literally printed the set-difference scope is ambiguous; the intended meaning, from "the defender is constrained to remain outside of the flag and return regions, while the attacker can move freely through either" (p. 1452), is $\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\{(x^a,x^d)\in\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}: x^d\notin\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R}\}$.)
- **Dynamics**: $\dot{x}^a=u$, $\dot{x}^d=d$ with $\|u\|_2\le V_{a,\max}$, $\|d\|_2\le V_{d,\max}$, and $V_{a,\max}=V_{d,\max}$ "for fairness" (p. 1452) — i.e., unit-speed simple motion after normalization. No obstacles in the base formulation; obstacles/geometry enter only through the constraint-encoding sets (Sec. 5.3).
- **Capture region** (unnumbered, p. 1452): $\mathcal{C}=\{(x^a,x^d)\in\mathbb{R}^4:\|x^a-x^d\|_2\le r_c\}$.
- **Information structure**: full observability; "the defender input $d(t)$ may be a function of the attacker input $u(t)$ at each time $t$" (p. 1452) — i.e., Player II (defender) holds the nonanticipative upper hand in the order of play, chosen "to prevent an infinite regression of second-guessing." The opposite order "leads to an equivalent problem formulation and identical solutions" (p. 1452), and footnote 1 (p. 1454) explains why: "the optimal defender and attacker inputs are independent of each other" (the Hamiltonian decouples in $(u,d)$, see 5.4).
- **Termination / victory conditions** (unnumbered bullet list, p. 1452, over a finite horizon $[0,T_f]$): attacker wins iff (i) *Flag capture*: $x^a(T_c)\in\mathcal{F}$ for some $0\le T_c\le T_f$; (ii) *Flag return*: $x^a(T_r)\in\mathcal{R}$ for some $T_c\le T_r\le T_f$; (iii) *Avoiding capture*: $x^a(t)\in\mathcal{D}\ \wedge\ (x^a(t),x^d(t))\notin\mathcal{C}$ for all $t\in[0,T_r]$. Defender wins by preventing any of (i)–(iii) up to $T_f$ while obeying $x^d\in\mathcal{D}\setminus(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R})$. The authors focus on the large-horizon regime "as $T_f$ approaches $\infty$" because small $T_f$ trivializes the attacker's options and induces simplistic delaying defenses (p. 1452).
- **Terminology** (pp. 1452–1453): Flag Capture Set $F_A$; Stop Capture Set $F_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus F_A$; Flag Return Set $R_A$; Stop Return Set $R_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus R_A$; attacker winning set $W_A$; defender winning set $W_D=\hat{\mathcal{D}}\setminus W_A$.

### 5.2 Result 1 — Reach-avoid operator and constrained HJI characterization (p. 1453, eq. (2))

- **Statement.** For $\dot{x}=f(x,u,d)$, $x(0)=x_0$ (eq. (1)), with Player I controls $u(t)\in\mathcal{U}$ (state-feedback policies $\mathcal{P}(T)$) and Player II strategies $d(t)\in\mathcal{D}$ allowed to depend on *both* $x(t)$ and $u(t)$ (strategies $\Gamma(T)$), the reach-avoid set $\mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A})$ — the set of $x_0$ from which $\exists\,\pi(T)\in\mathcal{P}(T)$ such that $\forall\,\gamma(T)\in\Gamma(T)$, $x(t)\in\mathcal{R}$ for some $t\in[0,T]$ and $x(s)\notin\mathcal{A}\ \forall s\in[0,t]$ — equals the zero sublevel set of the viscosity solution $\phi$ of the **constrained terminal-value HJI PDE**
$$\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\min\!\Big[0,\,H\Big(x,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\Big)\Big]=0,\qquad \phi(x,0)=\phi_{\mathcal{R}}(x),\qquad \text{subject to } \phi(x,t)\ge-\phi_{\mathcal{A}}(x),\tag{2}$$
with optimal Hamiltonian $H(x,p)=\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\,p^{\mathsf T}f(x,u,d)$, and $\mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n:\phi(x,-T)\le 0\}$ (p. 1453).
- **Assumptions**: "suitable conditions, given in [14] and [19]" (p. 1453) — not restated; $\phi$ is the viscosity solution in the sense of Crandall–Lions [21]. Sets represented as sublevel sets $\mathcal{G}=\{x:\phi_{\mathcal{G}}(x)\le 0\}$.
- **Proof approach**: none given; cited wholesale to Mitchell–Bayen–Tomlin [14] (TAC 2005) and Mitchell's thesis [19]. The $\min[0,\cdot]$ switching is the constrained (state-constraint-enforcing) formulation; the constraint $\phi\ge-\phi_{\mathcal{A}}$ makes $\mathcal{A}$ an invariant barrier.
- **Physical meaning**: $\phi\le 0$ regions are exactly the configurations from which Player I can force reaching $\mathcal{R}$ while avoiding $\mathcal{A}$ against *any* Player II behavior — the game-theoretic (worst-case) generalization of backward reachable tubes.
- **Extensibility**: the operator is geometry-agnostic — only the level-set functions $\phi_{\mathcal{R}},\phi_{\mathcal{A}}$ change with the scenario; this is what lets the authors claim arbitrary geometries including obstacles (p. 1453).
- **Numerics**: solved with the Level Set Toolbox for MATLAB [22] (UBC), described as "a highly accurate numerical solution" (p. 1453).

### 5.3 Result 2 — Composition of the CTF victory sets (pp. 1453–1454, eqs. (3)–(5))

The attacker is Player I (minimizer of the HJI Hamiltonian), the defender Player II. Game-rule constraints are encoded "as part of the winning conditions for the other" player (p. 1453): with $\mathcal{D}^C$ the complement of $\mathcal{D}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and the "bad" sets
$$\mathcal{G}_A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^4: x^a\in\mathcal{D}^C\},\qquad \mathcal{G}_D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^4: x^d\in\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{D}^C\},$$
the attacker's flag-return winning condition is $\mathcal{O}_R\vee\mathcal{O}_D$ (unnumbered, p. 1453):
$$\mathcal{O}_R=\{\exists t\in[0,T_r]:\ x^a(t)\in\mathcal{R}\ \wedge\ x(s)\notin\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A\ \forall s\in[0,t]\},\qquad
\mathcal{O}_D=\{\exists t\in[0,T_r]:\ x^d(t)\in\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{D}^C\ \wedge\ x(s)\notin\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A\ \forall s\in[0,t]\},$$
i.e., the attacker also wins if the *defender* violates its own constraints (enters flag/return region or leaves the domain). The same encoding "can also be used to encode other state constraints such as obstacles" (p. 1453).

- **Result (3) — Flag Return Set** (p. 1454): with $\mathcal{G}_R=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^4: x^a\in\mathcal{R}\}$,
$$R_A=\mathcal{R}^A_{T_r}(\mathcal{G}_R\cup\mathcal{G}_D,\ \mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A).\tag{3}$$
Target = attacker reaches $\mathcal{R}$ **or** defender breaks its constraints; avoid = capture region plus attacker leaving the domain.
- **Result (4) — Flag Capture Set** (p. 1454): with $\mathcal{G}_F=\{x: x^a\in\mathcal{F}\}$ and attacker condition $\mathcal{O}_F\vee\mathcal{O}_D$, $\mathcal{O}_F=\{\exists t\in[0,T_c]: x^a(t)\in\mathcal{F}\wedge x(s)\notin\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A\ \forall s\in[0,t]\}$,
$$F_A=\mathcal{R}^A_{T_c}(\mathcal{G}_F\cup\mathcal{G}_D,\ \mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A).\tag{4}$$
- **Result (5) — Overall Attacker Winning Set** (the paper's central analytic device, p. 1454): arriving at the flag is insufficient — if the joint state at flag capture lies outside $R_A$, "the defender can prevent the attacker from returning safely to $\mathcal{R}$". Hence the attacker must drive the *joint* state into $\tilde{R}_A=\mathcal{G}_F\cap R_A$ (flag captured AND return still winnable), giving the modified objective $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_F\vee\mathcal{O}_D$ with $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_F=\{\exists t\in[0,T_c]: x(t)\in\tilde{R}_A\wedge x(s)\notin\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A\ \forall s\in[0,t]\}$ and
$$W_A=\mathcal{R}^A_{T_c}(\tilde{R}_A\cup\mathcal{G}_D,\ \mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{G}_A).\tag{5}$$
- **Assumptions**: horizons $T_c,T_r$ finite and (implicitly) compatible with $T_f$; the composition is exact for the game as posed when the second phase retains enough time; the authors explicitly target $T_f\to\infty$ where the timing bookkeeping becomes immaterial (p. 1452). Proof approach: none — (3)–(5) are definitions-by-operator, correct by construction of the encoded winning conditions; the reachability theory guaranteeing correctness of the operator is cited.
- **Physical meaning**: (5) is a *sequential composition* of two reach-avoid problems — the second phase's winning set becomes the first phase's terminal target set. This is what makes CTF (attack-then-retreat with a defender that repositions) tractable numerically, which the authors argue "is very difficult to analyze otherwise" (p. 1453). It is the direct ancestor of the phase-bridging machinery later formalized in the TCST 2015 version and of the "transition phase" of the closed-form treatment in Garcia–Casbeer (P038).
- **Proof gap (minor, timing)**: eqs. (3)–(5) use fixed horizons $T_r$, $T_c$ never tied numerically to $T_f$; the composition is exactly equivalent to the original victory conditions only in the large-$T_f$ regime where the sets converge (see 5.4); the paper asserts this regime informally (pp. 1452, 1454) without a proposition.

### 5.4 Result 3 — Winning strategy synthesis (p. 1454, eqs. (6)–(11))

- **Statement.** Following Lygeros–Tomlin–Sastry [23] and Tomlin et al. [24], wherever the solution $\phi$ of (2) is differentiable, for $x\in\mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A})$ Player I's winning feedback law is
$$u^*(x,t)=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\,p(x,-t)^{\mathsf T}f(x,u,d),\quad t\in[0,T],\tag{6}$$
and for $x\notin\mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A})$ Player II's blocking law is
$$d^*(x,t)=\arg\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\,p(x,-t)^{\mathsf T}f(x,u^*,d),\quad t\in[0,T],\tag{7}$$
with $p=\partial\phi/\partial x$. "For pursuit-evasion games, the solution to the HJI equation is typically differentiable away from some singular surfaces where the winning strategies are not uniquely defined [18]. With arbitrarily small perturbations from these surfaces, the solution becomes differentiable" (p. 1454) — a standard dispersal-surface caveat, cited to Basar–Olsder [18].
- **Explicit form for CTF.** The capture-the-flag Hamiltonian
$$H(x,p)=\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\ p_u^{\mathsf T}u+p_d^{\mathsf T}d,\qquad p_u=(p_1,p_2),\ p_d=(p_3,p_4),$$
separates in $(u,d)$, so the saddle-point inputs are the decoupled extremals
$$u^*(x,t)=-V_{a,\max}\frac{p_u(x,-t)}{\|p_u(x,-t)\|_2},\tag{8}\qquad
d^*(x,t)=V_{d,\max}\frac{p_d(x,-t)}{\|p_d(x,-t)\|_2}.\tag{9}$$
i.e., the attacker drives **along** $-\nabla_{x^a}\phi$ (steepest descent of $\phi$) and the defender **along** $+\nabla_{x^d}\phi$, both at full speed. Footnote 1 (p. 1454): the player order does not affect the strategies because these optimal inputs are mutually independent.
- **Two-phase strategy switching** (p. 1454): under the attacker law built from $W_A$ via (8), the joint trajectory is "guaranteed to enter $\tilde{R}_A$ within $[0,T_c]$"; at the first instant $x(t)\in\tilde{R}_A$ the attacker switches to the law built from $R_A$ via (8) with the (3) value function and "safely return[s] to $\mathcal{R}$, thus winning the game."
- **Infinite-horizon approximation** (p. 1454): if $\lim_{t\to-\infty}\phi(x,t)=\phi_\infty(x)$ exists, choose index $k_0$ at sampling instants $-kT_s$ such that $\|\phi(\cdot,-k_0T_s)-\phi(\cdot,-(k_0+1)T_s)\|_\infty<\epsilon$; then the time-invariant laws
$$u^*(x)\approx\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\,p(x,-k_0T_s)^{\mathsf T}f(x,u,d),\tag{10}\qquad
d^*(x)\approx\arg\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\,p(x,-k_0T_s)^{\mathsf T}f(x,u^*,d)\tag{11}$$
approximate the long-term play. The paper asserts "for our application, the sets $R_A$ and $W_A$ indeed converge numerically for sufficiently large $T_r$ and $T_c$, leading to approximate long-term strategies" (p. 1454) — convergence is **empirical/numerical, not proven** (see Sec. 7).
- **Proof gap (acknowledged by design)**: derivatives of numerically computed $\phi$ are "not available in closed form"; approximate laws are obtained by temporal discretization at instants $kT$ and numerical differentiation of $\phi(x,-kT)$ (p. 1454), so all strategy claims are approximate up to grid error; the guarantee is inherited from [23], [24] only where $\phi$ is smooth.

**Numerical error study (p. 1456)**: the only quantitative robustness discussion — grid-based HJ differentiation "is poorly equipped to handle sharp set boundaries"; near the intersection of $F_D$ with $\mathcal{F}$ the zero sublevel set "sometimes slightly under-approximates the stop capture set," producing defender trajectories starting just outside $F_D$ (below grid resolution) that nevertheless capture the attacker. Remedies: finer discretization (costlier) or over-bounding the error by $\epsilon$ and choosing the $-\epsilon$/$+\epsilon$ level-set boundaries to guarantee the attacker's/defender's winning conditions, respectively.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated:**
1. Two players only, full observability (p. 1452); extension "to scenarios with partial observability and teams of multiple players" is future work (p. 1456).
2. Equal speed cap assumed "for fairness" (p. 1452) — heterogeneous speeds not treated.
3. Grid-induced errors near sharp boundaries; under-approximation of $F_D$ near $\mathcal{F}$; error-bounding by $\pm\epsilon$ level sets proposed but not formalized (p. 1456).
4. Strategies are approximate: valid only where $\phi$ is differentiable (singular/dispersal surfaces excluded, handled only "with arbitrarily small perturbations", p. 1454) and computed from numerical derivatives (p. 1454).
5. Time-invariant long-term strategies rely on numerically observed convergence of $R_A$, $W_A$ to fixed points (p. 1454) — an empirical claim, not a theorem.

**Critical observations (this archive):**
6. **No formal results**: the paper offers no proposition on existence/uniqueness of the value, on determinacy of the two-phase composition, or on correctness of the switched strategy; everything is inherited from [14], [19], [23], [24]. The convergence at ~6 time units (p. 1454) and the exactness of (5) for finite $T_f$ are asserted, not proven (cf. the $T_c+T_r$ vs. $T_f$ bookkeeping gap noted after eq. (5)).
7. **Notation**: $\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\setminus(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R})$ (p. 1452) is loosely written — the attacker must be *allowed* inside $\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R}$ during play, so the restriction is defender-only; the HJI formulation actually enforces it through the $\mathcal{O}_D$ winning condition rather than through the state space (p. 1453).
8. **"Winning" is numerical**: because $\phi$ is computed on a grid and the laws (8)–(11) use discrete derivatives, the guaranteed-capture/guaranteed-escape claims hold only up to discretization error — the authors themselves exhibit a case where the defender wins from outside the computed set (p. 1456). The $\pm\epsilon$ fix is sound in spirit (reminiscent of numerical over/under-approximation of reachable tubes) but no error bound is derived.
9. **Scalability**: 4-D state at 45 pts/dim ≈ 4.1M grid nodes ≈ 1 h per set on 2011 laptop hardware; each new attacker position slice, geometry change, or parameter change requires recomputation — the app in Fig. 5 works only with offline precomputed sets, and dimension growth (multi-player) would make the approach intractable (motivating the later closed-form/geometric literature, e.g., P038's analytic CTF solution).
10. **Scenario geometry**: base formulation has no obstacles; obstacles and rule regions enter only via the constraint sets $\mathcal{G}_A,\mathcal{G}_D$ (pp. 1453, 1456) — claimed extensible, demonstrated only for the plain rectangular domain.

## 8. Citation Value

This is the **founding paper of the differential-game treatment of capture-the-flag**: it fixed the now-standard two-phase decomposition (flag capture → flag return), the constraint-encoding trick (a player's rule violation is the opponent's winning condition, reusable for obstacles), and the sequential-composition device $\tilde{R}_A=\mathcal{G}_F\cap R_A$ (eq. (5)) by which HJI reach-avoid sets handle games with staged objectives — a device that later reappears, in closed form, as the "transition phase" of the analytic CTF solution (Garcia et al., CDC 2018 / P038) and in the journal extension (Huang et al., TCST 2015, with the smartphone-based automation-assisted CTF system). Within this library it serves as: (a) the canonical numerical-HJI benchmark against which geometric/Apollonius CTF and reach-avoid solutions are compared (P038 explicitly cites it as the approximation-based precursor); (b) an early example of human-in-the-loop use of reachable sets (Fig. 5), relevant to abstraction/interface work [17]; and (c) evidence for the empirical fact that the combined defender winning set strictly contains the union of the phase-wise winning sets ($W_D\supsetneq F_D\cup R_D$, Fig. 2) — the phenomenon that justifies transition-phase analysis in any two-phase pursuit game. Suggested citation sentence: "Huang et al. [P039] formulated two-player capture-the-flag as a zero-sum differential game and characterized the attacker's and defender's winning regions via constrained HJI reach-avoid computations, sequentially composing the flag-capture and flag-return phases through the coupled target set $\tilde{R}_A=\mathcal{G}_F\cap R_A$ and synthesizing approximate winning feedback strategies from the value-function gradient; their numerical study showed that the defender's overall winning set strictly contains the union of the phase-wise winning sets, since the defender can exploit the capture phase to pre-empt the return path."
