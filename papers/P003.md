## 2. Research Background

The paper sits at the interface of classical guidance-and-interception theory (pure pursuit, constant-bearing/lead pursuit, proportional navigation — the missile-guidance canon of Zarchan's textbook) and modern deep RL for continuous control. The authors' motivating observation is a classical dilemma: geometric/rule-based interceptors are computationally cheap and interpretable but brittle under speed mismatch, maneuvering evaders, and infeasible geometries, whereas RL policies adapt but "when trained from scratch... may initially lack structure and produce unreliable results" (p. 1). The framing scenario is perimeter/airspace defense: a circular protected zone with tripwire sensors; an intruder that either crosses the boundary or heads for a protected goal; and interceptors launched on detection (Fig. 1, p. 2). The design inspiration is explicitly credited to Weintraub, Von Moll, and Pacheter's range-limited pursuit-evasion formulation (AIAA SciTech 2023), which categorizes PE feasibility from kinematic and sensor constraints and shows classical interception can fail when evader speed and escape geometry preclude guaranteed capture (p. 1, Related Work). The work is an AFRL–Bowie State collaboration (public-release AFRL-2026-0443), positioned as a step toward realistic autonomous defense systems, with a companion single-agent validation followed by a multi-agent extension in which pursuer and evader policies co-evolve via adversarial self-play.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem** (authors' formulation, pp. 1–2): given a circular defended domain of radius $R$ with stationary detection sensors, launch a pursuer upon detection to intercept an evader before the evader (i) crosses the protected boundary or (ii) reaches a designated goal region; do so in real time for both a single pursuer–single evader instance and a 4-pursuer vs. 3-evader two-station defense. The controller must remain reliable when the classical intercept problem is infeasible or ill-conditioned (evader faster than the assumed speed, direction changes, negative/undefined intercept time).

**Gap as claimed by the authors** [authors' claim, p. 1]: (i) classical methods "are often brittle in real-world settings with uncertainty, noise, or infeasible initial configurations"; (ii) RL trained from scratch is unstructured/unreliable; (iii) existing hybrid RL+classical control work (their cited exemplar is Yoo, Kim, and Shim 2020, RL+PID for quadrotor flight, IEEE TIE) does not cover pursuit-evasion interception, and multi-agent PE with RL-trained adversarial flocks is underexplored. Their fill: a PPO + classical-intercept blending architecture with curriculum learning, extended to multi-agent station defense trained by alternating adversarial self-play.

**[Verified from cited text]**: The cited anchors check out bibliographically — Schulman et al. 2017 is the PPO arXiv report (arXiv:1707.06347, printed in the reference list as such); Bengio et al. 2009 (ICML) is curriculum learning; Reynolds 1987 (SIGGRAPH) is the flocking model the authors deliberately do NOT hard-code; Lowe et al. 2017 (NeurIPS) is MADDPG/multi-agent actor-critic, cited as the multi-agent RL context; Weintraub, Von Moll, and Pachter 2023 "Range-Limited Pursuit-Evasion" (AIAA SciTech Forum) exists and its scope (feasibility under range/sensor limits) is consistent with how the authors use it — though the paper cites it only as inspiration and inherits **no theorem or feasibility criterion** from it (no Apollonius/barrier construction appears anywhere in this paper). The claim that no prior work combines classical PE interception heuristics with PPO blending under curriculum + self-play is the authors' positioning and is not substantiated by a systematic literature comparison — no related hybrid-interception baseline is experimentally compared (baselines are classical-only controllers).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Modeling**: A range-limited circular perimeter-defense environment (single-agent: radius 10, 3 tripwire sensors at 120°; multi-agent: radius 50 m, 2 stations, 4 pursuers with 35 m radar/360° FOV/50 m comms/battery model, 3 RL-trained evader flock) with explicit win/lose/timeout conditions (Sec. 5.6 below; pp. 2–5).
2. **Algorithm (single-agent)**: Hybrid PPO interception — the control input is a convex blend $u_{hybrid} = \omega \hat u_{classic} + (1-\omega)\hat u_{RL}$ (eq. (5), p. 2) with the classical component computed from a relative-kinematics intercept solver (eqs. (1)–(4)), $\omega$ annealed from 1 toward 0 during training; three-phase speed-ratio curriculum (0.7× → 0.9× → 1.1× evader/pursuer speed; p. 2); a shaping reward that includes a classical-bridge term $+0.2\cos(\theta_{err})$ on intercept-heading alignment (Table 1, p. 3). Algorithm 1 (p. 3) states the 5-line control loop.
3. **Algorithm (multi-agent)**: Alternating adversarial self-play (Algorithm 2, p. 6): freeze evader policy, train pursuers 100k PPO steps; freeze pursuers, train evaders 100k steps; iterate over 5M total timesteps (Table 4). Evader flock has explicit role encoding (leader bit $r_i$, eq. (6), p. 4) and shaped flocking rewards (separation/cohesion/alignment via Table 3) instead of hard-coded Reynolds rules; emergent role specialization (leader/follower/decoy) is reported (p. 5).
4. **Experiments**: Two evaluation suites, 100 episodes each, hybrid vs. classical baselines: single-agent capture rate 94% vs. 24%, median capture time 1.8 s vs. 5.0 s, mean reward 33.3 vs. −4.4 (Fig. 2, p. 4); multi-agent defense success rate 78.5% vs. 52.3%, per-role capture rates, capture-time IQRs, battery efficiency 45.2% vs. 28.7%, pursuer win rate 68% vs. 35% (Table 5, Fig. 4, p. 7).
5. **Analysis (qualitative)**: behavioral analysis (curved cut-off trajectories, trapping), self-play strategy co-evolution narrative (goal-seeking → flock splitting → zone defense → decoys → station-proximity target prioritization; p. 8), failure-case analysis (speed ratios > 4:1 evader:pursuer; simultaneous two-station attacks), and an interpretability argument (every action decomposes into classical + learned components; p. 8).

**No formal theory**: the paper contains no theorem, lemma, proposition, or proof; all results are empirical. Section 5 therefore records every analytic artifact (7 numbered equations + 2 algorithms + 2 reward tables) exactly as printed.

### 5.1 Classical geometric interception (p. 2, eqs. (1)–(4))

Setup: evader position $\vec p_e$, velocity $\vec v_e$; pursuer position $\vec p_p$, velocity $\vec v_p$; both assumed constant over the engagement. Find intercept time $t$ and intercept point $\vec I$ satisfying

$$\vec I = \vec p_e + \vec v_e\, t = \vec p_p + \vec v_p\, t \tag{1}$$

Rewriting,

$$(\vec v_e - \vec v_p)\, t = \vec p_p - \vec p_e \tag{2}$$

"This is solved for $t$ using vector projection if $\vec v_e \neq \vec v_p$ and $t > 0$":

$$t = \frac{(\vec p_p - \vec p_e)\cdot(\vec v_e - \vec v_p)}{\|\vec v_e - \vec v_p\|^2} \tag{3}$$

and the intercept point is

$$\vec I = \vec p_e + \vec v_e\, t \tag{4}$$

- **Meaning**: produces a constant-bearing / lead-pursuit path, which the authors contrast with pure pursuit's tail chase; "performs well under straight-line motion assumptions but can fail if the evader is faster or changes direction frequently. In such cases, the computed $t$ may be negative or undefined, indicating infeasibility" (p. 2).
- **Analytic gap (this archive's verification, not the authors')**: eq. (2) is a 2-scalar vector equation in ONE unknown $t$ and in general has no exact solution unless $\vec p_p - \vec p_e \parallel (\vec v_e - \vec v_p)$. Eq. (3) is the least-squares projection of the relative-separation vector onto the relative-velocity direction, i.e., the time of closest approach of the straight-line relative motion, NOT a guaranteed collision time; after heading to $\vec I$, a miss distance generally remains and would need a second correction. The paper never states this residual and never derives the true constant-bearing solution (which solves for the pursuer's heading angle by aligning $(\vec v_e - \vec v_p)$ with the line of sight — the classical lead-collision-course construction). Consequently the claimed "infeasibility" signal (t ≤ 0 or $\vec v_e = \vec v_p$) is only a partial feasibility test: even with $t>0$ the computed $\vec I$ can lie beyond the boundary or behind the evader's escape arc before the (slower) pursuer arrives. A rigorous feasibility treatment would require the range-limited PE / Apollonius-type analysis the authors cite (Weintraub et al. 2023) but do not use. **Proof gap: no error bound between the eq. (3) projection time and any true intercept time is given; the solver's correctness is asserted by construction only (p. 2, eqs. (2)–(3)).**
- **Extensibility**: the solver is used both as the action prior (through eq. (5)) and as an observation feature ("classical intercept angle" in the RL state, Algorithm 1, p. 3) — the dual use is the paper's actual "classical-to-learned bridge" mechanism.

### 5.2 Hybrid action blending (p. 2, eq. (5))

$$\vec u_{hybrid} = \omega \cdot \hat u_{classic} + (1-\omega)\cdot \hat u_{RL}, \qquad \omega \in [0,1] \tag{5}$$

- $\omega$ is the trust scalar between classical and learned behavior: "During early training, $\omega$ is closer to 1 to stabilize learning. As training progresses, $\omega$ is gradually annealed to rely more on the learned policy" (p. 2). **The annealing schedule (initial value, final value, step/count) is UNREPORTED** — this is the paper's central "dynamic-domain adaptation" knob and its dynamics are not specified anywhere.
- **Dynamic-domain adaptation mechanism (assembled from p. 2)**: two coupled mechanisms — (a) $\omega$-annealing on the action blend, (b) a three-phase curriculum on the evader/pursuer speed ratio: Phase 1 evader speed 0.7× pursuer with direct path; Phase 2 0.9×; Phase 3 1.1× with random exits (citing Bengio et al. 2009). This lets the agent master geometrically solvable engagements before facing evader-faster edge cases where eq. (3) fails.
- **RL observation and role of the classical channel**: "The RL agent observes the evader's position, velocity, and the classical intercept heading. Its output modifies the heading direction, allowing for smarter maneuvers like cutting across arcs, cornering, or trapping" (p. 2).

### 5.3 Algorithm 1 — Hybrid PPO Interception Control (p. 3)

```
Input: Observed state s_t = {pursuer pos, evader pos, evader vel, classical intercept angle}
1: a_classic ← InterceptSolver(s_t)
2: a_RL ← π_θ(s_t)                    (PPO policy)
3: a_t ← ω·a_classic + (1−ω)·a_RL
4: Update pursuer heading with a_t and take action
```

(Step numbering as printed: lines 2–5 combine the input line.) Note that both branches consume the same state $s_t$, and the state contains the classical intercept angle — i.e., the learned policy can learn to override or exploit the classical prior, while eq. (5) additionally hard-wires the prior into the action.

### 5.4 Reward structures

**Table 1, Single-Agent Reward Structure (p. 3)** — terminal: successful capture +50.0; evader escapes boundary −30.0; evader reaches goal −30.0; timeout (max steps) −10.0. Per-step shaping: closing distance to evader $+1.0\,\Delta d$; increasing distance $-0.5\,\Delta d$; time step penalty $-0.05$; boundary proximity penalty $-0.3$; intercept angle alignment $+0.2\cos(\theta_{err})$. The authors single out the alignment term as "bridging the classical geometric strategy with learned behavior... particularly important during early curriculum phases" (p. 3). ($\Delta d$ sign convention and $\theta_{err}$ definition are not printed; $\theta_{err}$ is evidently the heading error to the predicted intercept point.)

**Table 3, Multi-Agent Reward Structure (p. 6)** — pursuer/evader columns: capture evader / get captured +20.0 / −15.0; evader reaches goal −10.0 / +30.0; all evaders eliminated +15.0 / –; timeout (survival) −5.0 / +8.0; closing distance +0.5·Δd / –; progress to goal – / +1.0·Δg; evasion success – / +0.8; decoy sacrifice bonus – / +8.0; time penalty −0.01 / −0.01; station unguarded −1.0 / –; low battery penalty −0.2 / –. Two structural observations: (i) the pursuer's "station unguarded" −1.0 per-step penalty is what the authors credit for the emergent partner-coverage division of labor (p. 8); (ii) the evader's +8.0 decoy-sacrifice bonus plus the explicit leader bit (eq. (6)) means decoy behavior is partly reward-engineered, not fully emergent — despite the "emergent role specialization" narrative (p. 5).

### 5.5 Evader flock formulation (pp. 4–5, eqs. (6)–(7))

Role-encoded observation (p. 4):

$$s_t^{evader} = [\,s_t^{shared},\; r_i\,], \qquad r_i \in \{0,1\} \ \text{one-hot: leader } (r_i{=}1),\ \text{support } (r_i{=}0) \tag{6}$$

with roles explicitly assigned at episode start (1 leader, 2 support agents) "to ensure consistent flock structure" (p. 4) — note the tension: the same page's Role Specialization paragraph (p. 5) claims self-play yields leader/follower/decoy specialization, while the results section names the roles leader/flanker 1/flanker 2 (p. 6). Three inconsistent role vocabularies (leader+support; leader+follower+decoy; leader+flanker1+flanker2) coexist; and a scalar $r_i\in\{0,1\}$ cannot one-hot encode a 3-way role distinction. **Proof gap: the role encoding as printed supports only 2 roles while results report 3 roles.**

Evader control map (p. 5):

$$\vec v_{evader} = \pi_\phi(s_t^{evader}) \tag{7}$$

$\pi_\phi$ outputs continuous velocity commands balancing goal-seeking, evasion, and flock coordination; separation/cohesion/alignment are encouraged through shaped rewards (Table 3) rather than Reynolds' hard-coded rules (p. 4–5). Evader observation (shared part, p. 4): own position relative to stations and pursuers, teammate positions, detected threats, and flock formation state.

### 5.6 Algorithms 2, dynamics, information structure, win conditions

**Algorithm 2 — Adversarial Self-Play Training (p. 6)**: initialize pursuer policy $\pi_\theta$, evader policy $\pi_\phi$; $k \leftarrow 100{,}000$ (alternation interval, environment steps); for iteration = 1 to N: Phase 1 freeze $\pi_\phi$, collect $k$ steps with pursuers acting/evaders fixed, update $\pi_\theta$ with PPO; Phase 2 freeze $\pi_\theta$, collect $k$ steps with evaders acting/pursuers fixed, update $\pi_\phi$ with PPO; evaluate both, save checkpoints. Rationale printed: pursuers counter the latest evaders, evaders exploit pursuer weaknesses, neither side overfits a static opponent, and alternating updates are "stable compared to simultaneous updates which can diverge" (p. 5). N is not printed; with 5,000,000 total timesteps (Table 4) and $k=100{,}000$, N = 50 alternations is implied (inference, not printed).

**Dynamics and information structure (as printed)**: kinematic single-integrator-with-speed-cap and turn-rate-limited agents (turn rate is an explicit parameter: pursuer 150 deg/s, evader 500 deg/s, Table 2, p. 4 — so unicycle-style heading dynamics, consistent with "update pursuer heading" in Algorithm 1); exact continuous dynamics equations are NOT printed. Pursuer sensing: radar 35 m, 360° FOV; inter-pursuer communication within 50 m broadcasts detected threats; a receiving pursuer decides to assist "based on distance and current station coverage" (p. 4) — decision rule not formalized. Battery: RETURNING state triggered below 20% battery; RECHARGING at 0.5 units/step at the station (p. 4). Execution is decentralized with local observations + communication (p. 8, Scalability Considerations).

**Termination / winning conditions (Fig. 3 annotation box, p. 5, printed)**: "Win Conditions — Evaders: Capture any station; Pursuers: Catch all evaders"; "Max episode: 1200 steps (2 min)" ⇒ a 10 Hz control loop (inference from printed 1200 steps / 2 min). Single-agent terminals (Table 1): capture, boundary escape, goal reached, timeout. Multi-agent terminal set (Table 3 + p. 6 metric definitions): all evaders captured (pursuer victory), any evader reaching a station (evader victory — "goal" = station, station capture radius 3.0 m), timeout scored as pursuer −5.0 / evader +8.0.

**Reward-based metrics definitions (p. 5, printed)**: Defense Rate $= N_{defended}/N_{episodes}$; Evader Capture Rate $= N_{captured}/N_{total\ evaders}$; Station Breach Rate = average evaders reaching stations per episode; Mean Capture Time over successful captures only; Battery Efficiency = average remaining battery at episode end; Coordination Score = frequency of successful threat handoffs / coordinated interceptions.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (p. 8, Failure Case Analysis + Future Work)**:
1. Single-agent: speed ratios exceeding 4:1 (evader:pursuer) degrade performance.
2. Multi-agent: coordinated flock attacks from multiple directions can overwhelm the two-pursuer station defense when evaders split and attack both stations simultaneously.
3. Future work (self-declared gaps): population-based self-play to avoid convergence to a single Nash equilibrium; sensor uncertainty (radar noise, false alarms, detection probability — currently idealized detection); heterogeneous teams; larger scales; no hardware validation (simulation only, though drone deployment is the stated motivation).

**Critical observations (this archive, with page/eq. traceability)**:
1. **Speed confound in the headline result**: the single-agent 94% vs. 24% comparison runs the hybrid pursuer at 10.0 m/s against the classical pursuer's 4.0 m/s (p. 3; Fig. 2 speed panel) — a 2.5× kinematic advantage for the hybrid side, plus the classical controller's deliberately stale evader-speed assumption (2.5 m/s vs. actual mean 3.23 m/s, up to 5.0). The "nearly four-fold improvement" therefore conflates control-law quality with platform superiority and assumption mismatch; no equal-speed comparison is reported. The same critique applies in part to the multi-agent runs where both sides have equal speeds (9 vs. 11 m/s, Table 2) — there the classical baseline is a non-learning controller facing RL-trained evaders, so part of the 26.2-pp gap is adversary asymmetry (evaders were trained against the current defenders; the classical defenders were never co-adapted).
2. **Projection intercept is not an intercept**: eq. (3) is the least-squares time along the relative-velocity direction, exact only when the line of sight is parallel to relative velocity; a miss distance remains otherwise and no correction/residual analysis is given (analytic gap logged at Sec. 5.1). The true constant-bearing construction is absent.
3. **The central knob is unspecified**: neither the ω schedule (initial/final/annealing law, eq. (5)) nor the curriculum transition criteria between Phases 1–3 are printed; Algorithm 1 uses ω but Algorithm 2 (training) never states whether ω is annealed within each alternation or across the whole run. Reproducing the reported numbers from the paper as written is impossible on this axis alone.
4. **Internal inconsistencies**: capture-time IQR "approximately 50–75 / 80–120" (p. 6) vs. "54–72 / 76–110" (p. 7); role vocabulary leader+support (p. 4, eq. (6) binary) vs. leader+follower+decoy (p. 5) vs. leader+flanker1+flanker2 (p. 6); "one-hot" misnomer for a scalar bit (eq. (6)); flanker rates 81/83% (text) rounded to 82% (Table 5, acceptable averaging but unstated).
5. **Battery asymmetry in Fig. 2** (100.0% vs. 97.9% final battery) is unexplained — single-agent pursuers apparently carry a battery model that is never described in the single-agent setup (battery appears only in the multi-agent state machine, p. 4).
6. **No variance/statistics discipline**: single-agent numbers come from one 100-episode run per controller with no seeds, confidence intervals, or training curves; multi-agent self-play is famously seed-sensitive, yet one training run (5M steps) is reported.
7. **Evader-faster regime underexplored**: although curriculum Phase 3 (1.1×) advertises evader-superior speed, evaluation never tests the hybrid agent at speed parity or disadvantage (evader ≤ 5.0 vs. pursuer 10 m/s); the paper's own failure threshold (>4:1) is thus never mapped. In the multi-agent game evaders ARE faster (11 vs. 9 m/s, Table 2) with 2.5× the turn rate (500 vs. 150 deg/s), but no parameter study on these ratios is given.
8. **No guarantee or certification**: despite the interpretability claim (action decomposable into classical + learned parts, p. 8), there is no analysis of worst-case behavior when $\hat u_{RL}$ dominates ($\omega \to 0$), and no formal connection (e.g., value-equivalence or invariance) between the blended policy and either component.

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: best cited as (a) an example of hybrid classical-guidance + PPO architectures for interception, i.e., "learning to modulate a geometric prior" rather than learning from scratch; (b) a template for alternating adversarial self-play in PE defense with role-encoded adversarial flocks (leader/decoy structures) against station-guarding pursuer teams; (c) a baseline/positioning reference for the claim that pure classical interception fails under speed mismatch and stale target-speed assumptions, in the range-limited-sensor perimeter-defense setting; and (d) a recent AFRL-affiliated data point that PE communities (perimeter defense, reach-avoid) are absorbing RL methodology — useful when arguing the need for the guarantees and feasibility certificates the differential-game literature provides (this paper has none, which is exactly the contrast a theory paper can draw). Its quantitative claims should be cited with the speed-confound caveat (Sec. 7.1), not as evidence of hybrid > classical per se.

**Suggested citation sentences**: "Akinmolayan et al. blend a constant-bearing intercept solver with a PPO policy via a annealed trust weight and curriculum learning, reporting 94% capture versus 24% for the classical interceptor in a sensor-triggered perimeter-defense scenario [P003]." Or, in a related-work paragraph on learning-augmented differential games: "Hybrid schemes that graft learned policies onto analytic guidance have recently been applied to range-limited perimeter defense, where an RL override learns to cut off escape arcs that fixed lead-pursuit geometry misses, and to multi-station defense trained by adversarial self-play [P003]."
