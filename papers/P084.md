## 2. Research Background

Multirobot systems (MRS) can pursue several evaders only if the pursuers are organized into cooperative subgroups, each shepherded onto one target; the assignment step is the multirobot task allocation (MRTA) problem, which is NP-hard [9], [10]. Classical solvers trade off optimality and speed: the Kuhn–Munkres (KM) algorithm gives the global optimum in O(n³) time [11], heuristics (GA [12], DPSO [13]) are fast but can stagnate in local minima, auction methods [14] risk single-point failure at the auctioneer, and learning methods (DQN [17]) need retraining per team-size configuration. An alternative, biologically inspired paradigm is the **k-winners-take-all (k-WTA) competitive network**, which selects the k largest responses among N inputs and, in the distributed form of Jin et al. [24] (Automatica 2019), lets pursuers compete for a single moving target. The paper positions itself in this competitive-allocation lineage [18]–[24] and argues that all prior k-WTA pursuit work is single-target only and lacks comprehensive experimental validation (p. 5990). The motion layer follows the predator–prey analogy: pursuers attracted to the target and repelled by peers/obstacles form an encirclement ring, echoing wolf-pack hunting studies [29] and encirclement-guaranteed MPC pursuit [30].

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem (Section II, pp. 5990–5991).** Given N_p pursuers P = {p_1,…,p_{N_p}} and N_e evaders E = {e_1,…,e_{N_e}} in a bounded environment S with unknown obstacles, task T = {P, E, S, k} where k is the number of pursuers needed to capture one evader. Evader positions u_{e_j} are broadcast to pursuers; pursuers have **no prior obstacle information**; inter-pursuer communication is limited to a (time-varying) neighbor set ℕ_i^c within communication range. Each evader flees when perceiving a pursuer and, after sustained chase, "becomes tired and its speed will be reduced" (fatigue, qualitative only). The task ends when all evaders are captured.

- **Assignment subproblem (eqs. (1)–(6), pp. 5990–5991)**: single-task robots, multirobot tasks, time-extended assignment (ST-MR-TA, taxonomy of Gerkey & Matarić [9]); formulated as a 0-1 quadratic integer program [26]: min Σᵢ Σⱼ ω_ij d_ij s.t. Σᵢ ω_ij ≥ k ∀j (eq. (2), at least k pursuers per evader), Σⱼ ω_ij = 1 ∀i (eq. (3), one target per pursuer), ω_ij ∈ {0,1} (eq. (4)), N_p ≥ k·N_e (eq. (5)), k ≥ 3 (eq. (6), needed for encirclement). Cost d_ij = Euclidean distance pursuer–evader; "cost of an algorithm" = sum of assigned distances.

**Gap — [authors' claim] (p. 5990):**
- [Authors' claim] Previous k-WTA-based coordination efforts (notably Jin et al. [24]) "were limited to single-target scenarios and did not include comprehensive experimental validation". [Verified from the paper's own description of [24]]: [24] is described on p. 5990 as having pursuers "engage in competition to be assigned the task, where only those with higher inputs are allowed to pursue the target, while the unsuccessful participants remain stationary" — i.e., a single-target distributed k-WTA; the multitarget extension claim is consistent with that description (the original [24] was not independently re-examined here).
- [Authors' claim] Multitarget pursuit is harder than single-target for three stated reasons: resources are dispersed, allocation must economize time/energy, and all pursuits must run simultaneously under inter-pursuer communication/coordination constraints (p. 5990).
- **Gap filled**: "For the first time, a multitarget k-WTA (DMK-WTA) algorithm based on competitive mechanisms is proposed by combining a distributed multitarget k-WTA network and a load reassignment unit", and "this is the first work to pursue and encircle multiple targets simultaneously in the framework of k-WTA" (contribution list, p. 5990). Note: no formal optimality or convergence claim is proved for the multitarget extension; "near/suboptimal" is supported empirically (Section IV-G).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Algorithm (modeling + distributed computation)**: DMK-WTA — a two-phase distributed task-allocation algorithm: (i) a *k-WTA phase* implemented as a three-variable (β, θ, z) per-pair dynamical network (eqs. (7)–(10)) that fuses the classical k-WTA dual-network structure [27] with the dynamic average-consensus estimator of Freeman, Yang & Lynch [25], so each pursuer estimates global winner counts from local neighborhoods; (ii) a *load-reassignment phase* (Algorithm 1, p. 5992, inspired by swarm clustering [28]) that resolves multi-load conflicts and exchanges targets to lower total cost, with a randomized transfer rule (probability ρ) to escape local optima (Remark 1, p. 5992).
2. **Control scheme (modeling)**: the Predator-Pack encirclement Model (PPM) — a Newton-second-law potential-field model (eqs. (11)–(19)) with target attraction, inter-pursuer repulsion modulated by a Gaussian of the distance-to-target (eq. (16)) that peaks exactly on the encirclement circle of radius d_a, obstacle repulsion, and a symmetric reactive evasion model for evaders; the k-nearest-pursuer encirclement condition (angle-based capture test, Section III-B end) closes the loop between allocation and capture.
3. **Complexity analysis (theory, lightweight)**: an explicit floating-operations count of the discretized k-WTA update — 6N_e + 15 flops per pursuer per iteration, i.e., O(N_e) per agent (p. 5991) — contrasting with O(n³) for KM.
4. **Empirical validation (simulation + experiment)**: (a) Webots/E-puck simulations (static targets, moving evaders, obstacle ring, sensor-noise robustness at 0/20/40%); (b) Gazebo/ROS simulations on the quadruped Aliengo with a legged-robot interface (eqs. (20)–(21), CHAMP controller) demonstrating mid-run subgroup re-assignment with a 2.97% cost reduction; (c) a benchmark of DMK-WTA vs random, GA, KM, DPSO, DQN over 24 (N_e, k) combinations with 20 repeats each, using cost rate vs the KM optimum; (d) physical Crazyflie 2.1 quadrotor experiments (PPM adaptability for k = 3,4,5; 2 evaders vs 6 pursuers; obstacle-avoidance pursuit) tracked by a 16-camera OptiTrack system.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

**There are no formal theorems, lemmas, propositions, or corollaries in this paper.** The mathematical content consists of: a constrained optimization formulation (1)–(6), a distributed dynamical-network allocator (7)–(10) with a resolution condition and a flops count, a heuristic reassignment algorithm (Algorithm 1), a potential-field dynamics model (11)–(19), and a robot-specific command interface (20)–(21). ### 5.0 Dynamics, information structure, cost, termination

- **Robot dynamics (eq. (11), p. 5992)**: Newton's second law at velocity level, v̇_i(t) = F_i(t)/m_i, for i = p_1,…,p_{N_p}, e_1,…,e_{N_e}; F_i resultant virtual force, m_i mass. Positions u_i; single integrator-then-force structure (acceleration-controlled; no nonholonomic constraint in the model — heading/steering appears only in the Aliengo interface (20)–(21)).
- **Information structure (p. 5990)**: evader positions u_{e_j} broadcast to all pursuers; pursuers know nothing about obstacles a priori (obstacles sensed within detection range O); each pursuer communicates only with neighbors ℕ_i^c (within communication range), topology may change during execution; pursuers detect nearby pursuers (set ℕ^d_{p_i}) and obstacles O. Evaders detect pursuers within ℕ^d_{e_j}. So: *local communication, global target-position broadcast, no obstacle map*.
- **Cost**: assignment cost Σᵢ Σⱼ ω_ij d_ij (eq. (1)); no differential-game payoff functional is defined anywhere — the "game" is not solved as a max–min problem; the evader follows a fixed reactive repulsion law (17)–(19) plus a fatigue rule (unmodeled speed drop after sustained chase, pp. 5990, 5993).
- **Termination / winning condition (Section III-B, p. 5993)**: an evader is *captured* when k pursuers are within its detection range **and** the angle subtended at the evader by two adjacent pursuers exceeds a threshold — "when k = 3, this condition is that the angle formed by two adjacent pursuers and the evader is greater than 100 degrees" (p. 5993). The overall task terminates when all evaders are captured (p. 5990). This angle test is asserted, not derived from reachability analysis.

### 5.1 ST-MR-TA formulation (eqs. (1)–(6), pp. 5990–5991)

min_{ω} Σ_{i=1}^{N_p} Σ_{j=1}^{N_e} ω_ij d_ij (1), subject to Σ_{i=1}^{N_p} ω_ij ≥ k ∀ 1 ≤ j ≤ N_e (2); Σ_{j=1}^{N_e} ω_ij = 1 ∀ 1 ≤ i ≤ N_p (3); ω_ij ∈ {0,1} (4); N_p ≥ k N_e (5); k ≥ 3 (6). Constraint (2)+(6) "guarantee a successful encirclement" (≥3 pursuers per evader); (3) forbids multi-target pursuit by one robot; (5) guarantees enough pursuers. Physical meaning: minimize total pursuit distance while endowing every evader with an encircling subgroup of at least k pursuers. Extensibility: any cost surrogate (time, energy) can replace d_ij; the paper uses Euclidean distance only.

### 5.2 DMK-WTA, phase 1: distributed k-WTA network (eqs. (7)–(10), p. 5991)

For every pursuer–evader pair (i, j), with inputs d_ij (Euclidean distance), local neighbor set ℕ_i^c, |ℕ_i^c| = N_{ℕ_i^c}:

- **State eq. (7)**: β̇_ij = −γ(β_ij − ω_ij) − (γ/N_{ℕ_i^c}) Σ_{k∈ℕ_i^c}(β_ij − β_kj) − (γ/N_{ℕ_i^c}) Σ_{k∈ℕ_i}(θ_ij − θ_kj).
- **State eq. (8)**: θ̇_ij = −Σ_{k∈ℕ_i^c}(β_ij − β_kj).
- **State eq. (9)**: ż_ij = −λ(N_p β_ij − k); **output eq. (9)**: ω_ij = f_Ω(z_ij + (−d_ij)/(2a)) = f_Ω(z_ij − d_ij/(2a)).
- **Nonlinearity eq. (10)**: f_Ω(x) = 1 if x ≥ 1; x if 0 < x < 1; 0 if x ≤ 0 (piecewise-linear saturation).

- **Interpretation (supported by text, pp. 5991, 5993)**: β_ij ∈ [0,1] is pursuer i's (soft) winner indicator for evader j; the first term of (7) drives β toward ω (winner status), the second is a proportional consensus-diffusion of β over neighbors, and (8) integrates the β-disagreement (integral/PI-type consensus action, θ), the θ-diffusion being the third term of (7). In (9), N_p β_ij acts as the consensus estimate of the *total* winner count for target j (since the average-consensus estimator [25] lets every agent recover the global sum from local exchanges), so ż_ij = −λ(N_p β_ij − k) is a dual-network constraint-violation signal that pushes the number of winners per target toward exactly k [27]; z_ij − d_ij/(2a) then thresholds the distance input so that the k *nearest* pursuers saturate ω = 1. "ω_ij = 1 indicates that pursuer p_i successfully wins in the allocation competition for evader e_j, resulting in a load l = j being generated" (p. 5991). β, θ, z are initialized to zero; the phase therefore starts with z increasing at rate λk (Fig. 1 narrative, p. 5993).
- **Resolution condition (p. 5991)**: a ≤ (1/2)(d_(k)j − d_(k+1)j) is the network resolution, where d_(k)j is the k-th smallest distance to evader e_j and is strictly greater than d_(k+1)j [27]. Physical meaning: the input shift −d_ij/(2a) can only separate winners from losers if the k-th and (k+1)-th distances differ by at least 2a. All assumptions as printed: γ > 0, λ > 0 "hyperparameters for accelerating convergence, which cannot be set too large" (no explicit stability bound given — **proof gap**: no convergence proof for the distributed network (7)–(9) is provided in this paper; stability is inherited by citation from [24], [25], [27]).
- **Typographical inconsistency (p. 5991, eq. (7))**: the third summation runs over k ∈ ℕ_i while its normalizer and the sum in (8) use ℕ_i^c; ℕ_i (without superscript) is never defined separately. Most plausibly a misprint for ℕ_i^c; the printed text is internally inconsistent.
- **Complexity count (p. 5991)**: Euler discretization β_ij := β_ij − γ(β_ij − ω_ij) − (γ/N_{ℕ_i^c})Σ_{k∈ℕ_i^c}(β_ij − β_kj) − (γ/N_{ℕ_i^c})Σ_{k∈ℕ_i}(θ_ij − θ_kj); θ_ij := θ_ij − Σ_{k∈ℕ_i^c}(β_ij − β_kj); z_ij := z_ij − λ(N_p β_ij − k). With a flop = one add/sub/mul/div of two floats: updating β_ij costs 4N_e + 7 flops, θ_ij costs 2N_e, z_ij costs 4, ω_ij costs 4; total per agent per iteration **6N_e + 15 flops = O(N_e) per agent** (decisive count, p. 5991). This is the paper's headline analytic selling point vs KM's O(n³).
- **Mechanism claim (p. 5991)**: "The k nearest pursuers are assigned to chase the target evader, which is beneficial for individuals and has a lower-overall cost" — asserted, not proved (no approximation-ratio statement).

### 5.3 DMK-WTA, phase 2: load reassignment (Algorithm 1, p. 5992; Remark 1)

Loads L_i = {j | ω_ij = 1} are target identities held by pursuer p_i. Algorithm 1 (inputs: cost matrix D_{N_p×N_e}, neighbor set ℕ_i^c, load set L_i; output: optimized L_i):

- **|L_i| > 1** (multi-load conflict, violates (3)): keep the load with minimum cost d_im (lines 2–5); for every remaining load j, choose transfer target t as (i) the first loadless neighbor (lines 8–11, len(L_k) = 0), else (ii) the neighbor with minimum evaluated cost d_tj (lines 12–15); **if the holder itself is the local minimum (d_ij < d_tj, line 17), t is chosen uniformly at random among neighbors** (line 18) — this randomization is the escape mechanism from local optima; then transfer load j to t (line 20).
- **|L_i| = 1**: pairwise swap with any neighbor p_k if swapping loads lowers the summed cost (lines 22–27).
- **|L_i| = 0**: take the nearest evader e_b and set L_i = {b} (line 29).
- **Remark 1 (p. 5992)**: "[t]he exchange is to decrease the total cost of distributed system while the probability ρ of sending load is to avoid local optimization to some extend. Thanks to the load reassignment, the algorithm is able to find a near optimal allocation scheme with less communication cost in a distributed manner." **Proof gap**: no proof that the post-reassignment allocation satisfies constraint (2) (≥ k pursuers per evader) is given — only (3) is enforced structurally; the paper asserts on p. 5991 that "the allocation results satisfy (2) and (3)". Unassigned pursuers defaulting to the nearest evader (line 29) is what heuristically restores (2).

### 5.4 PPM: predator-pack pursuit and encirclement (eqs. (11)–(19), pp. 5992–5993)

- **Pursuer resultant force (eq. (12))**: F_i(t) = F_i^T(t) + Σ_{k∈ℕ^d_{p_i}} F_{i,k}(t) + Σ_{o∈O} F_{i,o}(t) — target attraction + detected-pursuer repulsion + obstacle repulsion.
- **Component forces (eqs. (13)–(15))**: F_i^T = C_P^E g(u_{p_i} − u_{e_T}) (13); F_{i,k} = C_P^P g_a(u_{p_i} − u_{p_k}) φ_ik (14); F_{i,o} = C_P^O g_a(u_{p_i} − u_o) φ_io (15).
- **Encirclement modulation (eq. (16))**: φ_ik = exp{−c_P[(R_i − d_a)² + (R_k − d_a)²]}, where R_i = ||u_{p_i} − u_{e_T}||, d_a is the enclosing-circle radius hyperparameter, c_P a decay constant. Physical meaning: inter-pursuer repulsion is suppressed when far from the target and peaks when *both* pursuers sit on the d_a-circle, so the subgroup "disperse[s] evenly around the evader, forming a circle of encirclement" (p. 5993) — the Gaussian is the device that turns pairwise repulsion into uniform angular spacing on the ring.
- **Potential shapes (p. 5992)**: g(x) = g_a(x) − g_r(x) with g_a(x) = −x/||x||² and g_r(x) = −x d_c²/||x||⁴, d_c = safe distance among robots. With g as printed, the target force (13) is attractive at range (−x/||x||² term) and repulsive at contact range (+x d_c²/||x||⁴ term, crossover near ||x|| = d_c) — a soft non-collision capture term. **As-printed sign inconsistency (proof gap, p. 5992)**: the same g_a(x) = −x/||x||² is used in the "repulsive" forces (14), (15), (18), (19); evaluated at x = u_{p_i} − u_{p_k} etc., it points *toward* the peer/obstacle/pursuer, i.e., attractive, contradicting the stated repulsive semantics ("repelled by other pursuers and obstacles", p. 5992) and the observed behavior in Figs. 2–15. Evidently the intended repulsion carries the opposite sign (or uses g_r with outward direction); the printed formulas cannot reproduce the reported dynamics as written.
- **Evader model (eqs. (17)–(19), p. 5993)**: F_{e_j}(t) = Σ_{i∈ℕ^d_{e_j}} F_{e_j,p_i}(t) + Σ_{o∈O} F_{e_j,o}(t) (17); F_{e_j,p_i} = C_E^P g_a(u_{e_j} − u_{p_i}) (18); F_{e_j,o} = C_E^O g_a(u_{e_j} − u_o) (19). Evaders wander (no pursuer within detection range) and flee once a pursuer is detected; fatigue reduces their maximum speed after sustained chase (qualitative, no equation/parameter printed). Evader–evader repulsion is mentioned in prose (p. 5992) but has no numbered force term.
- **Capture/encirclement condition (p. 5993)**: k pursuers within evader detection range and all adjacent-pursuer subtended angles > threshold (100° for k = 3; k = 4 used in the E-puck simulations "according to the encirclement condition [29], [30]", p. 5993).

### 5.5 Legged-robot interface (eqs. (20)–(21), p. 5995)

From PPM body-frame velocities v′_ix, v′_iy of pursuer p_i: ẋ′ = v_ix·dt (20, forward displacement) and α̇ = arctan(v′_iy·dt / v′_ix·dt) (21, steering angle), fed to the CHAMP quadruped controller for Aliengo. (20) as printed uses v_ix while (21) uses v′_ix/v′_iy — minor notational inconsistency.

### 5.6 What is and is not guaranteed

- Guaranteed by construction (heuristically): (3) exactly one target per pursuer after phase 2; O(N_e) per-agent computation; fully distributed information flow (consensus over ℕ_i^c).
- Not proved: convergence of (7)–(9) under switching topology (inherited from [24], [25] by citation only, and only for their settings); satisfaction of (2) after reassignment; any bound of DMK-WTA cost vs the KM optimum (empirical cost-rate curves only, Fig. 9(b)); capture success or capture-time bounds — the angle-based capture condition is checked online, and encirclement formation is argued via the force balance of (12)–(16), not via an invariance/ Lyapunov argument.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (Conclusion, p. 5999)**: future work on (i) heterogeneity, e.g., cooperative pursuit of UAVs and UGVs; (ii) extension to three-dimensional space ("quadrotor is very flexible, this scheme can be fully extended to 3D"); (iii) more constraints such as energy.

**Critical observations (with page/evidence)**:
1. **No game-theoretic content**: despite the title, no strategy pair, value function, Nash/saddle-point analysis, or capture-time guarantee is derived; the evader is a fixed reactive APF law (17)–(19) with an unquantified fatigue speed drop (pp. 5990, 5993). The work is an allocation + control architecture, not a solved differential game.
2. **No convergence/optimality proof for the multitarget extension**: stability of (7)–(9) is inherited from [24], [25], [27] by citation; the resolution condition a ≤ ½(d_(k)j − d_(k+1)j) (p. 5991) presumes strictly separated costs (ties break the guarantee); "near optimal" of Algorithm 1 has no approximation bound (Remark 1, p. 5992). γ, λ "cannot be set too large" (p. 5991) — no admissible range given. **Proof gap.**
3. **Constraint (2) not enforced**: the paper claims the final allocation satisfies (2) and (3) (p. 5991), but only (3) is structurally guaranteed (multi-load transfer + one-load rule); restoration of ≥ k pursuers per evader relies on the heuristic default "losers head to the nearest evader" (line 29, p. 5992) and is never verified against (2) in the benchmark.
4. **Sign/notation inconsistencies as printed**: (a) g_a(x) = −x/||x||² used for both attraction (13) and repulsion (14), (15), (18), (19) — as printed the "repulsive" terms are attractive (p. 5992); (b) third sum of (7) over undefined ℕ_i vs ℕ_i^c elsewhere (p. 5991); (c) a = 0.0005 in the benchmark text (p. 5997) vs a = 0.005 in Tables II/IV/VI; (d) "Initial positions of evaders" printed for the pursuer list u_1…u_6 (p. 5995); (e) v_ix vs v′_ix between (20) and (21) (p. 5995).
5. **Global target-position broadcast** (p. 5990): although communication is local, every pursuer needs d_ij to *all* N_e evaders (6N_e + 15 flops/agent assumes the full cost row), so sensing/who-broadcasts-whom is left implicit; scalability in N_e is per-agent linear in memory and traffic.
6. **Benchmark scope**: comparison covers the *assignment subproblem only* (static cost matrices), not the closed-loop pursuit; DQN excluded beyond 10 tasks (p. 5996–5997); results presented as curves without numeric tables or variance statistics; DMK-WTA itself is stochastic (randomized transfers, probability ρ) yet no Monte-Carlo dispersion is reported for it in Fig. 9 despite 20 repeats.
7. **Physical-experiment caveats**: evader positions come from an external 16-camera OptiTrack system (0.1 mm accuracy, p. 5997) broadcast over LAN — onboard perception limits, localization latency, and communication dropouts are not exercised; evaders are identical Crazyflies running the same reactive law, i.e., speed-symmetric players with fatigue not demonstrated in hardware.
8. **Capture condition is conventional**: the angle threshold (100° for k = 3, p. 5993) and k ≥ 3 (eq. (6)) are design choices justified by ethology [29] and prior encirclement work [30], not derived from encirclement-reachability analysis.

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: a systems-and-algorithms paper in the cooperative-pursuit literature — the reference point for *distributed competitive task allocation* (k-WTA networks) in *multitarget* pursuit, and for potential-field *encirclement* control validated across three locomotion modalities (wheeled E-puck, legged Aliengo, aerial Crazyflie 2.1). It is the natural citation when a paper needs (i) a distributed, communication-light alternative to KM/GA/auction/DQN assignment for pursuit subteam formation with empirical cost-rate evidence against those baselines; (ii) the Gaussian-modulated inter-pursuer repulsion trick (eq. (16)) that yields uniform ring formations at radius d_a; or (iii) a representative of the "competition instead of consensus" allocation philosophy contrasted with consensus-based MRTA. It should **not** be cited for game-theoretic guarantees (no value/barrier/equilibrium analysis, no capture-time bounds); pair it with differential-game sources when the caller's work proves what this paper assumes. Suggested citation sentences: "Distributed multitarget assignment can be obtained by a competitive k-WTA network coupled with a consensus estimator and load reassignment, achieving near-KM allocation cost at O(N_e) per-agent computation and sub-second decisions, and was validated on wheeled, legged, and aerial robot teams [P084]." Alternatively: "Potential-field encirclement with target-distance-modulated inter-pursuer repulsion spreads k pursuers uniformly on a ring around each evader and supports hardware demonstration with quadrotors [P084]."
