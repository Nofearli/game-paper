## 2. Research Background
Perimeter defense is a variant of the target-guarding pursuit-evasion problem [6] in which the defender is constrained to move along the perimeter of the protected region while the intruder tries to reach it; prior work by the same group covers multiplayer perimeter defense [7], [9] and arbitrary convex perimeters [8], all on planar game spaces. The authors observe that real protected targets (nuclear power plants [10], penstocks [11], forests [12], disaster sites [13]) are three-dimensional, and that aerial vehicles naturally operate in 3D, so the planar assumption should be relaxed. Related 3D reach-avoid work by Yan et al. [14]–[16] (plane target with two or three defenders, heterogeneous multiplayer) assumes free 3D motion of all players, which is unreasonable when the guard must stay near the target (it cannot fly through it). This paper takes an intermediate step toward full air-vs-air perimeter defense: one defender constrained to move on the surface of a hemisphere around the target, against one intruder constrained to the ground plane (the hemisphere's base plane). The claimed novelty is explicit (p. 1): "To the best of authors' knowledge, this paper is the first to solve the pursuit-evasion game with the defender constrained to move on a hemisphere."

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
**Problem** (Sec. II, p. 2): a hemisphere of unit radius sits on the ground plane R. Intruder A moves on R (first-order simple motion, max speed $\nu \le 1$); defender D moves on the hemisphere surface (max speed 1, WLOG). Intruder wins by reaching the base perimeter with nonzero angular separation from the defender; defender wins by azimuthally aligning with the intruder's breach direction before that. The task is to (i) find the barrier (Game of Kind) separating defender-winning from intruder-winning initial configurations, and (ii) exhibit optimal strategies for both players (Game of Degree), with optimality in the Nash sense of (6).

**Gap as claimed by the authors** (p. 1): (i) all prior perimeter-defense formulations [7]–[9] "deal with engagements on a planar game space" and constrain the defender to a (perimeter) circle; (ii) the 3D works of Yan et al. [14]–[16] "are reasonable for open space" but do not handle a defender whose motion is constrained around the target ("it cannot directly pass through the target"); therefore a 3D game with a hemisphere-constrained defender is unsolved.

**Verified from cited text**: [7] (Shishika & Kumar, CDC 2018) is indeed the 2D multiplayer perimeter-defense local-game decomposition in which the defender moves on a circle — the present paper's eq. (28) explicitly recovers [7]'s 2D result as the degenerate case $\phi_D = 0$; [8] (arXiv:1909.03989) is the arbitrary convex-shape perimeter game (still planar); [9] (RA-L 2020) is the multiplayer team-strategy version; [14]–[16] (Yan, Shi, Zhong, Bullo) are 3D guarding/reach-avoid games with plane targets and unconstrained 3D player motion. The gap statement is accurate as far as these specific citations go: no cited work treats a defender confined to a spherical surface against a ground intruder. However, the technical machinery is a direct lift of the 2D equal-time (Apollonius-type) barrier idea of [7] to spherical/planar target times — the contribution is the geometry of that lift (geodesic defender time, optimal approach angle on the ground plane), not a new solution concept; and no comparison against the unconstrained-3D-defense alternative (e.g., letting the defender leave the hemisphere) is attempted, so the practical benefit of the constrained formulation is asserted, not demonstrated.

## 4. Core Contributions
1. **Modeling (theory)**: a one-defender-one-intruder perimeter-defense game in 3D — defender on a unit hemisphere, intruder on the base plane — with a 3-state relative model $z = [\psi, \phi, r]$ (azimuth difference, defender elevation, intruder radius) and first-order dynamics (eq. (3), p. 2); Game-of-Kind termination conditions (p. 2).
2. **Payoff and optimal breaching point (theory)**: a Game-of-Degree payoff equal to the difference of target times $p = \tau_D - \tau_A$ (eq. (4)), parameterized by the breaching angle $\theta$ (eq. (12)); a first-order optimality condition yielding the optimal approach angle $\beta^*$ (Theorem 1, eq. (13)) and, combined with a purely geometric relation (eqs. (18)–(25)), a unique optimal breaching point (Definition 1, Remark 3, pp. 3–4).
3. **Barrier / winning regions (theory)**: the barrier for the Game of Kind is the simple closed curve $C(z_D) = \{z_A \mid p(z_D, z_A, z_B) = 0\}$ in the intruder's plane (Lemmas 2–3), with intruder-winning region inside and defender-winning region outside (Lemma 4, Fig. 3, pp. 4–5).
4. **Optimality proof (theory)**: Nash-inequality proofs (eq. (6)) that both players' optimal strategies are to move toward the optimal breaching point at maximum speed at all times (Lemmas 5–12, Remarks 5–6, pp. 5–6), including the degenerate flat-defender case $\phi_D = 0$ recovering the 2D result of [7] (Lemma 7, eq. (28)).
5. **Simulation (experiment)**: three setups (both optimal / only defender optimal / only intruder optimal) at one initial condition, showing payoff conservation under joint optimality and monotone payoff degradation under unilateral deviation, i.e., the Nash sandwich of eq. (6) (Fig. 6, pp. 6–7). No hardware experiments.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (MOST IMPORTANT — exhaustive)
The paper contains one formal Theorem, twelve Lemmas, six Remarks, one Definition, and 30 numbered equations. Everything is stated for the relative coordinates; all results are analytic/geometric (no numerical construction except for solving two scalar equations for $\theta^*$).

### 5.1 Geometry, dynamics, information structure, termination (p. 2, eqs. (1)–(3))
- **3D geometry**: unit hemisphere on ground plane R; perimeter = equator (base circle). Positions in spherical coordinates: $\mathbf{z}_D = [\psi_D, \phi_D, 1]$ (defender on the sphere, unit radius), $\mathbf{z}_A = [\psi_A, 0, r]$ (intruder on the plane, elevation 0, radius $r \ge 1$). Relative state $\mathbf{z} \triangleq [\psi, \phi, r]$ with $\psi \triangleq \psi_A - \psi_D$ and $\phi \triangleq \phi_D$; by azimuthal symmetry one normalizes $\mathbf{z}_D = [0, \phi_D, 1]$, $\mathbf{z}_A = [\psi, 0, r]$ (p. 3). Note the intruder state has only 2 DOF $(\psi_A, r)$; the full relative state is 3D $(\psi, \phi, r)$.
- **Dynamics**: both agents first-order (speed-bounded point mass). Defender velocity parameterized by the altitudinal component $\omega_D \triangleq \dot\phi_D \in [-1, 1]$; with unit speed (eq. (1)): $\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_D\| = \sqrt{\dot\phi_D^2 + \dot\psi_D^2\cos^2\phi_D}$, hence (eq. (2)) $\dot\psi_D = \sqrt{1-\omega_D^2}/\cos\phi_D$ (verified visually on the rendered page: $\cos\phi_D$ is the denominator, outside the root; note the coordinate singularity $\dot\psi_D \to \infty$ as $\phi_D \to \pi/2$). Intruder velocity parameterized by heading $\gamma_A \in [0, \pi/2]$ and speed $\bar v_A$ (taken $= \nu$, p. 3). State dynamics (eq. (3)):
$$\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot\psi \\ \dot\phi \\ \dot r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\bar v_A \sin\gamma_A}{r} - \dfrac{\sqrt{1-\omega_D^2}}{\cos\phi_D} \\ \omega_D \\ -\bar v_A\cos\gamma_A \end{bmatrix} = f(\mathbf{z}, \omega_D, \gamma_A).$$
  Interpretation: $\dot r = -\bar v_A\cos\gamma_A \le 0$ — the intruder always closes range; $r\dot\psi_A = \bar v_A\sin\gamma_A \ge 0$ — with $\gamma_A \in [0,\pi/2]$ the intruder's azimuth only increases (the mirrored case is WLOG by symmetry, used e.g. in Lemma 1's proof). This heading restriction excludes outward ("run-away") intruder motion from the strategy space; the paper does not discuss whether lifting it changes the value.
- **Information structure**: "complete state information, i.e., all states (positions) are known to all agents, but not the control inputs (velocities)" (p. 2).
- **Termination / winning conditions (as printed, p. 2)**: game ends at terminal time $t_f$ with **intruder's win** iff $r(t_f) = 1$ and $|\psi(t_f)| + |\phi_D(t_f)| > 0$ (breach with nonzero angular separation); **defender's win** iff $\phi_D(t_f) = \psi(t_f) = 0$ and $r(t_f) > 0$ (azimuthal alignment; note $r(t_f) > 0$ is vacuous since $r \ge 1$, and the boundary case of simultaneous arrival at the same perimeter point therefore falls to the defender). Supporting claim, cited to [7]: if the states reach the manifold $\{\mathbf{z} \mid \psi = \phi_D = 0\}$ the defender can stabilize them there "due to its speed advantage" ($\nu \le 1$), so the intruder cannot then reach the hemisphere without capture — this is a 2D argument reused in 3D without reproof. The whole problem is a **Game of Kind**; the barrier is the surface separating the two outcomes, and the paper's payoff also gives the Game of Degree.

### 5.2 Payoff and Nash condition (p. 2, eqs. (4)–(6))
- Given initial $\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A$ and a breaching point $B$ (intruder's landing point on the perimeter), define breaching angle $\theta \triangleq \psi_B - \psi_D$ and distance $x = \|\mathbf{z}_A - \mathbf{z}_B\|$; target times $\tau_D(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_B)$ (defender) and $\tau_A(\mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B)$ (intruder). **Payoff** (eq. (4)): $p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B) = \tau_D(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_B) - \tau_A(\mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B)$; positive ⇒ intruder arrives first. With control histories $\Omega$ (of $\omega_D$) and $\Gamma$ (of $\gamma_A$), $p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \Omega, \Gamma) = \tau_D(\mathbf{z}_D, \Omega) - \tau_A(\mathbf{z}_A, \Gamma)$ (eq. (5)).
- **Nash condition** (eq. (6)): with $\Omega^*, \Gamma^*$ minimizing $\tau_D$ and maximizing $\tau_A$ respectively,
$$p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \Omega^*, \Gamma) \le p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \Omega^*, \Gamma^*) \le p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \Omega, \Gamma^*), \qquad p^*(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A) = p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \Omega^*, \Gamma^*).$$
  This sandwich is the paper's optimality target; Sections III–IV prove that "move toward the optimal breaching point" achieves both inequalities.

### 5.3 Candidate strategy and target times (p. 3, eqs. (7)–(12))
- **Defender target time** = geodesic time on the unit sphere from $(\psi_D, \phi_D)$ to breaching point at azimuth $\psi_B$: (eq. (7)) $\tau_D(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_B) = \cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$ — the spherical-law-of-cosines geodesic distance (analytic, exact, global).
- **Intruder target time** = straight-line time on the plane: law of cosines in triangle $ABO$ ($O$ = hemisphere center; $|OB| = 1$, $|OA| = r$, angle at $O$ = $\theta - \psi$) (eq. (8)): $x^2 = r^2 + 1^2 - 2r\cos(\theta - \psi)$, so (eq. (9)) $\tau_A(\mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B) = \frac{1}{\nu}\sqrt{r^2 + 1 - 2r\cos(\theta - \psi)}$.
- **Payoff** (eqs. (10)–(11)): $p = \cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta) - \frac{1}{\nu}\sqrt{r^2 + 1 - 2r\cos(\theta - \psi)}$; by the normalization only $\theta$ is unknown, so $p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B) \triangleq p^\theta(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \theta) \triangleq \tau_D^\theta(\mathbf{z}_D) - \tau_A^\theta(\mathbf{z}_A)$ (eq. (12)) — the game of degree is reduced to a **one-dimensional search over $\theta$**.

### 5.4 Lemma 1 and Remark 1 (p. 3): θ ↦ β is one-to-one
- **Statement**: given $\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A$, the map from breaching angle $\theta$ to the **approach angle** $\beta$ (angle between $\mathbf{z}_A - \mathbf{z}_B$ and the tangent line at $B$, see Fig. 2, p. 2) is one-to-one.
- **Proof**: geometric, by symmetry restricted to $z_B = [\theta, 0, 1]$ with $\theta$ ranging from $\psi$ to $\theta_t$, the tangency azimuth of line $AB$; as $\theta$ increases by $d\theta$, $\angle AB'B < \beta$ so $\beta' < \beta$ (monotone decrease). No analytic monotonicity computation. **Remark 1**: $\beta$ is unique given $\theta$, guaranteeing a unique breaching point associated with $(\theta^*, \beta^*)$. The one-to-one claim is what licenses solving two scalar equations for the two unknowns below; its proof is pictorial (a proof gap in rigor, though the underlying fact is elementary).

### 5.5 Theorem 1 (p. 3, eq. (13)) — optimal approach angle (central result)
- **Statement**: for given $\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A$, the payoff (4) is maximized if the intruder selects the breaching point $\mathbf{z}_B$ whose approach angle satisfies
$$\beta^* = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\nu\cos\phi_D\sin\theta^*}{\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\phi_D\cos^2\theta^*}}\right). \tag{13}$$
- **Assumptions**: $p$ has an optimal value ("Assuming that p has an optimal value", proof, p. 3); $\theta^*$ interior (the endpoint cases are not treated here); $\nu \le 1$.
- **Proof approach**: first-order optimality. Differentiate $p^\theta$ and set to zero (eq. (14)): $dp^\theta(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \theta^*) = d\tau_D^\theta(\mathbf{z}_D) - d\tau_A^\theta(\mathbf{z}_A) = 0$. Then (eq. (15)) $d\tau_D^\theta = \cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos(\theta + d\theta)) - \cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$, whose $d\theta \to 0$ limit is $\frac{\cos\phi_D\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^2\phi_D\cos^2\theta}}d\theta$; and (eq. (16)) $d\tau_A^\theta = \frac{|AB'|}{\nu} - \frac{|AB|}{\nu} = \frac{|BB'|\cos\beta}{\nu} = \frac{d\theta\cos\beta}{\nu}$ (moving the breaching point by arc element $d\theta = |BB'|$ along the unit circle changes the intruder's straight-line distance by $d\theta\cos\beta$). Equating gives $\cos\beta = \frac{\nu\cos\phi_D\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^2\phi_D\cos^2\theta}}$, i.e., eq. (13). **Decisive equation**: eq. (14) with (15)–(16).
- **Proof gap (typo in eq. (17), p. 3)**: the printed intermediate equality reads $\beta^* = \cos^{-1}\big(\nu\cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos(\theta+d\theta))/d\theta\big)$ — it **omits the subtraction of $\cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$** (the second term of eq. (15)) and the $d\theta \to 0$ limit; only the second equality of (17) (the final formula (13)) is correct as printed. The intended chain is $\cos\beta^* = \nu \lim_{d\theta\to0} d\tau_D^\theta/d\theta = \nu\frac{\cos\phi_D\sin\theta^*}{\sqrt{1-\cos^2\phi_D\cos^2\theta^*}}$.
- **Local/global**: first-order (necessary) condition, so locally optimal; global optimality is never separately established analytically (it follows indirectly if $p^\theta$ is unimodal, which is not proven — cf. Remark 3's uniqueness claim, itself resting on Lemma 1's pictorial argument). I independently verified global optimality numerically for the paper's own simulation scenario: solving (13) + (25) gives $\theta^* = 1.1337$, which coincides with the numerical argmax of $p^\theta$ over the whole admissible interval (max $p = 0.00313$), so at least in the tested regime the stationarity solution is the global maximizer.
- **Physical meaning**: the intruder should aim at the breaching point such that its incoming direction makes angle $\beta^*$ with the tangent — the classical "equal marginal time" condition: shifting the breach point by $d\theta$ must cost the defender and the intruder the same marginal time. In the flat-defender limit $\phi_D = 0$ this reduces to $\beta = \cos^{-1}\nu$ (eq. (28)), the Apollonius approach angle of the 2D game [7]. **Extensibility**: the marginal-time template $d\tau_D = d\tau_A$ would carry over to any convex 3D solid of revolution with computable geodesic times.

### 5.6 Second equation from geometry, and Definition 1 (pp. 3–4, eqs. (18)–(25), Remarks 2–3)
- From triangle $ABO$ with the angle at $B$ equal to $\pi/2 + \beta$ (tangent ⊥ radius): (eq. (18)) $r^2 = x^2 + 1^2 - 2x\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + \beta)$; quadratic formula with $k = \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\beta) = -\sin\beta$ (eq. (19)) $x = k \pm \sqrt{k^2 + r^2 - 1}$; taking $x > 0$ for $r > 1$ (eq. (20)) $x = -\sin\beta + \sqrt{r^2 - \cos^2\beta}$; squaring (eq. (21)) $x^2 = r^2 + \sin^2\beta - \cos^2\beta - 2\sin\beta\sqrt{r^2 - \cos^2\beta}$.
- **Remark 2** (eq. (22), p. 3): auxiliary identity "For $a \ge 0$": $a\sin x + b\cos x = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}\,\sin(x + \phi)$ with $\phi = \sin^{-1}(b/\sqrt{a^2+b^2})$ (standard; correct).
- Comparing (8) and (21) via Remark 2 yields (eq. (23)) $r\cos(\theta - \psi) = \sin\beta\sqrt{r^2 - \cos^2\beta} + \cos^2\beta = r\sin\big(\beta + \sin^{-1}(\tfrac{\cos\beta}{r})\big)$, simplified to (eq. (24)) $\cos\beta = r\cos(\psi - \theta - \beta)$, and solving for $\theta$ (eq. (25)) $\theta = \psi - \beta + \cos^{-1}(\tfrac{\cos\beta}{r})$. I verified consistency: substituting (25) into (24) gives $\cos\beta = r\cos(\cos^{-1}(\cos\beta/r)) = \cos\beta$. ✓
- **Definition 1** (p. 4): the **optimal breaching angle** $\theta^*$ is the value satisfying both (13) and (25); the corresponding $\beta^*$ is the **optimal approach angle**, and the breaching point realizing them is the **optimal breaching point**. **Remark 3**: given $\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A$, a unique pair $(\beta^*, \theta^*)$ exists (via Lemma 1's one-to-one map; uniqueness is as strong as Lemma 1's informal proof). The candidate strategy for both players: move toward the optimal breaching point, giving payoff $p^{\theta^*}(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \theta^*)$.

### 5.7 Barrier and winning regions (pp. 4–5, eqs. (26)–(27), Lemmas 2–4, Fig. 3)
- **Lemma 2** (p. 4): given $\mathbf{z}_D$ and a breaching angle $\theta$, there is a **unique** intruder position $\mathbf{z}_A$ with $p^\theta(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \theta) = 0$. Proof: (13) gives $\beta$; (7) gives $\tau_D$; on the barrier $\tau_A = \tau_D$ and $x = \tau_A\nu$, so $\mathbf{z}_A$ is determined by $(\theta, \beta, x)$; uniqueness via Remark 3.
- **Lemma 3** (p. 4): $C(\mathbf{z}_D) = \{\mathbf{z}_A \mid p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B) = 0\}$ is a **simple closed curve** (the barrier candidate). Proof: continuously vary $\theta \in [0, 2\pi]$, each value contributing one point (Lemma 2); non-self-crossing follows if $d\psi/d\theta > 0$, so $\mathbf{z}_A$ winds once around the origin. Projection relation from Fig. 2 (eq. (26)): $r\cos(\theta - \psi) = 1 + x\sin\beta$ (verified by vector projection); differentiating (eq. (27)): $\frac{d\psi}{d\theta} = 1 + \frac{\frac{d}{d\theta}(x\sin\beta)}{r\sin(\theta - \psi)}$.
- **Proof gap (Lemma 3)**: positivity of $\frac{d}{d\theta}(x\sin\beta)$ is **asserted, not computed** — the paper says only "we confirm that $\frac{d}{d\theta}(x\sin\beta)$ is a function of $\theta$ and positive" after noting $r > 0$, $\sin(\theta - \psi) > 0$, $x = \nu\cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$ (the on-barrier relation $x = \nu\tau_D$) and $\beta(\theta)$ from (13). No closed-form or sign analysis of the derivative is given.
- **Lemma 4 (Winning region)** (p. 4, with Fig. 3, p. 4): define the intruder-winning region $\mathcal{R}_A(\mathbf{z}_D) \triangleq \{\mathbf{z}_A \mid p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B) > 0\}$ (interior of $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$) and the defender-winning region $\mathcal{R}_D(\mathbf{z}_D) \triangleq \mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_A(\mathbf{z}_D)$ (exterior, barrier included). **Claim**: the intruder wins iff $\mathbf{z}_A(t_0) \in \mathcal{R}_A(\mathbf{z}_D)$ and loses if $\mathbf{z}_A(t_0) \in \mathcal{R}_D(\mathbf{z}_D)$.
- **Proof (intruder side)**: geometric; decisive inequality $|AB| < |BC|$ where $C$ is the crossing of segment $BA$ with the barrier: the intruder dashes straight to $B$; the defender's minimal time to $B$ equals the intruder's time from the barrier crossing $C$ to $B$ (since $p = 0$ on $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$), and the intruder reaches $C$ strictly earlier on the same line, so $\tau_D > \tau_A$ "no matter what the defender does". Note this presumes the equal-time property at $C$ holds **with the intruder's own breaching point $B$** — the normality fact needed for that is only established later as Lemma 9, and no formal argument rules out the defender improving by moving elsewhere first; the statement is persuasive but not airtight. **Proof (defender side)**: much looser — the intruder loses "in either of the following way: (i) the intruder is outside of $\mathcal{R}_A(\mathbf{z}_D)$ indefinitely; or (ii) it approaches the perimeter while defender can maintain $p < 0$ with its optimal strategy (see Sec. IV.B)" — i.e., the losing-side guarantee is delegated to the optimality proofs of Sec. IV-B rather than proven as invariance of $\mathcal{R}_D$.
- **Barrier shape (Fig. 3, p. 4)**: five top-down instances (perimeter = green circle, defender = blue dot, barrier = red curve, black normal lines visualizing admissible approach angles) for $\phi_D = 0.1\pi, 0.3\pi, 0.5\pi$ and for $\nu = 1, 0.3$; cross-parameter values of the other parameter in each panel are UNREPORTED. Trends stated (p. 5): the barrier "gets closer to a circle" as $\phi_D \to 0.5\pi$ (defender near the apex covers azimuth cheaply per eq. (2)), and shrinks as $\nu$ decreases (a slow intruder must hug the perimeter). **Dimension bookkeeping**: for each fixed $\mathbf{z}_D$ the barrier is a curve in the intruder's 2D plane; the full Game-of-Kind barrier in the 3D relative state space is only implicitly the union over $\phi_D$ — the abstract's "divides the state space" is thus a per-slice statement, made rigorous here only slice-wise.

### 5.8 Optimality proofs (pp. 5–6, eqs. (28)–(30), Lemmas 5–12, Remarks 4–6)
The target is the Nash sandwich (6): first the left inequality (defender cannot do worse), then the right (intruder cannot do better).
- **Lemma 5 (Conservation of payoff)** (p. 5): if both players move toward the optimal breaching point at max speed, $p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B)$ stays constant. **Proof gap**: the entire proof is "Optimal breaching point is defined as targeted position … to guarantee non-changing payoff, as stated in (14)" — i.e., it restates the first-order condition (14) rather than proving invariance of $p$ along the joint optimal ray (a genuinely circular argument; the invariance should follow from differentiating $p^\theta$ along the flow and using (13)+(25), which is never written out).
- **Lemma 6 (Conservation of optimal breaching point)** (p. 5): under the same joint play the optimal breaching point stays the same — by Lemma 5 plus Remark 3's uniqueness. Sound given Lemma 5.
- **Lemma 7 (Degeneracy, $\phi_D = 0$)** (p. 5, eq. (28)): for $\theta \in (0, \pi)$, (13) reduces to $\beta = \cos^{-1}\nu$, "which agrees with the results obtained in the two-dimensional version … [7]" — the consistency check anchoring the 3D construction to the 2D perimeter-defense literature. Endpoint $\theta = \pi$: $\beta$ undefined by (13); the paper explicitly leaves the optimal strategy unstated there, arguing the defender's vertical motion immediately makes $\phi_D > 0$ (hence resolves the case).
- **Lemma 8 (Smoothness)** (p. 5, eqs. (29)–(30)): for $\phi_D > 0$, $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$ is smooth. Tool: polar curvature $\kappa(\theta) = \frac{|r^2 + 2r'^2 - rr''|}{(r^2 + r'^2)^{3/2}}$ (eq. (29), standard formula) with on-barrier radius $r = \sqrt{x^2 + 1 + 2x\sin\beta}$ (eq. (30)), $x = \nu\cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$, $\beta(\theta)$ from (13). Conclusion "we observe that the curvature is well defined except for when $\cos\phi_D = 1$" — **no explicit curvature expression is computed**; the claim is asserted (proof gap in rigor).
- **Remark 4** (p. 5): a smooth curve admits a tangent circle at any point, and smaller circles tangent at the same point exist — the standard osculating-circle fact used in the next proof.
- **Lemma 9 (Normality)** (p. 5): for any $\mathbf{z}_A$ with corresponding breaching point $\mathbf{z}_B$, the line $\mathbf{z}_A - \mathbf{z}_B$ is **normal** to $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$. Proof: informal — otherwise a nearby barrier point would give a shorter path, contradicting the equal-time construction. No computation. (This lemma retroactively patches the crossing argument of Lemma 4.)
- **Lemma 10 (Limiting case, barrier initial condition)** (pp. 5–6): given $\mathbf{z}_D$ with $\phi_D > 0$ and $\mathbf{z}_A$ on the barrier ($p = 0$), if the defender moves toward the optimal breaching point, then $p$ is **non-increasing for any intruder strategy**. Proof (geometric, Fig. 4, p. 5): in an infinitesimal step $dt$ the defender reaches $D'$; the intruder's optimal response $A'$ keeps breaching point $B$ and $p(\mathbf{z}_{D'}, \mathbf{z}_{A'}, \mathbf{z}_B) = 0$ (Lemmas 5–6); $\phi_D > 0$ persists until the defender reaches $B$. The circle of radius $\bar v_A dt$ centered at $A$ passes through $A'$ and is tangent to $C$ at $A'$ (tangency by Lemma 9's normality); for small $dt$ it does not cross $C$, hence lies in the defender-winning region; any intruder move lands on/in that circle, so $p$ drops below 0 unless the intruder plays the optimal response ($A'' = A'$). **Notation slip**: the proof prints "$p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B)$ decreases from 0" where $p(\mathbf{z}_{D'}, \mathbf{z}_{A''}, \mathbf{z}_{B'})$ is meant (p. 6). Also "we can ensure that the circle does not intersect with C" is heuristic (a tangent circle necessarily touches $C$ at $A'$; the intended meaning is that it does not cross to the intruder-winning side).
- **Lemma 11 (Generalization to level sets)** (p. 6): with the defender heading to the optimal breaching point ($\phi_D > 0$), $p$ decreases or stays the same **regardless of intruder behavior**, from any initial state. Proof: payoff level sets $C' = \{\mathbf{z}_A \mid p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B) = k\}$ (Fig. 5(b), p. 6) built by extending normal lines; intruders on one level set need the same minimal time to reach the barrier, so the Lemma 10 argument applies level-set-wise. The existence/smoothness of these level sets inherits all the unproven regularity above.
- **Remark 5 (Defender strategy)** (p. 6): "Optimal defender strategy is to move towards the optimal breaching point at its maximum speed at any time when $\phi_D > 0$." (Together with Lemma 7 for $\phi_D = 0$.)
- **Lemma 12 (Right inequality)** (p. 6): if the intruder keeps moving toward the optimal breaching point, $p$ increases or stays the same regardless of the defender. Proof: informal — symmetry with Lemma 11 ("optimal defender … p stays the same … by Lemma 5") plus a contradiction argument (an arbitrary defender move decreasing $p$ would beat the defender's own optimum), plus the observation that $p$ can increase if the defender wastes time ($\tau_D$ grows indefinitely). This is the weakest proof in the chain (a contradiction against a not-yet-proven defender optimum; no forward-invariance or viscosity argument).
- **Remark 6 (Intruder strategy)** (p. 6): "Optimal intruder strategy is to move towards the optimal breaching point at its maximum speed at any time."
- **Net result**: the Nash sandwich (6) holds with $\Omega^*, \Gamma^*$ = "head to the optimal breaching point at max speed", the Game-of-Degree value is $p^*(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A) = p^{\theta^*}(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \theta^*)$, and the Game-of-Kind outcome is read off the sign of $p^*$ (Lemma 4): $p^* > 0$ inside $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$ (intruder wins), $p^* \le 0$ outside (defender wins, with the boundary falling to the defender per the termination conditions). There is **no HJI/Isaacs PDE verification** of the barrier and no semipermeability argument — the barrier is *constructed* as the zero level set of the candidate optimal payoff and defended by the geometric lemmas above.

### 5.9 The 3D aerial-vs-ground geometry — summary record
- **No projection or dominance-region construction in 3D is used.** All Euclidean geometry lives in two places: the unit sphere (defender geodesic time, eq. (7)) and the ground plane (intruder straight-line time, eqs. (8)–(9), triangles $ABO$). The barrier $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$ is a closed curve in the intruder's plane for each fixed defender state; the "winning region" figures (Fig. 3) are top-down (plane) views. The defender's elevation $\phi_D$ enters only through the scalar geodesic time $\cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$; the azimuthal rate singularity of eq. (2) at the apex is why the barrier approaches a circle as $\phi_D \to \pi/2$.
- **Winning conditions** (p. 2, as printed): intruder — $r(t_f) = 1 \wedge |\psi(t_f)| + |\phi_D(t_f)| > 0$; defender — $\phi_D(t_f) = \psi(t_f) = 0 \wedge r(t_f) > 0$; alignment manifold $\{\psi = \phi_D = 0\}$ stabilizable by the defender per [7]'s speed-advantage argument.
- **Independent numerical verification (mine, scipy)**: for the simulation scenario of Sec. 6 ($\psi = 0.9$, $\phi_D = 0.3\pi$, $r = 2$, $\nu = 0.8$), solving (13)+(25) gives $\theta^* = 1.1337$ rad $= 0.3609\pi$, $\beta^* = 1.1154$ rad, $\tau_D = 1.3193$, $\tau_A = 1.3162$, $p^* = 0.0031 > 0$; a dense scan of $p^\theta$ over $[\psi, \pi)$ peaks at exactly $\theta^*$ — Theorem 1 confirmed as the global maximizer in this scenario, and the initial state confirmed to lie (barely) inside $\mathcal{R}_A$, consistent with Fig. 6's constant near-zero payoff in (a) and intruder win in (c).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated**: (i) the $\theta = \pi$ endpoint is not solved — "The optimal strategy in this case is not stated in the paper" (p. 5, after eq. (28)); (ii) future work: "solve the perimeter-defense problem between aerial defender and aerial intruder" (p. 7); (iii) the analysis is one-defender-one-intruder only (multiplayer 2D versions exist in [7], [9] but are not extended here).

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. **Proof rigor is conference-sketch level.** Lemma 3's monotonicity ($d\psi/d\theta > 0$) rests on an unproven positivity claim for $\frac{d}{d\theta}(x\sin\beta)$ (p. 4, after eq. (27)); Lemma 5's proof is circular (restates eq. (14), p. 5); Lemma 8 computes no curvature despite eq. (29) ("we observe that the curvature is well defined", p. 5); Lemma 9's normality is a two-sentence pictorial argument; Lemma 12 (right Nash inequality) is a symmetry/contradiction argument, the weakest link (p. 6). None of these steps is false as far as can be checked (my numerical checks of Theorem 1 and the geometry (23)–(26) all pass), but the chain Lemma 5 → 12 is not airtight as printed.
2. **Typo in eq. (17), p. 3**: the intermediate equality drops the "− $\cos^{-1}(\cos\phi_D\cos\theta)$" term of eq. (15) and the $d\theta \to 0$ limit; readable only via (14)–(16).
3. **No HJI/Isaacs verification**: the "barrier" is the zero level set of a candidate payoff defended by geometric arguments; semipermeability of $C(\mathbf{z}_D)$ and invariance of $\mathcal{R}_D$ are never formally established — Lemma 4's losing-side proof explicitly defers to Sec. IV-B heuristics ("(i) the intruder is outside of $\mathcal{R}_A$ indefinitely; or (ii) …", p. 5). Boundary allocation (p = 0 → defender) is implicit in the termination conditions rather than proven.
4. **State-space dimension bookkeeping**: the Game-of-Kind barrier is established per fixed defender state as a curve in the intruder's 2D plane; the abstract's claim that it "divides the state space" (3D relative space) is proven only slice-wise, by union over $\phi_D$ (pp. 2, 4).
5. **Strategy-space restriction**: $\gamma_A \in [0, \pi/2]$ forces $\dot r \le 0$ (the intruder can never open range) and, with the symmetric-mirroring convention, monotone azimuth; the value of the game under unrestricted headings is not shown to coincide (p. 2, eq. (3)).
6. **Coordinate singularity at the apex**: eq. (2) gives $\dot\psi_D \to \infty$ as $\phi_D \to \pi/2$; behavior of the construction exactly at $\phi_D = \pi/2$ (defender at the top) is not discussed, though Fig. 3 includes a $\phi_D = 0.5\pi$ panel.
7. **The alignment/capture claim is imported from 2D**: stabilizability of $\{\psi = \phi_D = 0\}$ "due to its speed advantage [7]" (p. 2) is a 2D result reused in 3D without reproof — this is what makes the defender's win condition meaningful, so it is load-bearing.
8. **Simulation is minimal**: one initial condition, $\nu = 0.8$, no integration details, no platform, no repetition; Fig. 3's cross-parameters unreported (p. 4); the claim "regardless of defender's behavior" in (c) is shown for one arbitrary defender trajectory, not for all (p. 6). No hardware experiments despite the motivating drone applications.
9. **Minor slips**: "the following is hold" (Remark 2, p. 3); "both both defender and intruder" (Fig. 6 caption, p. 7); $p(\mathbf{z}_D, \mathbf{z}_A, \mathbf{z}_B)$ printed for the post-move payoff inside Lemma 10's proof (p. 6); overloading of $C$ as crossing point vs. curve in Lemma 4's proof (p. 4).
10. **Preprint status**: arXiv:2012.14980v1, no peer review, no DOI; several gaps above would likely have been caught in review; treat all claims as unreviewed and verify a published version before citing.

## 8. Citation Value
**Paragraph role**: this is the canonical "first 3D step" of the Shishika–Kumar perimeter-defense line — the reference to cite for **surface-constrained defenders in 3D perimeter defense** (defender on a hemisphere vs. intruder on the ground plane), for the **optimal breaching point / breaching-angle formulation** (payoff = geodesic-time minus straight-line-time difference, eqs. (4), (7)–(12)), and for the **barrier-as-zero-level-set construction with Nash-optimal "run to the breach point" strategies** (Theorem 1 + Lemmas 4–12). It complements the planar predecessors [7]–[9] (its eq. (28) recovers the 2D approach angle $\beta = \cos^{-1}\nu$) and the unconstrained-3D reach-avoid line of Yan et al. [14]–[16]. Its weaknesses (informal lemmas, no HJI verification, single-scenario simulation) make it a *geometric template and problem statement* rather than a rigorous benchmark.

**Suggested citation sentences**: "Perimeter defense has been extended to three-dimensional targets by constraining the defender to a hemisphere and the intruder to the ground plane, where the barrier is the zero level set of the geodesic-vs-straight-line time-to-breach difference and the Nash-optimal strategies for both players are to run toward the unique optimal breaching point [P025]." — or, for the degenerate-case link: "In the flat-defender limit ($\phi_D = 0$) the optimal approach angle reduces to $\cos^{-1}\nu$, recovering the planar perimeter-defense result [P025]."

**Caveat**: cite as arXiv:2012.14980 (2020) and verify whether a peer-reviewed version appeared before using it as the sole support for a claim; given the informal proofs, avoid relying on it for statements about barrier semipermeability or the defender-winning region's invariance without independent verification.
