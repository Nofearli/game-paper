## 2. Research Background

Surveillance and securing of perimeters is a canonical civilian/military multi-robot task, and deploying large numbers of autonomous agents for intrusion countermeasures has become practical [p. 1]. Counter-measure literature spans detection/tracking [1], patrolling schedules [2], and GPS spoofing [3]; adversarial interception scenarios are naturally pursuit-evasion games, where the two-player case is solvable via the Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs equation [4] but the joint state space of multi-player games makes HJI intractable, motivating Voronoi [5], [6], cyclic-pursuit [7] and vehicle-routing [8] approximations. When a pursuer must guard an area/target, the game becomes Isaacs' target-guarding problem; its multi-player version is the reach-avoid game [12]–[14]. The paper builds on the pairwise-outcome assignment approach of Chen–Zhou–Tomlin [14] and on the authors' own local-game decomposition for perimeter defense [15] (with the barrier machinery of [16]).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem (perimeter-defense reach-avoid game)** [Sec. II, pp. 1–2]: a convex target region T ⊂ R² with arc-length-parameterized perimeter γ: [0, L) → ∂T; N_D defenders D = {D_i} constrained to move ON the perimeter (position s_Di, x_Di = γ(s_Di); control = signed speed, ṡ_Di = ω_Di, |ω_Di| ≤ v̄_D); N_A intruders A = {A_j} with first-order dynamics in the plane (ẋ_Aj = v_Aj, ‖v_Aj‖ ≤ v̄_A); speed ratio ν = v̄_A/v̄_D ∈ (0, 1] (eq. (1)), i.e., defenders at least as fast; N_A < N_D assumed "for conciseness" [p. 2]. An intruder is captured at zero distance; A_j scores by reaching ∂T uncaptured; **each defender is consumed/eliminated after one capture**. Perfect state feedback: z = [s_D1, …, s_DND, x_A1^T, …, x_ANA^T]^T known to all. Team strategies Γ_D, Γ_A are state-feedback maps; the problem is min_{Γ_D} max_{Γ_A} Q(z0; Γ_D, Γ_A) (eq. (2)) and the value Q*(z0), where Q counts intruders that score.

**Gap, as claimed by the authors** [Sec. I, p. 1] vs verification:
- [authors' claim] The assignment approach of [14] (solve all pairwise 2-player games, assign each defender one feasible intruder via maximum matching) gives an upper bound on the intruder score but is "potentially suboptimal because it can not consider cooperative maneuvers for capture." — Verified structurally in Sec. III-C: MM draws an edge only if x_Aj ∈ R_D(D_i) (individual winning region), so a pair-required capture (intruder in R_pair, eq. (4)) is invisible to MM.
- [authors' claim] The authors' prior extension [15] added 2v1 "pincer" cooperative defense but "required a solution to the maximum-independent-set problem which is NP-hard." — Consistent with the MIS construction described in Sec. III-C (augmented graph with pair nodes; conflicting assignments excluded via independent set) and confirmed empirically in Fig. 9 (MIS computed by exhaustive search; for N_A ≥ 30 some searches could not complete, p. 10).
- [authors' claim] "To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to show such optimality (equilibrium strategies) in a pursuit-evasion game played between teams." — Plausible within the paper's own related-work survey ([14] provides bounds only, no equilibrium for teams); the "first" claim is not independently verifiable from this PDF.
- [authors' claim] Existing methods "suffer from either suboptimal defender performance or computational complexity." — Verified by the chain Q_LG ≤ Q_MIS ≤ Q_MM [p. 3] (MIS better than MM but NP-hard; MM polynomial but suboptimal) and quantified in Figs. 8b/9.

**Residual gap the paper leaves**: the optimality (saddle-point) result holds only for the class of configurations with q̂^k ≤ 1 ∀k after removal of Q_LG(z0) uncapturable intruders (Corollary 1); outside this class the LGR policy still supplies a polynomial-time certificate but its exactness is open.

## 4. Core Contributions

Stated at [p. 1, end of Sec. I] and verified against the technical sections:

1. **Theory (decomposition)**: A novel *local-game decomposition* of the perimeter into N_D² local game regions (LGRs) generated by ordered defender pairs (D_R^k, D_L^k) [Sec. IV-A, p. 3], turning the global team game into a family of n_D-vs-n_A local games whose combinatorics live on a circular-arc graph.
2. **Theory (intruder side / lower bound)**: The guaranteed intruder score Q_LG = max_G Σ_{k∈G} max{q^k, 0} (eq. (6)) computable in O(N_D^4) as a maximum-weight independent set on a circular arc graph [Sec. IV-C, p. 4], with an intruder team strategy (Theorem 1, eq. (7)) realizing it against ANY defender strategy.
3. **Algorithm (defender side)**: A polynomial-time *LGR defense policy* (Algorithms 1–4, pp. 5–6) that dynamically re-assigns defenders through three engagement types — 1v1, cooperative 2v1 (pincer), and the newly identified *implicit assignment* (Def. 1, Lemma 2) and *sequential paired defense* (Def. 2) structures — with complexities O(N_A N_D^4) (one-shot removal), O(N_D² N_A²) (2v1 assignment), O((N_A+N_D)^2.5) (1v1 assignment via fast bipartite matching [18]).
4. **Theory (optimality)**: Performance guarantees — zero score under Q_LG(z0) = 0, q̂^k ≤ 1 ∀k (Theorem 2, via Lemmas 3–5 and Hall's marriage theorem [17]); the defender-side bound Q(z0; Γ*_D, Γ_A) ≤ Q_LG(z0) for the general Q_LG > 0 case after removing Q_LG(z0) uncapturable intruders (Lemma 6, Theorem 3, eq. (10)); and the **saddle-point equilibrium with game value Q*(z0) = Q_LG(z0)** for that class (Corollary 1, eq. (11)) — the paper's headline result.
5. **Experiment (numerical)**: A 6-vs-5 illustrative simulation showing emergent sequential 2v1 defense without look-ahead planning (Fig. 7); Monte-Carlo statistics over 10,000 configurations quantifying MM's suboptimality gap Q_MM − Q_LG (up to ≈15 points, Fig. 8); and computation-time scaling validating the polynomial bounds against NP-hard MIS (Fig. 9, Appendix C). A multi-robot simulation video is provided (https://youtu.be/6zUPkzh_iPU).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

### 5.1 Setup: dynamics, information, winning conditions

- Defenders: 1-DOF on the perimeter curve, ṡ_Di = ω_Di, |ω_Di| ≤ v̄_D; x_Di = γ(s_Di) [p. 2]. Intruders: single integrators in R², ẋ_Aj = v_Aj, ‖v_Aj‖ ≤ v̄_A [p. 2]. Speed ratio ν = v̄_A/v̄_D ∈ (0, 1] (eq. (1)) — defenders never slower; N_A < N_D.
- Termination/scoring: capture at zero distance; A_j scores at x_Aj ∈ ∂T uncaptured; **a defender that captures is eliminated** ("consumed"), justified by [authors' claim, p. 2] "[15] shows a method for the intruders to ensure one defender can not make multiple captures" (not re-proved here).
- Information: perfect state feedback z; strategies are feedback maps Γ_D, Γ_A [p. 2]. Payoff Q(z0; Γ_D, Γ_A) = number of scoring intruders; zero-sum problem (2).
- No HJI PDE is solved anywhere in this paper; all geometry (barriers, winning regions) is imported from prior work [15], [16] — "only their existence is sufficient to complete the analysis in this paper" [p. 2].

### 5.2 Imported building blocks (Properties, pp. 2–3)

- **1v1 game** [Sec. III-A, p. 2]: game space splits into intruder-winning R_A(s_D) and defender-winning R_D(s_D), separated by the **barrier** [16] — for perimeter defense a closed curve through the defender position.
- **Property 1 (p. 2)**: the barrier consists of left and right barriers; from x_A ∈ R_D the intruder cannot penetrate the left (resp. right) barrier so long as the defender moves ccw (resp. cw) [15], [16]. Consequence: from R_D the defender keeps the intruder in R_D forever (global, analytic result inherited from [16]).
- **2v1 game** [Sec. III-B, p. 2]: naive independent combination R_I(i, j) = R_A(i) ∩ R_A(j) (eq. (3)); the true intruder-winning region R_C(i, j) (subscript C = cooperative) is *strictly smaller*; its boundary combines the left barrier of D_i, a circle centered at the defenders' midpoint, and the right barrier of D_j (Fig. 2). **Paired-defense region**: R_pair(i, j) ≜ R_I(i, j) − R_C(i, j) (eq. (4)).
- **Property 2 (p. 2)**: if x_A ∈ R_pair(i, j) and the pair performs the pincer maneuver [ω_Di, ω_Dj] = [1, −1] (boundary defenders converge at full speed toward the midpoint), then x_A ∈ R_D(i) or x_A ∈ R_D(j) occurs before the intruder reaches the perimeter [15], [16]. This is the workhorse transition lemma for everything downstream.
- **Existing assignment bounds** [Sec. III-C, p. 3]: Maximum Matching on the bipartite graph (edge iff x_Aj ∈ R_D(D_i)) captures N_MM^cap intruders, upper bound Q_MM = N_A − N_MM^cap (unnumbered eq., p. 3), polynomial time [14]; Maximum Independent Set augments with defender-pair nodes, Q_MIS ≤ Q_MM but NP-hard [15]. The proposed bound satisfies Q_LG ≤ Q_MIS ≤ Q_MM (the first inequality is forced by consistency: Theorem 1 makes Q_LG a lower bound on the outcome under ANY defense, while Q_MIS is an upper bound under MIS defense — both cannot hold with Q_MIS < Q_LG).

### 5.3 Local-game decomposition and the intruder guarantee (pp. 3–4)

- **LGR / I-LGR** [Sec. IV-A, p. 3]: each ordered pair D_pair^k = (D_R^k, D_L^k) (right/left-boundary defenders), k ∈ {1, …, N_D²}, generates the LGR R_C^k (= 2v1 intruder-winning region R_C) and the Independent-LGR R_I^k; degenerate i = j gives R_I(i, i) = R_C(i, i) = R_A(i).
- **Property 3 (p. 3)**: the boundary of an I-LGR consists of the left barrier of D_R^k and the right barrier of D_L^k.
- **Property 4 (p. 3)**: the intersection of two I-LGRs is an I-LGR (closure property underpinning the induction arguments in Lemmas 3–4).
- **Subteams** (p. 3): defender sub-team S_D^k = defenders on the ccw segment from s_D_R^k to s_D_L^k (excluding the boundary defenders), n_D^k = |S_D^k|; intruder subteams S_A^k ⊂ R_C^k (n_A^k) and Ŝ_A^k ⊂ R_I^k (n̂_A^k); since R_C^k ⊆ R_I^k, n_A^k ≤ n̂_A^k, and the difference Δn_A^k ≜ n̂_A^k − n_A^k counts intruders inside R_pair^k. (Fig. 3 example: 7th LGR has D_pair^7 = (D4, D3), S_D^7 = {D1, D2}, S_A^7 = {A2}, Ŝ_A^7 = {A1, A2}.)
- **Lemma 1 (Local game score, pp. 3–4)**: define q^k ≜ n_A^k − n_D^k. If ∃k with q^k > 0, the intruder team scores at least q^k. *Proof*: all n_A^k intruders in R_C^k play the 2v1 game against the boundary pair (approach their midpoint); defenders outside the LGR cannot reach them (barrier geometry), and at most n_D^k are captured since each defender captures at most once — the remaining q^k score. Local, analytic; the decisive counting inequality is q^k = n_A^k − n_D^k > 0 under the one-capture-per-defender assumption.
- **Partitioning and guaranteed score** [Sec. IV-C, p. 4]: G ⊂ {1, …, N_D²} a set of pairwise-disjoint LGRs (R_C^k ∩ R_C^l = ∅); Q_low(G) ≜ Σ_{k∈G} max{q^k, 0} (eq. (5)); G* ≜ arg max_G Q_low(G); **Q_LG ≜ Q_low(G*) = max_G (Σ_{k∈G} max{q^k, 0})** (eq. (6)). Computable in O(N_D^4) by maximum-weight independent set on a circular arc graph [15]. Cheap intrusion test: Q_LG > 0 ⇔ ∃ q^k > 0 (p. 4). (Fig. 4 example: 21st LGR has q²¹ = 3 − 3 = 0; optimal partition G* = {10, 11} gives Q_LG = q¹⁰ + q¹¹ = 2.)
- **Theorem 1 (p. 4, eq. (7))**: the intruder team strategy Γ*_A (team up into the disjoint LGRs of G*, then each intruder approaches the midpoint between the boundary defenders of its LGR) guarantees Q(z0; Γ_D, Γ*_A) ≥ Q_LG(z0), ∀Γ_D ∈ U_D (eq. (7)). *Proof*: Lemma 1 plus the disjointness of the subteams in G*. Global guarantee, analytic; assumes the one-capture-per-defender rule and the 2v1 winning strategy of [15], [16]. Physical meaning: no defender team — however cooperative — can hold back more than N_A − Q_LG intruders; Q_LG is a true game lower bound, not a heuristic.

### 5.4 Extended engagement types: the two mechanisms absent from MM/MIS (pp. 4–5)

- **Definition 1 (Implicit assignment structure, p. 4)**: a 2-vs-2 engagement where a defender pair is assigned to one intruder in R_pair(i, j) while the other intruder lies in R_D(i) ∩ R_D(j) (the *implicit assignment zone*). **Lemma 2 (p. 4)**: this structure resolves into two 1v1 assignments before any intruder reaches the perimeter. *Proof*: executing the 2v1 assignment, D_i moves ccw and D_j moves cw; the second intruder A2 remains in R_D(i) ∩ R_D(j) because it cannot penetrate D_i's left barrier nor D_j's right barrier (Property 1); before the defenders meet at the midpoint, A1 is driven into R_D(i) or R_D(j) (Property 2), freeing the other defender for a 1v1 on A2. Physical meaning: a greedy pincer against one intruder *implicitly* guards a second — one capture "for free," invisible to both MM (A1 has no individual edge) and MIS (which must choose between the pair-assignment and the single assignment, both worth one capture).
- **Definition 2 (Sequential paired defense, p. 4)**: an (n+1)-vs-n engagement generated by n interrelated 2v1 games with overlapping defenders (e.g., Fig. 6: D_j participates in two 2v1 games — an assignment prohibited in the MIS formulation).
- **Definition 3 (Conflicting assignments, p. 5)**: overlapping 2v1 assignments are *conflicting* if they require opposing (cw and ccw) motion of the same defender. Non-conflicting overlap is exactly what lets one defender serve several pincers sequentially.
- **Independent local game score (I-LGS)** [Sec. V-C, p. 5, eq. (8)]: q̂^k ≜ n̂_A^k − n_D^k = q^k + Δn_A^k, where Δn_A^k counts intruders in R_pair^k. Critical values: q^k = 0 was critical for the intruder side (Lemma 1); for the defense the critical number is **q̂^k = 1** — with q^k ≤ 0 and q̂^k = 1 the pincer on the single R_pair intruder plus the subteam S_D^k can still catch everyone, but the boundary defenders (D_R^k, D_L^k) MUST perform the pincer; otherwise that intruder enters R_C^k, making q^k > 0 and guaranteeing a score [p. 5].

### 5.5 The LGR defense policy (Algorithms 1–4, pp. 5–6)

- **Algorithm 1 (LGR Defense, p. 5)**: (i) once at start, remove uncapturable intruders (Alg. 2) to obtain a virtual game with Q_LG = 0; (ii) every time step, assign 2v1 defenses (Alg. 3), then 1v1 defenses (Alg. 4); the output per defender is a *direction of motion* (the assignments are re-computed online and change dynamically, Sec. V). Restricted throughout to configurations with q̂^k ≤ 1 ∀k after removal.
- **Algorithm 2 (Removal of uncapturable intruders, p. 5)**: while Q_LG > 0, test intruders one by one; if deleting A_i reduces Q_LG, delete it (Armv). By Lemma 6 (below) each intruder needs to be visited only once (the answer is independent of prior removals); < N_A iterations; bottleneck is the O(N_D^4) Q_LG evaluation → **O(N_A N_D^4)** [p. 7, Appendix C: ≈ O(N_A^5) when N_D = N_A + 1].
- **Algorithm 3 (Assign 2v1 defense, p. 5)**: while ∃k with q̂^k = 1, pick m = arg max_k n_D^k among those k, assign one intruder in R_pair^m to the boundary pair (allowed to overlap existing assignments → creates sequential paired defense), remove the pair's defenders from the 1v1 pool, recompute all q̂ without assigned intruders. Terminates in < N_A iterations; bottleneck = O(N_D² N_A) q̂ recomputation → **O(N_D² N_A²)** [p. 5].
- **Algorithm 4 (Assign 1v1 defense, p. 6)**: build a bipartite graph over {D_2v1, D_1v1} × A_1v1; a 2v1 pair gets *implicit-assignment* edges (to intruders in R_D(D_R^k) ∩ R_D(D_L^k)) only if it is the outermost pair of its sequential structure, i.e., **n_D^k > n_D^m, ∀m ∈ D_2v1 s.t. D_pair^m ∩ D_pair^k ≠ ∅** (eq. (9), p. 6) — necessary and sufficient since each (n+1)-vs-n structure can absorb at most one extra intruder; single defenders in D_1v1 get edges to all intruders in their R_D; solve maximum matching but keep only the D_1v1 edges (2v1 assignments are "implicit," unaffected by the matching). With ≤ N_D + N_A nodes, matching costs **O((N_A + N_D)^w), w < 2.5** [Mucha–Sankowski, 18] (restated as O((N_A+N_D)^2.5), p. 10). (Fig. 7a walk-through, p. 6: D2 ends up with two 2v1 games; the (D1, D4) pair takes A5 implicitly and the matching gives A4 to D5.)
- The novelty relative to MM/MIS is precisely the *dynamic* re-assignment: implicit assignments appear and resolve in flight (Lemma 2), and 2v1 assignments are consumed one by one (Lemma 5) — "dynamically changing assignments which the existing MM and MIS approaches fail to capture" [Sec. V, p. 4].

### 5.6 Performance guarantees and saddle-point equilibrium (pp. 6–7)

- **Lemma 3 (p. 6)**: if Q_LG(z) = 0 and q̂^k(z) ≤ 1 ∀k, then no intruder is unmarked — every intruder has a 1v1, 2v1, or implicit assignment. *Proof*: Hall's marriage theorem [17] on the Algorithm-4 graph; show |W| ≤ |N(W)| ∀W ⊆ A_1v1 by induction. Base |W| = 1: an edge-less intruder would put q̂ ≥ 1 on the smallest I-LGR containing it, contradicting Algorithm 3's termination. Inductive step: if the graph splits into m components, neighbor sets are disjoint so |N(W)| = Σ|N(w_i)| ≥ Σ|w_i| = |W|; if connected with |N(W)| ≤ n, "there exists an I-LGR whose defender subteam is N(W) and the intruder subteam is W," giving q̂ ≥ (n+1) − n = 1 — contradiction. **Proof gap (minor)**: the existence of that I-LGR for an arbitrary connected subgraph is asserted from the interval structure of I-LGRs (Properties 3–4) but not demonstrated; likewise the disjointness of intruder sets across components is used implicitly.
- **Lemma 4 (p. 6)**: the sequential paired defense generated by Algorithm 3 has no conflict (Def. 3). *Proof*: D_i can conflict only if it has two I-LGRs l, r with q̂^l = q̂^r = 1 and D_R^l = D_i = D_L^r. Consider R_I^m ≜ R_I(D_R^r, D_L^l) ⊇ R_I^l ∪ R_I^r; then q̂^m ≥ (n_D^l + 1) + (n_D^r + 1) − (n_D^l + n_D^r + 1) = 1 — the decisive counting inequality (defender subteam of m is the union, size n_D^l + n_D^r + 1; intruder count inherits q̂ = 1 on both sides). Since Q_LG = 0 ⇒ q^m = 0, R_pair^m contains ≥ 1 intruder assigned to D_pair^m, whose pincer removes one intruder from l or r; hence D_i performs 2v1 for only one side — no conflict. **Proof gap (minor)**: the count n̂_A^m ≥ n̂_A^l + n̂_A^r implicitly assumes the l- and r-intruder subteams are disjoint (geometrically plausible on opposite sides of D_i, not argued).
- **Lemma 5 (p. 6; full proof Appendix A, p. 9)**: if initially Q_LG(z0) = 0 and q̂^k(z0) ≤ 1 ∀k, then (i) the number of 2v1 assignments decreases over time and (ii) Q_LG(z) = 0 and q̂^k(z) ≤ 1 ∀k are invariant. *Proof (App. A)*: by Lemma 4 each pair plays its pincer optimally; take the FIRST transition (Property 2) among all 2v1 games, indexed k1: q̂^{k1} drops 1 → 0. Any I-LGR containing that intruder after the transition already contained it before, so q̂^k is non-increasing ∀k. q^k cannot turn positive: route (i) an intruder moving R_I^k → R_C^k is a R_pair → R_C move with q̂^k = 1, blocked by the pincer (Property 2); route (ii) an intruder entering from outside R_I^k is impossible since R_C^k ⊂ R_I^k and no intruder newly enters any R_I (monotonicity above). Hence Q_LG = 0 is preserved, Lemma 4's hypotheses persist, and transitions repeat until no 2v1 assignment remains. (Footnote 1: simultaneous transitions handled similarly.)
- **Theorem 2 (p. 7)**: if initially Q_LG(z0) = 0 and q̂^k(z0) ≤ 1 ∀k, the LGR defense (Algorithm 1) guarantees overall score zero. *Proof*: Lemmas 2 and 5 exhaust the implicit and 2v1 assignments over time; the invariants persist (Lemma 5); Lemma 3 then marks every intruder, so every intruder holds a 1v1 assignment before reaching the perimeter — by the 1v1 barrier property (Property 1) none can score. Global, analytic, exact.
- **Lemma 6 (p. 7; full proof Appendix B, p. 9)**: if Q_LG(z0) > 0, there exists a set of exactly Q_LG(z0) intruders whose removal yields a new game with Q_LG = 0. *Proof by contradiction*: if no single removal reduces Q_LG by 1, there exist two optimal partitions G1*, G2* with no common intruders (a shared intruder's removal would cut both scores). Take k ∈ G1* with q^k > 0 and try to merge it into G2*: (a) if R_C^k ∩ R_C^l = ∅ ∀l ∈ G2*, then G3 = {k} ∪ G2* is a valid disjoint partition with Q_low(G3) > Q_low(G2*) — contradicting optimality of eq. (6); (b) if k intersects some l ∈ G2*, let m be the smallest LGR containing both; intersecting regions give n_D^m < n_D^k + n_D^l while disjoint intruder sets give n_A^m ≥ n_A^k + n_A^l, hence **q^m = n_A^m − n_D^m > q^k + q^l**, again contradicting optimality. Iterate; removal order is irrelevant. (Minor gap: existence/uniqueness of the "smallest containing LGR" appeals to the interval structure without proof.)
- **Theorem 3 (p. 7, eq. (10))**: if q̂^k(z0) ≤ 1 ∀k after removing the Q_LG(z0) intruders found by Algorithm 2, then Γ*_D guarantees Q(z0; Γ*_D, Γ_A) ≤ Q_LG(z0), ∀Γ_A ∈ U_A (eq. (10)). *Proof*: Lemma 6 turns the original game into a virtual Q_LG = 0 game satisfying Theorem 2's hypotheses (all remaining intruders captured); in the original game at most the Q_LG(z0) ignored intruders can score.
- **Corollary 1 (p. 7, eq. (11))**: under the same condition, Q(Γ*_D, Γ_A) ≤ Q(Γ*_D, Γ*_A) ≤ Q(Γ_D, Γ*_A) (eq. (11)) — the pair (Γ*_D, Γ*_A) is a **saddle-point equilibrium** and the value is **Q*(z0) = Q_LG(z0)**. This is the paper's central result: the combinatorial quantity eq. (6) is not merely a bound but the exact game value for the class {q̂^k ≤ 1 ∀k after removal}; Theorem 1 supplies the ≤ side for all configurations, Theorem 3 the ≥ side for the class. If intruders stick to Γ*_A the defenders cannot reduce the score; if defenders keep Γ*_D the intruders cannot raise it [p. 7].

### 5.7 Complexity summary

Q_LG/G*: O(N_D^4) (circular-arc MWIS, eq. (6)); Alg. 2 (run once): O(N_A N_D^4); Alg. 3 (per step): O(N_D² N_A²); Alg. 4 (per step): O((N_A + N_D)^2.5) [pp. 4–7, 10]. All polynomial — versus NP-hard MIS [15] — while strictly dominating MM in bound quality.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (future work, Sec. VIII, p. 8)**: extension to three dimensions; environments with obstacles; distributed algorithms with relaxed information structure (local sensing and communication); cooperative defense with heterogeneous teams.

**Stated restrictions of the analysis** (with locations):
- N_A < N_D "for conciseness" [p. 2]; ν ≤ 1 (defenders never slower, eq. (1)); defenders confined to the perimeter (1-DOF) [pp. 1–2]; perfect global state feedback [p. 2]; each defender eliminated after one capture, justified only by reference to [15] [p. 2].
- The optimality result (Theorems 2–3, Corollary 1) is confined to the class {q̂^k ≤ 1 ∀k after Algorithm-2 removal}; for configurations with some q̂^k ≥ 2 the LGR policy still outputs assignments but no exactness claim is made — the game value there is open [pp. 5–7].
- All barrier/winning-region geometry (Properties 1–2) is imported from [15], [16]; proofs are not self-contained ("only their existence is sufficient," p. 2), so the archive's verification of the equilibrium chain is conditional on those prior results.
- Algorithm 2's removal is computed once at t = 0 [p. 5]; no claim is made about re-running it mid-game after defender losses (defenders being consumable, the Q_LG = 0 invariant could in principle break if a defender is eliminated — the paper's guarantees implicitly assume the capture order keeps the invariant; the elimination dynamics are not explicitly re-examined after Theorem 2).

**Critical observations (this archive, with page/eq. pointers)**:
- Minor proof gaps: Lemma 3's induction step asserts the existence of an I-LGR realizing an arbitrary connected subgraph's defender/intruder subteams [p. 6]; Lemma 4's count q̂^m ≥ 1 assumes disjointness of the l- and r-intruder subteams [p. 6]; Lemma 6 uses the "smallest LGR containing both k and l" without proving existence/uniqueness [p. 9]. All are plausible from the interval structure of I-LGRs (Properties 3–4) but are asserted rather than proven.
- Editorial issues: the Introduction's roadmap skips Section V ("Section IV presents the decomposition method. Section VI proposes the defense policy," p. 1); "At t = 80" should read t = 0.080 s and "the other four defenders will be captured" should read "intruders" [p. 7]; reference [17] (Hall) shows garbled page numbers "2630" (should be 26–30) [p. 8]; "resulting to" for "resulting in" [p. 7].
- The statistical comparisons (Figs. 8–9) compare *certified bounds* (Q_MM, Q_MIS, Q_LG) rather than simulated closed-loop scores; the closed-loop guarantee is established theoretically (Theorems 1–3), but no Monte-Carlo of actual play against a non-equilibrium intruder is reported.
- The illustrative scenario's own parameters (ν, distances, speeds) are UNREPORTED; reproducibility of Fig. 7 relies on the companion video and prior-work conventions.
- The value Q*(z0) = Q_LG(z0) is established per initial condition; a characterization of the winning class {Q_LG = 0, q̂ ≤ 1} in terms of simple geometric primitives (e.g., counts vs. spacing) is not attempted.

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: this is the landmark "team-vs-team exactness" paper for perimeter-defense reach-avoid games: it converts the assignment-based defense paradigm (pairwise outcomes [14], NP-hard cooperative augmentation [15]) into a polynomial-time, provably optimal team strategy for a class of configurations, by decomposing the perimeter into local games induced by defender pairs and proving a matching pair of intruder/defender bounds (eqs. (6)–(7) vs. (10)) that closes into a saddle-point equilibrium (Corollary 1). It is the natural citation for (i) local-game / dominant-region decomposition of team games, (ii) cooperative 2v1 (pincer) and implicit-assignment defense mechanisms, (iii) circular-arc-graph algorithms for perimeter-defense combinatorics, and (iv) equilibria in multiplayer PE games between teams; it also anchors the follow-up line of Shishika et al. (team composition with patrollers [19], arbitrary convex shapes [16]).

**Suggested citation sentences**:
- "Shishika et al. proved the first saddle-point equilibrium for a multi-player perimeter-defense game, showing that the local-game-decomposition score Q_LG is the exact game value whenever every independent local game score satisfies q̂^k ≤ 1 after removal of uncapturable intruders [P015]."
- "Unlike maximum-matching defenses, which cannot exploit cooperative capture, the polynomial-time LGR policy of [P015] dynamically composes 1v1, 2v1 (pincer), and implicit assignments, strictly improving the certified score bound (Q_LG ≤ Q_MIS ≤ Q_MM) at O(N_D²N_A²) per-step cost."
