## 2. Research Background

The lifeline game, originated by Isaacs in 1960 [7] (ref. list prints the Wiley book as 1965), is a pursuit-evasion game of kind in which the evader tries to reach a boundary ("lifeline") of the play area before a faster pursuer closes within the capture radius; prior literature ([7], [8], [13], [14], [23], [36]) treats only a single lifeline. This paper motivates the two-lifeline extension with engineering scenarios: two defense ships guarding two coastlines from an intruder, RobMaster robots attacking an opponent before it reaches a buffer/target line, and collision avoidance where a robot must reach one of two target lines while dodging two moving obstacles (p. 1). The planar game area Ω_play is the strip between two parallel lifelines L1 (y = 0) and L2 (y = d) separated by distance d, and the two target areas Ω_goal lie beyond them; the evader wins by crossing either lifeline uncaptured, while two pursuers win by capturing it (nonzero capture radius l) inside the strip (p. 2, Fig. 1). Two lifelines give the evader two candidate terminal manifolds, so the classical single-manifold Isaacs barrier machinery (BUP + retrograde path equations) no longer applies directly, and the presence of two pursuers adds a multi-agent coupling. The methodological backdrop is the reach-avoid game literature ([20]–[24]), whose HJI-based resolution suffers from dimensionality for multi-player dynamics (p. 2), which motivates the paper's decomposition + explicit (geometric) policy approach ([27]–[32]).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

- **Problem (authors' statement, p. 1–3)**: a two-pursuer one-evader differential game between two parallel lifelines: determine (Q1) "Under what initial state conditions can optimal play guarantee a win for either the pursuers or the evader?" and (Q2) "Assuming a guaranteed win ..., what are the optimal strategies for both to either minimize or maximize the cost function (4)?" (p. 3).
- **Gap claim 1**: [authors' claim] "To the best of our knowledge, only the case of one lifeline has been studied in the relevant literature. This paper first presents the problem of pursuit-evasion game with two lifelines" (p. 1). [Verified from cited text]: every lifeline reference in their list indeed concerns one lifeline — Isaacs' simple two-player lifeline game [7], limited-lifetime lifeline game [8], time-optimal lifeline games [13], [14], three-player (single) lifeline game [23], and the classical l-capture lifeline game [36]; the reach-avoid works [20]–[24] allow general target sets but are solved by HJI/numerics or matching, not by explicit barriers for two lifelines. The novelty claim is consistent with the paper's own 36-item corpus; no contradicting reference appears in the list (independent verification beyond this corpus not performed here).
- **Gap claim 2**: [authors' claim, p. 2 / Remark 5.1, p. 7] "In cases involving multiple terminal conditions, the classical Isaacs method cannot be applied." [Verified]: this is the authors' assertion; the paper itself notes "for a game with three players and two lifelines, it is difficult to solve the RPEs due to the existence of more than one terminal manifold" (Conclusion, p. 8). Plausible (RPE integration needs a single manifold of terminal conditions), but no formal impossibility proof is given.
- **Gap claim 3**: [authors' claim, p. 2] maximal-matching decompositions [29], [30] reduce multiplayer games only to one-on-one subgames; [verified] the authors state their decomposition instead yields two (one pursuer vs. one evader with two lifelines) sub-games whose barriers are then merged, and additionally propose the resultant-force method that "integrates the effects of multiple pursuers and lifelines on the evader" (p. 2).
- **Gap claim 4**: [authors' claim, p. 3] under simple motion and a Mayer-type cost "the optimal trajectory of these players is represented as straight lines" — asserted (standard for simple motion), not proven in the paper.

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **(Modeling)** First formulation of a two-lifeline differential game with two pursuers P1, P2 (speed vP) and one evader E (speed vE), capture radius l > 0, speed ratio a = vP/vE > 1, two target sets C (capture) and D (lifeline crossing), terminal payoff J = min{ye(tf1), d − ye(tf2)} (eq. (4)) and value V = max_{u1,u2} min_{uE} J (eq. (5)), under a non-anticipative information structure (p. 2–3).
2. **(Theory — reachable region)** Characterization of the evader's guaranteed reachable region R = R1 ∩ R2 by two Cartesian ovals C1, C2 (Lemma 3.1, eq. (7)), generalizing the Apollonius circle [10], [11] to nonzero capture radius; a = 1 degenerates to hyperbolas (Fig. 2).
3. **(Theory — pursuit)** Closed-loop pursuer capture strategy (8) (heading angle ε as a function of the evader's control angle ς) guaranteeing capture on or inside the Cartesian oval, robust to evader direction changes via a tangency-chaining argument credited to [36] (Lemma 3.2).
4. **(Theory — barriers/winning regions)** Barrier construction for the one-pursuer–one-evader two-lifeline sub-game (Theorem 4.1, eq. (17)–(18) plus companion barrier (27)), vertical-escape boundaries B3/B4 (eq. (28)–(30)), and region-wise optimal strategies (eq. (31)–(32)); extension to the two-pursuer game by decomposition and merging of eight barriers/boundaries B1–B8 (Theorem 5.1, eq. (33)).
5. **(Algorithm — mechanics-inspired evasion)** A resultant-force method: pursuer repulsions (inverse-square) and lifeline attractions (2/ye-type, from integrating an infinite line) determine the evader's heading (Theorem 5.2, eq. (34)–(38)), extended to n pursuers (eq. (39)); motivated because the exact intersection points of two Cartesian ovals with the lifelines are "difficult to obtain ... due to the high degree of the equation" (p. 7).
6. **(Experiment)** Simulations validating the region partition (Figs. 4–6) and the escape/capture strategies (Figs. 8–9), including a three-pursuer case.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.0 Setup (p. 2–3, eq. (1)–(6), termination criteria)

- **Dynamics (eq. (1))**: simple motion, ẋi = vP cos ϕi, ẏi = vP sin ϕi (i = 1, 2); ẋe = vE cos φ, ẏe = vE sin φ. Controls are headings u1 = (cos ϕ1, sin ϕ1)⊤, u2 = (cos ϕ2, sin ϕ2)⊤, uE = (cos φ, sin φ)⊤ in admissible sets U1, U2, Ue. Speeds fixed, directions piecewise-continuous in time; **a = vP/vE > 1** ("If a < 1, it would not be meaningful ... as the evader can successfully reach the target area when the initial distance between pursuers and evader is greater than l", p. 3). L1 fixed at y = 0, L2 at y = d; ye is the evader's distance from L1.
- **Target sets (eq. (2)–(3))**: C = {x(x1, x2, xE) ∈ R^6 | ‖xE − x1‖2 ≤ l ∨ ‖xE − x2‖2 ≤ l} (capture), D = {x(x1, x2, xE) ∈ R^6 | ye ≤ 0 ∨ ye ≥ d} (lifeline crossed). Capture means distance to at least one pursuer ≤ l.
- **Termination criteria (p. 3, unnumbered)**: (1) if min{ye, d − ye} < 0 and min{‖x1 − xE‖2, ‖x2 − xE‖2} > l, the evader wins; (2) if min{ye, d − ye} ≥ 0 and min{‖x1 − xE‖2, ‖x2 − xE‖2} ≤ l, the pursuers win. (Measure-zero simultaneous events are not covered.)
- **Information structure (p. 3)**: non-anticipative, after [19], [20]: pursuers use perfect information plus the evader's current input, u1(t) = α(x1(t), xE(t), uE(t)), u2(t) = β(x2(t), xE(t), uE(t)); the evader knows the opponents' positions and the speed ratio a.
- **Payoff and value (eq. (4)–(5))**: J1 = ye(tf1) for targeting L1, J2 = d − ye(tf2) for L2; overall J(u1, u2, uE; x0) = min{ye(tf1), d − ye(tf2)} — the evader minimizes (reaches the nearer lifeline fast, choosing L2 iff ye(tf1) > d − ye(tf2), free choice at equality), pursuers maximize: V(x0) = max_{u1,u2} min_{uE} J. *Gap note*: the payoff value upon capture (neither tf1 nor tf2 realized) is not defined.
- **Hamiltonian (p. 3, unnumbered)**: H = Σ_{i=1}^{2} vP(λxi sin ϕi + λyi cos ϕi) + vE(λxe sin φ + λye cos φ). *Printed exactly so* — the sin/cos roles are swapped relative to dynamics (1) (cos ϕi multiplies ẋi there); this is a typo in the paper, harmless for the paper's arguments since they never differentiate H (they invoke straight-line optimality from the Mayer-type cost instead).
- **Evader reachable region (eq. (6))**: R = {x ∈ R² × [t0, ts] | ∃uE(·) ∈ Ue, ∀u1(·) ∈ U1, u2(·) ∈ U2, tf ∈ [t0, ts], xE(tf; x0_E, uE, t0) = x} — points E can guarantee to reach for any pursuer controls. *Minor formal gap*: the prose adds "without being captured by the pursuers", but the quantified formula does not include an explicit path constraint x(t) ∉ C for t < tf; capture-avoidance enters only implicitly through the oval construction.

### 5.1 Lemma 3.1 (p. 3, eq. (7)) — Reachable region by Cartesian ovals

- **Statement**: The evader's reachable region and its boundaries are R = R1 ∩ R2 with C1 = {xG | ‖xG − xE‖2 = (1/a)(‖xG − x1‖2 − l)}, C2 = {xG | ‖xG − xE‖2 = (1/a)(‖xG − x2‖2 − l)}, where R1 = {xG | ‖xG − xE‖2 < (1/a)(‖xG − x1‖2 − l)} and R2 = {xG | ‖xG − xE‖2 < (1/a)(‖xG − x2‖2 − l)}; xG = (xG, yG) any point of R².
- **Assumptions**: initial distances from E to each pursuer exceed l; a > 1; capture radius l > 0.
- **Proof approach** (1 step, by citation): "As shown in a proof provided by [35]" (Fu & Liu, Automatica 2023 — HJI justification of the geometric solution of target-defense games with faster defenders and convex target area). *Proof gap*: no self-contained derivation here; the HJI-based justification is outsourced.
- **Decisive equation**: the oval condition a‖xG − xE‖2 = ‖xG − x1‖2 − l, i.e., time balance vE-time = (pursuer distance − l)/vP: E reaches xG exactly when P1's capture disc (radius l) does.
- **Meaning/properties**: C1, C2 are Cartesian ovals ("a close and strictly convex curve [36]"), intersecting at O1, O2 (Fig. 2a); for a = 1 they become hyperbolas and the region between them is R (Fig. 2b). R is the intersection of the two per-pursuer regions — a global (2-D plane) analytic characterization, the exact analog of the Apollonius circle for l > 0. **Extensibility**: per-pursuer intersection form extends verbatim to more pursuers (used in Sec. V-A regions 7–8 and in (39)).

### 5.2 Lemma 3.2 (p. 4, eq. (8)–(14)) — Closed-loop capture strategy on the oval

- **Statement**: If the evader adopts control angle ς (Fig. 3a), the pursuer captures E in or on the Cartesian oval C using ε = arcsin((r2(ς)/(a·r2(ς) + l))·sin ς) (eq. (8)), where r2(ς) = (al + δ cos ς − √((al + δ cos ς)² − (1 − a²)(δ² − l²)))/(1 − a²), ς ∈ [−π, π), with δ = |PE|, r1 = |PC|, r2 = |EC| for C ∈ C.
- **Proof approach** (5 steps, synthetic geometry): (i) straight-line trajectories form triangle △PCE, law of sines gives sin ς/sin ε = r1/r2 (eq. (9)); (ii) capture-time balance with radius l: (r1 − l)/vP = r2/vE ⟹ r1 = a·r2 + l, which is exactly the oval (eq. (10)); (iii) combine (9)–(10) into ε = arcsin((r2/(ar2 + l)) sin ς) (eq. (11)); (iv) law of cosines r1² = r2² + δ² − 2δr2 cos ς (eq. (12)), substituting (10) yields the quadratic (1 − a²)r2² − 2(al + δ cos ς)r2 + δ² − l² = 0 (eq. (13)) with two roots r2(ς) = (al + δ cos ς ± √((al + δ cos ς)² − (1 − a²)(δ² − l²)))/(1 − a²) (eq. (14)); (v) the "+" root is an *outer* Cartesian oval violating (10), the "−" root is the *inner* oval satisfying it — hence the minus branch in (8).
- **Decisive intermediate**: the time-balance identity r1 = a·r2 + l (eq. (10)) — simultaneously the oval definition and the capture condition.
- **Well-definedness (checked)**: since r2/(ar2 + l) < 1/a < 1 for l > 0, the arcsin argument never exceeds 1; ε is defined for every ς.
- **Robustness to direction changes (p. 4, Fig. 3b)**: if E turns (E → E1 → E2 → Q2), P successively reaches P1, P2, P3 under (8); the final capture point Q2 lies on the disc of radius l around P3, and the successive reachable-region boundaries C, C1, C2 are mutually tangent (C1 tangent to C at Q, C2 tangent to C1 at Q1); "The related proof can be found in Reference [36]" (Dutkevich & Petrosyan, SIAM J. Control 1972). *Proof gap*: the tangency/chaining argument is cited, not reproduced.
- **Locality/character**: state feedback in the evader's current input ς — consistent with the non-anticipative structure (pursuer reacts to current uE); analytic, global on the plane.
- **Physical meaning**: whatever direction E takes, P can make the pair meet on the precomputed oval; hence inside C, capture is guaranteed; outside, E reaches C's exterior safely.

### 5.3 Winning regions (p. 4–5, eq. (15)–(16))

- **PWR (eq. (15))**: WP = {(x1, x2, xE, t) ∈ R^6 × [t0, ts] | ∀uE(·) ∈ Ue, ∃u1(·) ∈ U1, u2(·) ∈ U2, tf ∈ [t0, ts], x(tf; x1⁰, x2⁰, xE⁰, u1, u2, uE, t0) ∈ C, ∀t ∈ [t0, tf], x(t; ...) ∉ D}.
- **EWR (eq. (16))**: WE = {(x1, x2, xE, t) ∈ R^6 × [t0, ts] | ∃uE(·) ∈ Ue, ∀u1(·) ∈ U1, u2(·) ∈ U2, tf ∈ [t0, ts], x(tf; ...) ∈ D, ∀t ∈ [t0, tf], x(t; ...) ∉ C}.
- "A barrier is used to separate these two regions. On the barrier, both the evader and the pursuers sides have strategies ensuring the opponent does not win" (p. 5). These are proper game-of-kind definitions with path constraints; no duality/existence theory is invoked.

### 5.4 Theorem 4.1 (p. 5, eq. (17)–(18)) — Barrier B1 of sub-game (P1, E) with lifeline L1

- **Statement**: Given P1's position x1, the barrier of the sub-game P1 vs. E with lifeline L1 is B1 = {xe(τ) = τ − n·m; ye(τ) = m·√(1 − n²)} (eq. (17)), where m = (1/a)(‖xT* − x1‖2 − l), n = (τ − x1)/(a‖xT* − x1‖2), and xT* = (τ, 0) is the optimal target point of E on L1 (τ = its abscissa). The barrier lies in the game area 0 ≤ ye ≤ d, and τ ranges over U = {R, if |y1| ≥ l; {τ | |τ − x1| ≥ √(l² − y1²), τ ∈ R}, if |y1| < l} (eq. (18)).
- **Assumptions**: a > 1; E assumed able to aim at a point xT = (τ, 0) on L1; straight-line optimal trajectories (Mayer cost + simple motion, asserted p. 3).
- **Proof approach** (6 steps, 1-D optimization + algebra):
  1. Rewrite the sub-game payoff as the terminal separation JP = ‖x1(tf) − xE(tf)‖2 (eq. (19)) ("the safety distance for E upon arrival"); value V = max_{uE} min_{u1} JP.
  2. If E runs the straight line to xT in the time ‖xT − xE‖2/vE, P1 covers a‖xT − xE‖2, so JP(τ) = ‖xT − x1‖2 − a‖xT − xE‖2 − l (eq. (20)) — the margin by which E's arrival separation exceeds the capture radius.
  3. E picks τ to maximize JP; the optimum xT* = (τ*, 0) is an extreme point, so dJP/dτ = 0 ⟹ (τ − x1)/‖xT* − x1‖2 = a(τ − xe)/‖xT* − xE‖2 (eq. (21)) — the aligning (Snell-like) condition tying P1, E, xT*.
  4. On the barrier, capture occurs exactly at arrival: JP(τ) = 0 ⟹ ‖xT* − x1‖2 − a‖xT* − xE‖2 − l = 0 (eq. (22)); hence ‖xT* − x1‖2 − l ≥ 0, which yields the admissible set U of eq. (18) (if |y1| < l the target point must lie outside P1's projection disc on L1).
  5. Set m = (1/a)(‖xT* − x1‖2 − l); then (22) reads ‖xT* − xE‖2 = m (eq. (23)), and (21) becomes τ − xe = (τ − x1)‖xT* − xE‖2/(a‖xT* − x1‖2) (eq. (24)); defining n = (τ − x1)/(a‖xT* − x1‖2) gives xe = τ − n·m (eq. (25)).
  6. Substituting (25) into (23): n²m² + ye² = m² ⟹ ye(τ) = m√(1 − n²) (eq. (26)); restricting 0 ≤ ye ≤ d places the barrier inside the play strip.
- **Decisive intermediate equation**: eq. (22) (JP = 0 on the barrier) combined with the FOC (21).
- **Feasibility (checked)**: |n| ≤ 1/a < 1 automatically since ‖xT* − x1‖2 ≥ |τ − x1|, so ye is real on U.
- **Meaning**: B1 is the locus of E positions from which, under optimal play of both, capture happens exactly when E arrives at its best target point on L1; it is the "indifference" surface of the game of kind for the (P1, L1) pair. Local in (xe, ye) for fixed x1; parametric-analytic in τ. In the 3-D state space with x1 = 0 fixed and y1 free, the barriers enclose, with the plane 0-xye, the PWR (Fig. 5); Fig. 4 is the y1 = 3 cross-section.
- **Companion barrier B2 (p. 5, eq. (27), proof omitted "as it is analogous")**: for the sub-game P1, E with lifeline L2: xe(τ) = τ − n·m, ye(τ) = −m√(1 − n²) + d, with m = (1/a)(‖xT* − xP'1‖2 − l), n = (τ − x1)/(a‖xT* − xP'1‖2), xP'1 = (x1, d − y1) — the mirror construction about y = d.

### 5.5 Vertical-escape boundaries and region-wise strategies (p. 5–6, eq. (28)–(32))

- **Vertical-escape test (eq. (28)–(29))**: for L1, t1 = ye/vE < (D1 − l)/vP with D1 = √((xe − x1)² + y1²); for L2, t2 = (d − ye)/vE < (D2 − l)/vP with D2 = √((xe − x1)² + (d − y1)²): E's straight vertical run is faster than P1's best interception (reach within l of the crossing point) minus capture radius.
- **Boundaries (eq. (30))**: B3: ye < (√((xe − x1)² + y1²) − l)/a for L1; B4: ye > −(√((xe − x1)² + (d − y1)²) − l)/a + d for L2. B3 ∩ B4 intersect at points s1, s2 (Fig. 4); inside "region 1" E escapes vertically ("E's actions need only focus on achieving its objective, without considering P1's strategy"), while P1 plays closed-loop toward E's target point on the lifeline (p. 5–6).
- **Two-player region classification (p. 6, Fig. 4)**: region 2 (enclosed by B1 and B2) = PWR, where "P1 can capture it either on or in the C1 by employing strategy (8)"; E's survival-maximizing move is along the vector P1E and the maximum survival time is ‖x1 − xE‖2/(vP − vE). Region 1 = vertical escape (escape time ye/vE or (d − ye)/vE). At s1, s2: escape vertically to the nearest lifeline. Regions 3, 4: E escapes; the optimal target point is "located at the minimum or maximum y-value on the Cartesian oval", and for the time-optimal objective both players use u1 = (xZ − x1)/‖xZ − x1‖2, uE = (xZ − xE)/‖xZ − xE‖2 (eq. (31)) toward xZ chosen by arg min_{xZ ∈ ((xZ1, xZ2) ∪ (xZ3, xZ4))} ‖xE − xZ‖2 (eq. (32)), where xZ1 = (xZ1, 0), xZ2 = (xZ2, 0), xZ3 = (xZ3, d), xZ4 = (xZ4, d) are the intersections of C1 with the two lifelines, satisfying √((x − x1)² + (y − y1)²) = a√((x − xe)² + (y − ye)²) + l (p. 6). No formal theorem is attached to this subsection — it is a region-map with strategy prescriptions derived from (17), (27), (30) and Lemma 3.2.

### 5.6 Theorem 5.1 (p. 6, eq. (33)) — Barriers of the two-pursuer one-evader two-lifeline game

- **Statement**: Given x1, x2, the barriers and boundaries of the 3-player game are (all within the game area):
  - B1: xe(τ) = τ − n1m1, ye(τ) = m1√(1 − n1²);
  - B2: xe(τ) = τ − n'1m'1, ye(τ) = −m'1√(1 − (n'1)²) + d;
  - B3: ye < (√((xe − x1)² + y1²) − l)/a;
  - B4: ye > −(√((xe − x1)² + (d − y1)²) − l)/a + d;
  - B5: xe(τ) = τ − n2m2, ye(τ) = m2√(1 − n2²);
  - B6: xe(τ) = τ − n'2m'2, ye(τ) = −m'2√(1 − (n'2)²) + d;
  - B7: ye < (√((xe − x2)² + y2²) − l)/a;
  - B8: ye > −(√((xe − x2)² + (d − y2)²) − l)/a + d,
  where mi = (1/a)(‖xT* − xPi‖2 − l), ni = (τ − xi)/(a‖xT* − xPi‖2), m'i = (1/a)(‖xT* − xP'i‖2 − l), n'i = (τ − xi)/(a‖xT* − xP'i‖2), xT* = (τ, 0), xPi = (xi, yi), xP'i = (xi, d − yi), i ∈ {1, 2}.
- **Proof approach** (decomposition + merge, 3 steps): (i) split the game into sub-game (a) P1 vs. E with two lifelines and sub-game (b) P2 vs. E with two lifelines; (ii) for (a) build B1, B2 by (17)/(27) and B3, B4 by (30); for (b) build B5, B6 "following Theorem 4.1" and B7, B8 by the (30) methodology; (iii) "merge these barriers and boundaries within the game area". *Proof gap*: no argument is given that the merged set is the exact boundary of the 3-player PWR/EWR (sufficiency of pairwise decomposition is asserted, not proven — e.g., two pursuers jointly could succeed where each sub-game alone fails, and conversely E might exploit sequential timing); regions 7–8 are indeed handled later by an ad hoc reachable-set test rather than by the merged barrier.
- **Notation inconsistency (as printed)**: xT* = (τ, 0) is stated for all of B1–B8, but B2/B6 target L2 at height d — by symmetry with (27) their target points should be (τ, d); also m'i, n'i in the printed n'i expression use xP'i in the denominator (‖xT* − xP'i‖2), consistent with (27).
- **Meaning**: the eight curves partition the strip into up to eight regions (Fig. 6, labels R1–R8); every piece is an analytic, closed-form function of the current pursuer/evader positions — a purely geometric state-feedback construction, global in the strip.

### 5.7 Region-wise strategies for the 3-player game (p. 6–7)

- Regions 1, 2, 3: E is captured by P1 or P2 "on or in the C1 or C2 by using strategy (8)".
- Regions 4, 5: E reaches the target area as fast as possible using strategy (31) (nearest reachable lifeline point).
- Region 6: E uses the vertical strategy to the nearest target area; the optimal point is Z = (xz, 0) if min(yz, d − yz) = yz and Z = (xz, d) if min(yz, d − yz) = d − yz (p. 7).
- Regions 7, 8: joint pursuit must be analyzed; build R by (7); "If R ∩ Ω_goal ≠ ∅, there exists a point T ∈ R ∩ Ω_goal, such that E can successfully reach the goal area. Furthermore, T is the time-optimal target point for E if and only if T satisfies ‖xE − xT‖2 = min_{x ∈ R ∩ Ω_goal} ‖xE − x‖2" (p. 7). Since the exact intersections of two Cartesian ovals with the lifelines are hard to obtain ("high degree of the equation"), the resultant-force method below is proposed instead.

### 5.8 Theorem 5.2 (p. 7, eq. (34)–(38)) — Resultant-force strategy

- **Statement**: From the resultant-force perspective, P1 and P2 adopt ui = (xE − xi)/‖xE − xi‖2, i = 1, 2 (eq. (34), pure pursuit); the direction of E's unit velocity is uE = (F_{k1} + F_{k2} + F1 + F2)/‖F_{k1} + F_{k2} + F1 + F2‖2 (eq. (35)), where the vector forces are F_{k1} = −F_{k1} (attraction toward L1, i.e., downward), F_{k2} = F_{k2} (upward toward L2), Fi = ui·Fi, with scalar magnitudes F_{k1} = 2/ye, F_{k2} = 2/(d − ye), Fi = 1/‖xi − xE‖2².
- **Assumptions**: the three players are "regarded as mass points" (Fig. 7); pursuers play (34).
- **Proof approach** (3 steps, line integration in a mechanics model): (i) each pursuer repels E with an inverse-square force Fi = 1/‖xi − xE‖2² along ui (pointing from pursuer to evader); (ii) for a lifeline, take an interval [x, x + ds] on L1 with attraction dF = ds/(s² + ye²), whose vertical component is dFy = ye·ds/(s² + ye²)^{3/2} (eq. (36)); integrating over the whole line gives F_{k1} = ∫_{−∞}^{+∞} ye(s² + ye²)^{−3/2} ds = 2/ye (eq. (37)) and similarly F_{k2} = ∫_{−∞}^{+∞} (d − ye)(s² + (d − ye)²)^{−3/2} ds = 2/(d − ye) (eq. (38)); (iii) by symmetry the horizontal components cancel, so the normalized resultant (35) is E's heading. (The integrals are correct: ∫ ye/(s² + ye²)^{3/2} ds = 2/ye.)
- **Decisive equations**: (37)–(38) — an infinite attracting line exerts a 2/(distance) vertical pull, balancing the two lifelines' pulls against the pursuers' inverse-square repulsions.
- **Character**: this is a *heuristic state-feedback strategy*, not a proven saddle-point solution — no optimality (value-function or Isaacs-side) argument is given; validity is supported by the reachable-region test of Sec. 5.7 and by simulation (Fig. 8: E = (1.2, 5.3) in region 7 reaches the target area keeping distance > l = 2 from both pursuers).
- **Extension to n pursuers (eq. (39))**: ui = (xE − xi)/‖xE − xi‖2, uE = (F_{k1} + F_{k2} + Σ_{i=1}^{n} Fi)/‖F_{k1} + F_{k2} + Σ_{i=1}^{n} Fi‖2, Fi = Fi·ui.
- **Remark 5.1 (p. 7)**: "In cases involving multiple terminal conditions, the classical Isaacs method cannot be applied. Instead, the resultant force approach can be utilized to derive strategies for both the evader and multiple pursuers", offering "a novel perspective from the field of mechanics".

**Summary of the two-lifeline setup and results**: strip 0 < ye < d between parallel lifelines; simple motion, a = vP/vE > 1, capture radius l; evader's guaranteed reachable region = intersection of two Cartesian ovals (7); per-pursuer barriers B1/B2 (L1/L2) are the parametric arcs (17)/(27) obtained from the terminal-payoff FOC (21) with JP = 0 (22); vertical-escape boundaries (30); 3-player barriers = pairwise merge (33); away from the barrier, region-wise strategies: capture by (8) in PWR, vertical run in region 1, nearest-oval-intersection sprint (31)–(32) elsewhere, and the resultant-force heading (35) in the mixed regions 7–8 and multi-pursuer games (39).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated**:
- Classical Isaacs RPE method cannot handle multiple terminal manifolds; hence the decomposition + geometric approach (Conclusion, p. 8; Remark 5.1, p. 7).
- Resultant-force strategy is a mechanics-inspired alternative, "providing players with more options" — i.e., a feasible strategy class, not a proven-optimal one (p. 7–8).
- Future work: "multiplayer differential games with obstacles and more complex environments" (p. 8).

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. **Hamiltonian typo (p. 3)**: H = Σ vP(λxi sin ϕi + λyi cos ϕi) + vE(λxe sin φ + λye cos φ) swaps sin/cos relative to dynamics (1); never used downstream, but the paper's only nod to the maximum principle is thus inconsistent as printed.
2. **Straight-line optimality asserted, not proven (p. 3)**: "the optimal trajectory of these players is represented as straight lines" — plausible for simple motion with Mayer cost but stated without derivation.
3. **Lemma 3.1 proof outsourced (p. 4)**: "As shown in a proof provided by [35]"; also the formal definition (6) omits the capture-avoidance path constraint stated in prose.
4. **Lemma 3.2 robustness step cited (p. 4)**: the tangency-chaining capture argument for direction changes is deferred to [36]; the paper does not re-derive it for a = 1 boundary cases.
5. **Theorem 5.1 merge lacks a sufficiency proof (p. 6)**: pairwise-decomposed barriers are merged without proving the merged set equals the true 3-player PWR/EWR boundary (joint-pursuit effects and sequential-timing effects on E's side are not analyzed); regions 7–8 are patched with a reachable-set test (R ∩ Ω_goal ≠ ∅) instead of a closed-form barrier — an implicit admission that the merged barrier is conservative/local.
6. **Notation inconsistency in Theorem 5.1 (p. 6, eq. (33))**: xT* = (τ, 0) printed uniformly, though B2/B6 concern L2 at y = d (should be (τ, d) by symmetry with (27)).
7. **Payoff under capture undefined (p. 3, eq. (4))**: J = min{ye(tf1), d − ye(tf2)} presumes E reaches a lifeline; if pursuers capture, tf1/tf2 do not exist and the value's meaning needs a convention (e.g., +∞) that is not given.
8. **Termination criteria leave gaps (p. 3)**: the two criteria do not cover, e.g., min{ye, d − ye} < 0 coinciding with min distance ≤ l, or exact equality cases.
9. **No value-function theory**: no existence/continuity/viscosity analysis of V (eq. (5)) and no proof that the barrier strategies constitute a saddle point on B1/B2 (the barrier property "both sides have strategies ensuring the opponent does not win" is asserted, p. 5).
10. **Resultant-force strategy (35) has no optimality guarantee**: forces are chosen ad hoc (inverse-square repulsion; unit-strength line integration); no invariance to force-strength rescaling is discussed (the heading depends on relative magnitudes of pursuer repulsions vs. lifeline attractions, whose relative scaling 1 vs. 2/d-units is arbitrary), and no failure-mode analysis (e.g., E guided toward a pursuer-poised stretch of lifeline) is provided beyond the single successful run of Fig. 8.
11. **Equal-speed case a = 1 treated only pictorially** (Fig. 2b, hyperbolas); all theorems assume a > 1.
12. **Figure parameter gaps**: Fig. 6's a and l, Fig. 8's pursuer positions, and all speeds are unreported; no statistical/quantitative performance evaluation anywhere.

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: P094 is the first systematic treatment of the *two-lifeline* pursuit-evasion differential game (two pursuers, one evader, strip between two parallel lifelines, nonzero capture radius), sitting at the intersection of the Isaacs lifeline lineage [7], [8], [13], [14], [23], [36] and the geometric reach-avoid/target-defense school [10]–[12], [29]–[32], [35]. Its reusable assets are: (i) the Cartesian-oval reachable region (7) generalizing the Apollonius circle to l-capture; (ii) the closed-loop oval-capture strategy (8); (iii) the parametric barrier formula (17)/(27) derived from a terminal-payoff FOC, and its 8-curve merge (33); and (iv) the mechanics-inspired resultant-force evasion strategy (35)/(39) for multi-terminal, multi-pursuer settings where Isaacs' method and HJI numerics become unwieldy. Cite it when surveying lifeline/barrier constructions, capture-radius-generalized Apollonius geometry, decomposition of multi-terminal games, or alternative (force-analogy) evasion policies.

**Suggested citation sentences**:
- "Extending the classical single-lifeline game of Isaacs, Xu et al. [P094] derived closed-form Cartesian-oval barriers for a two-pursuer one-evader game between two parallel lifelines and partitioned the play strip into pursuer- and evader-winning regions with region-wise optimal strategies."
- "For multi-terminal pursuit-evasion settings where retrograde path equations are intractable, the resultant-force strategy of [P094] — balancing inverse-square pursuer repulsions against 2/d-type lifeline attractions — offers a computable state-feedback evasion law, albeit without saddle-point optimality guarantees."
