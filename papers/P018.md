## 2. Research Background

Reach-avoid games model one team of agents that must enter a sequence of target regions while avoiding capture, with applications to air traffic control [1], path planning [2], and robot surveillance [3]. For **single-phase** reach-avoid games (one target region), geometric tools — Voronoi diagrams [14], Apollonius circles [5], [6], [15], Cartesian ovals [7], [16] — are established instruments for deciding the game of kind and building equilibrium strategies for players with simple motion. A parallel literature studies **two-phase** reach-avoid games (a.k.a. capture-the-flag games), where a player must sequentially reach a target/"flag" region and then a "safe"/"return" region: HJI numerical computations [17], [18], classical Isaacs-style analysis [19], point-target/point-safe special cases solved via key-point distances [20], and point-target/half-plane-safe via constrained nonlinear optimization [21] — but all of these treat **two-player** games. The paper positions itself as bringing the geometric-tool methodology of the single-phase literature into the two-phase, multiplayer setting, using the **ellipse** (the locus of points whose summed distance to two foci is constant) as the natural shape for a two-leg path.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem**: decide the game of kind (winning set / winning condition) for a two-phase multiplayer reach-avoid game in $\mathbb{R}^2$: a thief with simple motion must enter a target region $\mathcal{G}$ (steal a treasure) and then reach a safe region $\mathcal{S}$, while $N_D$ guarders attempt capture; guarder $D_i$ captures within radius $r_{D_i}$.

**Gap**:
- [authors' claim] "This paper aims to discuss the potential application of geometric tools in two-phase reach-avoid games, **addressing a gap in the existing literature**" (p. 1): existing two-phase studies [17]–[21] are all two-player; "[19] … analyzed the first and second phase separately in cases of zero and positive capture radius scenarios, **but no solution is provided for the overall game problem**" (p. 1) — this characterization of [19] is the authors' summary of the cited text (consistent with that paper's stated scope, but not independently verified here); [20] solves only a point-target/point-safe special case, and [21] links the optimal strategy to a constrained nonlinear optimization.
- [authors' claim, verified as internally consistent] The specific gaps the paper fills: (i) game of kind solvable *directly* by an ellipse test for multiplayer point-safe-region scenarios (Theorems 1–2); (ii) a cooperative two-guarder non-singleton-safe-region scenario solved by an ellipse test (Theorems 3–4); (iii) the concept of **dominance region** generalized from single-phase to two-phase games, with an explicit boundary construction (Definition 1, Theorem 5, Lemmas 1–2).
- [verified from cited text context] The convexity of the guarder-competition curve $\{\mathbf{x}: (\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_1}\|_2 - r_{D_1})/v_{D_1} = (\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_2}\|_2 - r_{D_2})/v_{D_2}\}$ is asserted as "can be proved to be convex [7]" (p. 3) — i.e., imported from the Cartesian-oval analysis of Yan et al. (Automatica 2022), not proved in this paper.
- Necessity of the dominance-region intersection test is explicitly left open: "The investigation into the necessity of the intersection condition in Theorem 5 will be pursued as a future endeavor" (p. 5).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Theory — point-safe-region game of kind (Thms. 1–2)**: the thief wins **iff** the target region intersects an open ellipse with foci at the thief's initial position and the safe point, and major axis $v_T \times$(fastest guarder's arrival time at the safe point). Theorem 1 holds for arbitrary guarder speeds but zero capture radii; Theorem 2 for nonzero capture radii but guarders no slower than the thief — a complementary pair. A logarithmic-spiral "bypassing path" construction handles slower guarders (p. 3).
2. **Theory — cooperative two-guarder defense (Thms. 3–4)**: when neither guarder alone can defend, a minimax characterization via the value functions $h_i$ (eq. (7)) plus Danskin's theorem; under a structural "uniform minimizer" assumption, the game of kind again reduces to a single ellipse intersection test at the dividing point $\mathbf{x}_1$ (eq. (8)).
3. **Concept + algorithm — two-phase dominance region (Def. 1, Thm. 5, Lemmas 1–2)**: the region of points the thief can pass through and still safely reach $\mathcal{S}$; shown to equal (outside $\mathcal{S}$) a union of ellipses with one focus fixed at the thief and the other sliding along $\partial\mathcal{S}$ (eq. (9)); its boundary is the **envelope** of this one-parameter ellipse family, with an explicit closed-form branch construction $\mathbf{x}_\pm(s)$, $\theta_\pm(s)$, $l_\pm(s)$ (eqs. (10)–(14)) — a constructive geometric algorithm.
4. **Numerical illustration (Example 1, Fig. 5)**: dominance boundary for a half-plane safe region with two guarders of equal speed and capture radius 0.1, exhibiting a discontinuity and an ellipse arc — validates the envelope construction; no other simulation, no HJI cross-comparison.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.0 Setup: dynamics, information, termination (Sec. II, pp. 1–2)

- **Dynamics (eq. (1), p. 1)**: first-order simple motion in $\mathbb{R}^2$,
  $\dot{\mathbf{x}}_T = v_T\mathbf{u}_T,\quad \dot{\mathbf{x}}_{D_i} = v_{D_i}\mathbf{u}_{D_i},\ i=1,\dots,N_D$, with $\|\mathbf{u}_T\|_2 \le 1$, $\|\mathbf{u}_{D_i}\|_2 \le 1$; $\mathbf{x}_T$, $\mathbf{x}_{D_i}$ positions; speeds $v_T$, $v_{D_i}$.
- **State/information**: state $X = [\mathbf{x}_T^\top, \mathbf{x}_{D_1}^\top, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{D_{N_D}}^\top]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{2(N_D+1)}$, initial state $X_0$. "It is assumed that the state is fully observable to all players. Each player can make decisions based on all the available state information up to the current moment. However, they do not have access to the current control of their opponents" (p. 2) — i.e., nonanticipative (delayed-opponent-input) information structure.
- **Capture**: thief captured by $D_i$ iff $\|\mathbf{x}_T - \mathbf{x}_{D_i}\|_2 \le r_{D_i}$ (p. 2).
- **Winning condition (unnumbered display, p. 2)**: the thief wins iff $\exists t_f \in \mathbb{R}$: $\mathbf{x}_T(t_f) \in \mathcal{S}$; $\exists t_c \in [0,t_f]$, $\mathbf{x}_T(t_c) \in \mathcal{G}$; $\forall t\in[0,t_f]$, $\min_{1\le i\le N_D}\|\mathbf{x}_T(t)-\mathbf{x}_{D_i}(t)\|_2 - r_{D_i} > 0$. Otherwise the guarders win. Note the **two-phase structure is encoded in the winning predicate**: pass through $\mathcal{G}$ at some $t_c$, then reach $\mathcal{S}$, never captured in between.
- **Ellipse tool (eq. (4), p. 2)**: $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,d) = \{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^2 \mid \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_1\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_2\|_2 < d\}$ — open interior of the ellipse with **foci** $\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2$ and **major axis length** $d$; empty if $\|\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_2\|_2 \ge d$ (printed: "is a empty set"). Physical meaning: an ellipse is exactly the set of points from which the **two-leg path** (go to focus 1, then to focus 2) costs less than $d$ of thief travel time — the natural object for a two-phase (steal-then-return) objective, playing the role Apollonius circles play in single-phase games (authors' own analogy, p. 3).

### 5.1 Theorem 1 (p. 2, uses eqs. (2)–(5)) — point safe region, zero capture radii, any guarder speeds

- **Statement**: Assume $\mathcal{S} = \{\mathbf{x}_S\}$, $\mathcal{G}$ is a **connected non-singleton** set, and $r_{D_i} = 0$ for all $i$. Let $t_m = \min_{1\le i\le N_D}\|\mathbf{x}_S - \mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}\|_2 / v_{D_i}$ (eq. (2), the fastest guarder's arrival time at $\mathbf{x}_S$). The thief can win **iff** $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T, \mathbf{x}_S, v_T t_m) \cap \mathcal{G} \neq \emptyset$ (eq. (5)).
- **Decisive intermediate steps**: for a steal point $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}$ the thief's two-leg time is $t(\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_S) = (\|\mathbf{x}_S-\mathbf{x}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{x}\|_2)/v_T$ (unnumbered, p. 2); "thief can bring the treasure to $\mathbf{x}_S$ before $t_m$ iff there is $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}$ with $t(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_S) < t_m$", equivalently
  $\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}} \|\mathbf{x}_S-\mathbf{x}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{x}\|_2 < v_T t_m$ (eq. (3)),
  which is exactly the set statement (5). Analytic, global, closed-form (an intersection test, no optimization needed beyond the inf over $\mathcal{G}$).
- **Proof approach**: Necessity — if the intersection is empty, then for any thief path through some $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}$, arrival at $\mathbf{x}_S$ takes $\ge (\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_S\|)/v_T \ge t_m$, so some guarder reaches $\mathbf{x}_S$ no later and the thief is caught at (or before) $\mathbf{x}_S$ (strict winning requires $>$, so ties lose). Sufficiency — pick $\mathbf{x}_g \in \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_S,v_T t_m)\cap\mathcal{G}$; the two-segment trajectory $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$ with $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T(t_1) = \mathbf{x}_g$, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T(t_2) = \mathbf{x}_S$, $t_1 = \|\mathbf{x}_g-\mathbf{x}_0^T\|_2/v_T$, $t_2 = t_1 + \|\mathbf{x}_g-\mathbf{x}_S\|_2/v_T < t_m$ wins "sufficiently close" to $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$: (a) guarders with $v_{D_i} \ge v_T$ cannot intercept, because interception at a point of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$ would let that guarder then follow $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$ and reach $\mathbf{x}_S$ before the thief, contradicting the definition of $t_m$; (b) guarders slower than the thief ($v_{D_i} < v_T$, index set $\mathcal{N}_s$, $\bar{v}_s = \max_{i\in\mathcal{N}_s} v_{D_i}$) are **bypassed** on a logarithmic spiral.
- **Bypassing path construction (p. 3, Fig. 3)**: triggered when the thief's distance to slow guarder $D_s$ drops below an adjustable parameter $\delta$ at time $t_p$; in polar coordinates centered at $\mathbf{x}_{D_s}(t_p)$, $r(\theta) = \delta e^{k_p(\theta-\theta_0)}$ with $\theta_0$ the direction angle of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T(t_p)-\mathbf{x}_{D_s}(t_p)$ and $k_p = \bar{v}_s/\sqrt{v_T^2-\bar{v}_s^2}$ (clockwise) or $k_p = -\bar{v}_s/\sqrt{v_T^2-\bar{v}_s^2}$ (counterclockwise). Key verified identity: $r$ satisfies $\tfrac{1}{\bar{v}_s}\tfrac{dr}{d\theta} = \tfrac{1}{v_T}\sqrt{r^2 + (\tfrac{dr}{d\theta})^2}$, so the thief's time to reach spiral angle $\theta$ is
  $\tfrac{1}{v_T}\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\sqrt{r^2 + (\tfrac{dr}{d\theta})^2}\, d\theta = \tfrac{1}{\bar{v}_s}\big|\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\tfrac{dr}{d\theta}d\theta\big| = \tfrac{r(\theta)-r(\theta_0)}{\bar{v}_s} \le \tfrac{r(\theta)}{v_{D_s}}$,
  i.e., the thief reaches any spiral point before $D_s$ can (guarder needs $\ge r(\theta)/v_{D_s}$ from distance $r(\theta)$). By shrinking $\delta$, bypassing time is made arbitrarily small; nested bypasses use a smaller $\delta$ than the current one; the thief "will only need to bypass each slower guarder at most once on each straight line of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$" if all $\delta$'s are sufficiently small (p. 3). In the special case of a guarder near $\mathbf{x}_g$, the thief picks the bypass direction whose path still intersects $\mathcal{G}$ — this is where **connected + non-singleton** $\mathcal{G}$ is used.
- **Physical meaning**: the thief's winning set is decided by whether the "treasure trip" (two legs through a steal point) fits inside the guarders' one-leg deadline to the safe point; the ellipse is the iso-cost surface of the two-leg trip.
- **Extensibility**: template for all later results; the ellipse test requires only checking $\mathcal{G}$ against one explicitly given region (graphically decidable).
- **proof gap (minor, p. 2–3)**: (i) sufficiency claims the thief "can reach $\mathbf{x}_S$ before $t_m$" via a trajectory "sufficiently close" to $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$, but no explicit bound ties total bypass delay to $t_m - t_2$ (the margin is positive but the $\delta$-shrinking argument is qualitative); (ii) the claim of at-most-one bypass per straight segment is asserted, not proved; (iii) the necessity argument uses the camping strategy (fastest guarder heads to $\mathbf{x}_S$) and is correct, but ties ($=$) are only handled implicitly through the strict winning predicate.

### 5.2 Theorem 2 (p. 3, eq. (6)) — point safe region, nonzero capture radii, guarders no slower

- **Shorthand (p. 3)**: $\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}, X) = \min_{1\le i\le N_D} \dfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}}{v_{D_i}}$ — the earliest time any guarder can get within capture range of $\mathbf{x}$ from state $X$.
- **Statement**: Assume $\mathcal{S} = \{\mathbf{x}_S\}$ and $v_{D_i} \ge v_T$ for all $i$. The thief wins **iff**
  $\mathcal{E}\big(\mathbf{x}_0^T, \mathbf{x}_S, v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S, X_0)\big) \cap \mathcal{G} \neq \emptyset$ (eq. (6)).
  No connectivity/interior assumption on $\mathcal{G}$ is needed here (no bypassing required).
- **Proof approach**: necessity as in Theorem 1 (camping at $\mathbf{x}_S$: guarder needs $\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0)$ to get within capture range of $\mathbf{x}_S$; any thief trip through $\mathcal{G}$ takes $\ge \tilde{t}$, so it is captured on arrival). Sufficiency: travel $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$; if some $D_i$ could capture at $t_c < \tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0)$ (i.e. $\|\mathbf{x}_{D_i}(t_c) - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T(t_c)\|_2 \le r_{D_i}$), then by first reaching the $r_{D_i}$-neighborhood of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T(t_c)$ and then "following a trajectory parallel to $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$" (legal since $v_{D_i} \ge v_T$), $D_i$ would arrive within $r_{D_i}$ of $\mathbf{x}_S$ before $\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0)$, giving $v_{D_i}\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0) > \|\mathbf{x}_S - \mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}$ — contradicting the definition of $\tilde{t}$ (which implies $v_{D_i}\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0) \le \|\mathbf{x}_S-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}$ for every $i$).
- **Local/global, analytic**: global, analytic, closed-form; reduces the game of kind to a set-intersection check.
- **proof gap (minor, p. 3)**: the parallel-shadowing argument yields arrival within $r_{D_i}$ of $\mathbf{x}_S$ at time $\le \tilde{t}$; the strict contradiction needs either $v_{D_i} > v_T$, a non-collinear (bent) thief path forcing the guarder's path to be strictly longer than the straight-line distance, or a tie-breaking convention; the degenerate equality case is not discussed.
- **Extensibility**: the shadowing ("parallel trajectory") lemma is reused verbatim in Theorems 3–5 and Lemma 1; it is the workhorse that converts point-target ellipse tests into region-defense statements.

### 5.3 Section IV setup (p. 3) — two guarders, non-singleton safe region

$N_D = 2$; $\mathcal{S}$ connected, closed, convex with boundary $\partial\mathcal{S}$; $v_{D_i} \ge v_T$. The curve
$\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^2 \mid \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_1}\|_2 - r_{D_1}}{v_{D_1}} = \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_2}\|_2 - r_{D_2}}{v_{D_2}}\}$
(a weighted Apollonius-type bisector with capture radii; "can be proved to be convex [7]") intersects $\partial\mathcal{S}$ at two points $\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2$ (initial-state assumption); segment $[\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2]$ splits $\mathcal{S}$ into convex $\mathcal{S}_1$ (near $\mathbf{x}_0^{D_1}$) and $\mathcal{S}_2$, so $D_i$ reaches $\partial\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}_i$ faster than the other guarder.
- **Value functions (eq. (7), p. 3)**: $h_i: \mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^2\times\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$,
  $h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_{D_i}) = \inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}} \dfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{z}\|_2}{v_T} - \dfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}}{v_{D_i}}$,
  i.e., (thief's two-phase time-to-$\mathbf{x}$ via a steal point of $\mathcal{G}$, already en route from $\mathbf{x}_T$) minus (guarder $i$'s time-to-capture-range of $\mathbf{x}$). If $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) < 0$ for $i = 1,2$, **neither guarder alone** can defend (the cooperative case studied here).
- **Assumption 1 (p. 4)**: there exists a **unique** point $\mathbf{x}^*$ such that $h_i(\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_{D_i}) = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_{D_i})$, for all $\mathbf{x}_T$, all $\mathbf{x}_{D_i}$, $i=1,2$ — a uniform (in the players' positions) unique minimizer over each sub-region; strong structural assumption, existence not discussed.

### 5.4 Theorem 3 (p. 4) — minimax characterization of the cooperative game of kind

- **Statement**: If $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) < 0$ for $i = 1,2$ and Assumption 1 holds, the thief wins **iff** $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\max_i h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) < 0$; and this condition **equals** "$\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) < 0$ for $i = 1$ or $2$".
- **Proof approach**: (a) *Minimax reduction*: in $\mathcal{S}_1$ the bisector inequality gives $h_1 > h_2$, so $\max_i h_i = h_1$ on $\mathcal{S}_1$ and $= h_2$ on $\mathcal{S}_2$; hence $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\max_i h_i = \min_i \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i$ — the max over guarders is eliminated by the territorial split. (b) *Sufficiency*: $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}\max_i h_i < 0$ yields $\mathbf{x}_S \in \mathcal{S}$ with $h_i(\mathbf{x}_S,\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) < 0$ for **both** $i$; since $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}} h_i < 0$ for each $i$ means $\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}(\|\mathbf{x}_S-\mathbf{z}\|+\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{z}\|)/v_T < \tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0)$, Theorem 2 delivers a winning path to $\mathbf{x}_S$. (c) *Necessity* — the decisive computation: if $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) \ge 0$, guarder $D_i$ plays "move towards $\mathbf{x}_i^*(\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_{D_i})$", the (unique, Assumption 1) minimizer. By **Danskin's theorem [23]**,
  $\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}_T}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i = \tfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial\mathbf{x}_T}(\mathbf{x}_i^*,\cdot)$ and $\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{D_i}}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i = \tfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{D_i}}(\mathbf{x}_i^*,\cdot)$,
  and along the play
  $\tfrac{d}{dt}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_{D_i}) = v_{D_i}\mathbf{u}_{D_i}^\top\tfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{D_i}} + v_T\mathbf{u}_T^\top\tfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial\mathbf{x}_T} = 1 + \tfrac{d}{dt}\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}\tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}^*-\mathbf{z}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{z}\|_2}{v_T} \ge 0$
  no matter what the thief does: the guarder's approach term contributes $+1$ (moving towards $\mathbf{x}_i^*$ reduces the distance term at rate $v_{D_i}\cdot 1/v_{D_i}$) and the thief's term is $\ge -1$ (it can shorten $\inf_{\mathbf{z}}\|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{z}\|$ at rate at most $v_T$). So the barrier value $\min_{\mathcal{S}_i} h_i$ is non-decreasing and stays $\ge 0$; when the thief arrives in $\mathcal{G}$,
  $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i}\Big[\tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{x}\|_2}{v_T} - \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}}{v_{D_i}}\Big] = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_{D_i}) \ge 0$,
  and "according to the result of single-phase reach-avoid game [24]" (Fu & Liu, Automatica 2023 — HJI justification of the geometric solution with faster defenders and a convex target area), $D_i$ prevents the thief from reaching $\mathcal{S}_i$; since $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{S}_1\cup\mathcal{S}_2$, the thief loses.
- **Analytic/global**: global condition, analytic proof; the barrier monotonicity $d\,\min h_i/dt \ge 0$ is the decisive inequality (unnumbered, p. 4).
- **proof gap (imprecision, p. 4)**: the printed **equality** $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i}[\|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{x}\|_2/v_T - (\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i})/v_{D_i}]$ at the moment the thief arrives in $\mathcal{G}$ requires $\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|+\|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{z}\|) = \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_T\|$, which holds only if the segment $[\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}]$ meets $\mathcal{G}$; the triangle inequality gives only "$\ge$", which however suffices for the argument (if the direct single-phase value is $\ge 0$ then $h_i \ge 0$), so the gap is cosmetic. Also, Danskin's application tacitly assumes the needed compactness/attainment of $\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}$ (no compactness of $\mathcal{G}$ is stated anywhere).
- **Physical meaning**: each guarder is the "owner" of the sub-region $\mathcal{S}_i$ it can defend alone; the cooperative game of kind decouples into a min over territories of per-guarder barrier values.

### 5.5 Theorem 4 (p. 4, eq. (8)) — ellipse test at the dividing point $\mathbf{x}_1$

- **Auxiliary function (p. 4)**: $f_i: \mathcal{S}_i\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_2}{v_T} - \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}}{v_{D_i}}$ — single-phase (already-stolen-at-$\mathbf{z}$) safety margin of the thief against $D_i$.
- **Statement**: Assume Assumption 1 holds. If for all $i \in \{1,2\}$ and all $\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}$, $f_i(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{z}) = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} f_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})$ (i.e., the dividing point $\mathbf{x}_1$ is a **uniform minimizer** of the single-phase margin over both sub-regions), then the thief wins **iff**
  $\mathcal{E}\big(\mathbf{x}_0^T, \mathbf{x}_1, v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_1, X_0)\big) \cap \mathcal{G} \neq \emptyset$ (eq. (8)).
- **Decisive intermediate equations (p. 4)**: at $\mathbf{x}_1$, by construction of the bisector curve, $\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_1,X_0) = \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_1}\|_2 - r_{D_1}}{v_{D_1}} = \tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_2}\|_2 - r_{D_2}}{v_{D_2}}$. Using the uniform-minimizer property to **swap min and inf**,
  $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}) = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i}\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}\Big[\tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{z}\|_2}{v_T} + f_i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\Big] = \inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}\Big[\tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{z}\|_2}{v_T} + f_i(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{z})\Big] = \inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}\tfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{z}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_1\|_2}{v_T} - \tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_1,X_0)$,
  so $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i < 0$ (either $i$) $\iff \inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}(\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{z}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_1\|_2) < v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_1,X_0)$, which is exactly (8); the conclusion then follows from Theorem 3.
- **Physical meaning**: "under certain conditions, the point $\mathbf{x}_1$ is a **critical defense position**" (p. 4–5) — the weak point of the two-guarder defense is where their territorial responsibilities meet; the whole game of kind is decided by the geometric relation between $\mathcal{G}$ and the single ellipse $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_1,v_T\tilde{t}_1)$ (Fig. 4 illustrates the losing case, no intersection). Extensibility: the min–inf swap technique converts any cooperative-defense problem with a uniform-minimizer "hinge" into an ellipse test; how restrictive the uniform-minimizer hypothesis is in practice is not discussed.
- **proof gap (minor, p. 4)**: the swap $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i}\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}} = \inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i}$ requires $\mathbf{x}_1 \in \mathcal{S}_i$ (or attainment on $\bar{\mathcal{S}}_i$); $\mathbf{x}_1$ lies on the common boundary $\bar{\mathcal{S}}_1 \cap \bar{\mathcal{S}}_2$, and the closed/open status of $\mathcal{S}_i$ is not specified.

### 5.6 Definition 1 + Theorem 5 (p. 5) — thief's dominance region; sufficient winning condition

- **Definition 1 (p. 5)**: $\mathcal{D}(X;\mathcal{S}) = \{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^2 \mid \exists \mathbf{x}_S\in\mathcal{S},\ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_T\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_S\|_2 < v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S, X)\}$ — the set of points the thief (currently at $\mathbf{x}_T$) can pass through and then reach the safe region without capture, using an open-loop strategy. When $\mathcal{S}$ is a singleton, $\mathcal{D}(X;\mathcal{S}) = \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}_S,v_T t_m)$ (the ellipse of Theorem 1); for non-singleton $\mathcal{S}$ it is **not** an ellipse.
- **Theorem 5 (p. 5)**: the thief wins **if** $\mathcal{D}(X_0;\mathcal{S}) \cap \mathcal{G} \neq \emptyset$. Proof: existence of $\mathbf{x}_g\in\mathcal{G}$, $\mathbf{x}_S\in\mathcal{S}$ with $\|\mathbf{x}_g-\mathbf{x}_0^T\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}_g-\mathbf{x}_S\|_2 < v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0)$; then, per the proofs of Theorems 1–2, the two-segment trajectory $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$ wins. **Sufficiency only** — "The investigation into the necessity of the intersection condition in Theorem 5 will be pursued as a future endeavor" (p. 5). Note the asymmetry with Theorems 1–2 (which are iff): for general $\mathcal{S}$ the thief might win while touching $\mathcal{D}$ only outside $\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{D}$-points... (e.g., paths whose steal point and safe point are not jointly "visible" in the ellipse sense), so the gap is real, not cosmetic.

### 5.7 Lemma 1 (p. 5, eq. (9)) — dominance region as a union of ellipses

- **Statement**: $\mathcal{D}(X;\mathcal{S}) \setminus \mathcal{S} = \bigcup_{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathcal{S}} \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_T, \mathbf{x}, v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x},X)) \setminus \mathcal{S}$ (eq. (9)).
- **Proof approach**: "$\supseteq$" — each ellipse is inside $\mathcal{D}$ (its foci witness a feasible pass-through then safe arrival). "$\subseteq$" — if $\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}$, there is $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}$ with $\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_T\|_2 + \|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\|_2 < v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x},X)$; by the Theorem 2 mechanism the thief reaches $\mathbf{x}$ through $\mathbf{z}$ un-captured; letting $\mathbf{y}\in\partial\mathcal{S}$ be the intersection of $\partial\mathcal{S}$ with the segment $[\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}]$, the thief also reaches $\mathbf{y}$ through $\mathbf{z}$ un-captured, hence $\mathbf{z} \in \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{y},v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{y},X))$.
- **proof gap (implicit assumption, p. 5)**: the argument presupposes the segment $[\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}]$ crosses $\partial\mathcal{S}$ in a single point (convexity of $\mathcal{S}$); Section V does not restate any convexity assumption on $\mathcal{S}$ (Example 1 uses a half-plane, so the construction is fine there). Also the claim "the thief can also reach $\mathbf{y}$ … without being captured" relies on the un-captured-until-$\mathbf{x}$ property along the same schedule (any capture at the earlier point $\mathbf{y}$ would, by the shadowing argument, imply capture at $\mathbf{x}$) — correct but compressed to one sentence.
- **Physical meaning**: the two-phase dominance region is exactly the union of single-phase-style ellipses whose moving focus scans the safe-region boundary — the bridge that lets single-phase ellipse machinery apply to two-phase games.

### 5.8 Lemma 2 + explicit construction (p. 5–6, eqs. (10)–(14)) — dominance boundary as the envelope of the ellipse family

- **Terminology (p. 5)**: dominance boundary $\mathcal{BD}(X_0) = \partial\mathcal{D}(X_0;\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathcal{S}$ (only the part outside $\mathcal{S}$). Parametrize $\partial\mathcal{S}$ by $\mathbf{x}_s: \mathcal{I}_\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^2$, piecewise $C^1$; define $\tilde{t}_s(s) = \tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_s(s),X_0)$ (piecewise $C^1$); one-sided derivatives $\mathbf{v}_s^+(s), \mathbf{v}_s^-(s)$ of $\mathbf{x}_s$ and $\tilde{t}_s^+(s), \tilde{t}_s^-(s)$.
- **Lemma 2 (p. 5)**: for each $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{BD}(X_0)$ there exists $s\in\mathcal{I}_\mathcal{S}$ with $\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_s(s),v_T\tilde{t}_s(s))$ and
  $\dfrac{(\mathbf{x}_s(s)-\mathbf{x})^\top}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s(s)\|_2}\mathbf{v}_s^+(s) \ge v_T\tilde{t}_s^+(s)$ (eq. (10)), \quad
  $\dfrac{(\mathbf{x}_s(s)-\mathbf{x})^\top}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s(s)\|_2}\mathbf{v}_s^-(s) \le v_T\tilde{t}_s^-(s)$ (eq. (11)).
  Proof: otherwise $\mathbf{x}$ would be interior to some $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_s(s'),v_T\tilde{t}_s(s')) \subset \mathcal{D}(X_0;\mathcal{S})$; on the boundary (eq. (12)): $\|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{x}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s(s)\|_2 = v_T\tilde{t}_s(s)$; the function $h(s') = \|\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{x}\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s(s')\|_2 - v_T\tilde{t}_s(s')$ attains a local minimum at $s$ (since $\mathbf{x}\notin\mathcal{E}(\cdot,s')$ nearby, $h \ge 0 = h(s)$), and (10)–(11) follow from the first-order optimality condition. Geometric meaning: the component of the boundary-point's velocity along the sight line $\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}_s(s)$ must keep pace with $v_T \times$ the growth rate of the interception time — the focus must "outrun" the shrinking ellipse for $\mathbf{x}$ to stay on the envelope.
- **Tangency/envelope equation (eq. (13), p. 5)**: when $\mathbf{x}_s$ and $\tilde{t}_s$ are both differentiable at $s$,
  $\dfrac{(\mathbf{x}_s(s)-\mathbf{x})^\top}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s(s)\|_2}\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}_s(s)}{ds} = v_T\dfrac{d\tilde{t}_s(s)}{ds}$ — precisely the envelope condition $\partial_s\big[\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^T\|_2 + \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_s(s)\|_2 - v_T\tilde{t}_s(s)\big] = 0$: the dominance boundary is the **envelope of the one-parameter ellipse family** $\partial\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_s(s),v_T\tilde{t}_s(s))$.
- **Explicit closed-form solution (eq. (14), p. 5–6)**: solving (12)–(13) simultaneously with $\alpha(s)$ the direction angle of $d\mathbf{x}_s/ds$:
  $\mathbf{x}_\pm(s) = \mathbf{x}_s(s) - l_\pm(s)\,\mathbf{e}_{\theta_\pm(s)}$, where $\mathbf{e}_{\theta_\pm(s)} = [\cos\theta_\pm(s);\ \sin\theta_\pm(s)]$,
  $\theta_\pm(s) = \alpha(s) \pm \arccos\Big(v_T\dfrac{d\tilde{t}_s(s)}{ds} \Big/ \Big\|\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}_s(s)}{ds}\Big\|_2\Big)$,
  $l_\pm(s) = \dfrac{v_T^2\tilde{t}_s^2(s) - \|\mathbf{x}_0^T - \mathbf{x}_s(s)\|_2^2}{2\big((\mathbf{x}_0^T-\mathbf{x}_s(s))^\top\mathbf{e}_{\theta_\pm(s)} + v_T\tilde{t}_s(s)\big)}$.
  (Verified by substitution: writing $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_s - l\mathbf{e}_\theta$ and squaring $\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0^T\| = v_T\tilde{t}_s - l$ recovers exactly this quadratic-formula expression; the tangency equation (13) gives $\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_s\|\cos(\theta-\alpha) = v_T\dot{\tilde{t}}_s$, hence the $\arccos$.)
- **Branch selection and exceptional cases (p. 5–6)**: at most two solutions; a boundary point must additionally satisfy the **second-order condition** $\tfrac{d^2h}{ds^2} > 0$ ($h$ as in Lemma 2's proof). When $s$ is an endpoint of $\mathcal{I}_\mathcal{S}$ or a nondifferentiability point of $\mathbf{x}_s$ or $\tilde{t}_s$, the portions of $\partial\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_s(s),v_T\tilde{t}_s(s))$ where (10)–(11) hold may also belong to $\mathcal{BD}(X_0)$.
- **Analytic/constructive, local characterization**: analytic per-parameter; global assembly (which branch, where jumps occur) is handled case-by-case — see Example 1. Not stated: the real-solvability requirement $|v_T\, d\tilde{t}_s/ds| \le \|d\mathbf{x}_s/ds\|_2$ for the $\arccos$ (implicit in the derivation; failure corresponds to the endpoint/nondifferentiability clauses).
- **Extensibility**: this is the paper's reusable "geometric tool" output — a ruler-and-compass-grade algorithm (per boundary parameter, compute two candidate points, filter by the second-order condition) for two-phase dominance boundaries; directly transferable to any simple-motion two-phase game with piecewise-smooth $\partial\mathcal{S}$.

### 5.9 Example 1 (p. 6, Fig. 5) — numerical illustration of the boundary construction

$\partial\mathcal{S}$: straight line $\mathbf{x}_s(s) = [s, 0]^\top$, $\mathcal{S}$ the half-plane above it; $\mathbf{x}_0^T = [0,-2]^\top$; two guarders $\mathbf{x}_0^{D_1} = [-2,-2]^\top$, $\mathbf{x}_0^{D_2} = [1.5,-2.2]^\top$; $r_{D_1} = r_{D_2} = 0.1$; all speeds $= 1$. Results as printed: $\mathcal{BD}(X_0)$ consists of (i) the branch $\mathbf{x}_+(s)$ (red), **discontinuous at $s_c = 0.13$**, and (ii) an **ellipse arc** of $\partial\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T, \mathbf{x}_s(s_c), v_T\tilde{t}_s(s_c))$ (black) bridging the jump; the branch $\mathbf{x}_-(s)$ (blue dashed) lies inside $\mathcal{S}$ and is excluded. The dominance region outside $\mathcal{S}$ is bounded by $\mathcal{BD}(X_0)$ and $\partial\mathcal{S}$; a family of dotted ellipses $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_s(s),v_T\tilde{t}_s(s))$ is overlaid and "these ellipses are tangent to the curve $\mathbf{x}_+(s)$" — visual confirmation of the envelope property. Note that here $\tilde{t}_s(s) = \min_i(\|\mathbf{x}_s(s)-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}\|_2 - 0.1)$ is continuous but only piecewise smooth (its kink occurs where the two guarders' weighted distances are equal), and the printed discontinuity of the $\mathbf{x}_+$ branch occurs at $s_c = 0.13$, where the second-order condition fails and the boundary switches to an ellipse arc of the family at the jump parameter.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated**:
1. Theorem 5 is **sufficient only**; necessity of $\mathcal{D}(X_0;\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{G}\neq\emptyset$ is explicitly deferred to future work (p. 5). Consequently the dominance-region test is conservative in general.
2. Only the **game of kind** is treated; no game-of-degree, no equilibrium strategies/values (p. 2: "This paper focus on the game of kind"; conclusion, p. 6).
3. The point-safe-region ellipse test with slower guarders (Theorem 1) requires zero capture radii; the nonzero-radii test (Theorem 2) requires $v_{D_i} \ge v_T$ — no result covers slower guarders **with** nonzero radii (pp. 2–3).
4. The cooperative two-guarder result needs strong structure: the bisector curve must hit $\partial\mathcal{S}$ at exactly two points (p. 3), Assumption 1 (unique uniform minimizer, p. 4), and Theorem 4's uniform-minimizer property of $\mathbf{x}_1$ (p. 4); how restrictive these are, and existence, are not discussed. Future work: "further application of dominance regions in general two-phase reach-avoid games" (p. 6).

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
5. Section-numbering typo in the introduction: the dominance-region section is announced as "Section VI" (should be V) and the conclusion as "Section VI" (p. 1). Other printed slips: "path planing" (p. 1), "This paper focus on" (p. 2), "is a empty set" (p. 2), "must cooperative with each other" (p. 3), "There is two guarders" and "to illustration the results" (p. 6).
6. Theorem 3, necessity (p. 4): the printed equality $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i} h_i = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}_i}[\|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{x}\|_2/v_T - (\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i})/v_{D_i}]$ at thief arrival in $\mathcal{G}$ holds only if the segment $[\mathbf{x}_T,\mathbf{x}]$ intersects $\mathcal{G}$; only "$\ge$" (triangle inequality) is justified, which happens to suffice — an imprecision rather than an error.
7. Theorem 2 sufficiency (p. 3): the shadowing argument gives arrival within $r_{D_i}$ of $\mathbf{x}_S$ at time $\le \tilde{t}$, so the strict contradiction ($v_{D_i}\tilde{t} > \|\mathbf{x}_S-\mathbf{x}_0^{D_i}\|_2 - r_{D_i}$) silently requires non-degeneracy (bent path or $v_{D_i} > v_T$); the collinear/equality corner case is unaddressed.
8. Theorem 1 sufficiency (pp. 2–3): the $\delta$-bypassing argument is qualitative — no explicit bound on cumulative bypass delay versus the winning margin $t_m - t_2$, and "at most once per straight segment" is asserted; also the strategy is feedback-flavored (bypass triggered by distance $< \delta$) although Definition 1 calls $\mathcal{D}$ a set admitting an **open-loop** strategy — a mild internal tension (resolvable by making $\delta$ small enough and pre-computing, but not spelled out).
9. Lemma 1 (p. 5): the proof implicitly assumes the segment $[\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}]$ meets $\partial\mathcal{S}$ in a unique point (convex $\mathcal{S}$), an assumption not restated in Section V; Danskin-based steps (Theorem 3) tacitly assume compactness/attainment for $\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}}$, and $\mathcal{G}$ is nowhere assumed bounded.
10. Eq. (14) (p. 5): real solvability needs $|v_T\, d\tilde{t}_s/ds| \le \|d\mathbf{x}_s/ds\|_2$ for the $\arccos$ — not stated; the global assembly of $\mathcal{BD}(X_0)$ (branch choice, jump locations such as $s_c = 0.13$, endpoint arcs) is only illustrated on one example, not algorithmized in general.
11. No numerical benchmark against the HJI-based winning-region computations of [17], [18], which would be the natural validation for a geometric method claiming ease of analysis; no code availability statement.

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: P018 is the first paper in this collection to treat **two-phase (capture-the-flag-type) multiplayer** reach-avoid games with a purely geometric method, and the origin of the *ellipse* as the two-phase analogue of the Apollonius circle: the thief's two-leg objective (enter $\mathcal{G}$, then reach $\mathcal{S}$) has iso-cost sets that are ellipses with foci at the thief's initial position and the safe point, with major axis $v_T \times$ the guarders' one-leg deadline. It is also the natural reference for **two-phase dominance regions** — regions the thief can pass through and still survive — characterized as unions (eq. (9)) and envelopes (eqs. (12)–(14)) of one-parameter ellipse families with a focus sliding along $\partial\mathcal{S}$, together with an explicit boundary-construction algorithm validated on a half-plane example. Cite it (i) when motivating ellipse-based (sum-of-distances) geometry for multi-stage pursuit-evasion, (ii) as the multiplayer generalization contrasting with the two-player capture-the-flag line [17]–[21], (iii) for its minimax-plus-Danskin pattern (Theorem 3) in cooperative defense where each guarder owns a sub-region and the dividing point is the critical defense position (Theorem 4), or (iv) as the geometric counterpart to HJI-based two-phase winning-region computations. Caveats to state when citing: Theorem 5 is sufficient-only; results are for the game of kind, simple motion, and (beyond the point-safe case) guarders no slower than the thief.

**Suggested citation sentence**: "Deng et al. [P018] show that in two-phase multiplayer reach-avoid games with simple motion the game of kind reduces to an ellipse-intersection test — the thief wins iff the target region meets $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_0^T,\mathbf{x}_S,v_T\tilde{t}(\mathbf{x}_S,X_0))$, the ellipse with foci at the thief's initial position and the safe point and major axis $v_T$ times the fastest guarder's interception time — and they construct two-phase dominance regions as the envelope of a one-parameter family of such ellipses whose moving focus slides along the safe-region boundary."
