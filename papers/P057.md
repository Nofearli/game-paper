## 2. Research Background

Low-altitude airspace security is under growing threat from malicious or adversarial UAVs, and the proposed countermeasure is teams of defense UAVs that protect high-value targets (critical infrastructure) by mitigating the reconnaissance and attack activities of opponent UAVs (p. 1, Sec. 1). The resulting attack–defense interaction between malicious UAVs and defense UAVs involves "complex, nonintuitive cooperation among team members and competition among players" (p. 1). The authors frame unmanned-swarm coordination through the classical OODA loop and its recent extension, the Interaction-Observation-Orientation-Decision-Action (IOODA) loop of Dong et al. (ref. 1, *Acta Aeronautica et Astronautica Sinica* 2025;46(4):6-43, in Chinese), and state that Anti-unmanned Aerial Vehicles (AUAVs) "have not been sufficiently studied within the IOODA loop" (p. 1) [authors' claim]. Differential games, introduced formally by Isaacs in the 1960s (ref. 2, *Differential Games*, Wiley, 1965), provide a rigorous mathematical framework for player interactions in continuous state and action spaces, and reach-avoid differential games — in which an evasion team tries to send as many evaders as possible into a goal region while a pursuit team tries to capture the evaders before they reach it — have over the last twenty years become the standard tool for modeling such adversarial scenarios (p. 1) [authors' claim]. The paper's thesis is that AUAV problems and reach-avoid games "intrinsically share many similarities, such as player goals, game dynamics, and mixed cooperation-competition strategies" (p. 1), so reach-avoid theory can serve as "a proper theoretical decision-making foundation for AUAVs" (p. 2); within the IOODA loop, reach-avoid games "focus on the task allocation in the decision phase and the generation of control strategies in the action phrase [sic]" (p. 2).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem (as a commentary)**: argue and delineate *which* reach-avoid differential-game technologies are ready to underpin AUAV decision-making, and *which* gaps separate current theory from practical low-altitude defense. The paper first gives a classic multiplayer reach-avoid formulation (Sec. 2), then organizes the "main methods" into three topics — (2.1) games of kind and degree, (2.2) task allocation, (2.3) capture styles — and finally lists four fundamental challenges with future directions (Sec. 3).

**Taxonomy recorded by the review** (all [authors' claim] unless cross-verified):

1. **Problem classes**: games of kind (qualitative: winning conditions deciding who wins) vs. games of degree (quantitative: maximize/minimize a payoff, "such as the distance between the evader and the goal region when the game is over") (Sec. 2.1, p. 3). Dynamics classes: simple motion (bounded speed, instantaneous heading change — "suitable for mobile robots or UAVs which have speed limitations and high maneuverability", "extensively studied with fruitful results") vs. Dubins-car models (bounded speed + minimum turning radius — "can describe the fixed-wing UAVs or UAVs with low maneuverability") (Sec. 2, p. 3). Player classes: Np pursuers vs. Ne evaders, with pursuit coalitions (many-vs-one subgames) as the operative unit (Sec. 2.2).
2. **Solution methods**: (a) numerical — reachable-set computation via Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs (HJI) equations, attributed as "one predominant numerical method", applicable to complex dynamical systems but "constrained by heavy computation demands" (Sec. 2.1, p. 3; ref. 3 = Chen, Herbert, Vashishtha et al., *IEEE TAC* 2018;63(11):3675-88, "Decomposition of reachable sets and tubes for a class of nonlinear systems"); (b) geometric — dominance regions, valid when optimal trajectories are straight lines (simple motion), constructed by "three principal techniques: Voronoi Cell, Apollonius Circle and Potential Function" (Sec. 2.1, p. 3; refs. 4–6). Dominance region definition quoted: "a point is said to be dominated by one of the players if that player can reach the point before the other players, regardless of the other players' best effort" (p. 3). (c) compositional — decompose the original multiplayer game into subgames, each "a pursuit coalition (comprising multiple pursuers) and a single evader" (Sec. 2.2, p. 3; refs. 3, 4), with coalition membership decided "by using the winning conditions from games of kind, instead of intuitive distance-based criteria" (p. 3), followed by assignment of coalitions to evaders to maximize the number of captured evaders; (d) complexity/approximation — task allocation for capturing the most evaders is "provably NP-hard"; the Sequential Matching Algorithm (SMA) is an approximation for real-time large-scale play that "ensures a lower bound of captured evaders compared with the optimal matching result" (p. 3); (e) coalition reduction — "if a coalition can ensure the capture of an evader, there exists a nontrivial upper bound of the number of pursuers in the coalition that are required to guarantee this capture, which largely reduces the computational burdens" (p. 3; ref. 6).
3. **Application scenarios (AUAV mapping, Sec. 2, p. 3)**: critical infrastructure ≡ goal region Ω_goal; malicious UAVs ≡ evaders E; defense UAVs ≡ pursuers P; capture styles — collision, lasers, nets — ≡ capture radius r_i. Reach-avoid theory answers "who is the game winner based on the game configurations" (winning conditions) and "how to win the game" (winning/optimal strategies) in practical AUAVs (p. 3).
4. **Trends identified**: capture styles "have recently entered an accelerated phase, driven by increasing threats from the malicious UAVs in low-altitude environments" (Sec. 2.3, pp. 3–4), with three families: energy weapons (lasers), self-destruction capture (collision), self-insured capture (nets); vision-based cooperative net-capture as the cited exemplar (ref. 7); LLM-based matching as an emerging direction (Sec. 3, challenge 2).
5. **Open problems**: the four challenges of Sec. 3 (model fidelity, allocation mechanism, capture styles, imperfect information) — detailed in Sec. 7 below.

**Gap claims**: [Authors' claim] AUAVs are insufficiently studied within the IOODA loop (p. 1); multiplayer reach-avoid games "are in the nascent stages despite the history of twenty-year research" (Sec. 3, p. 4); game models, task allocation, capture-style integration, and imperfect information are the four gaps separating theory from practice. These gap statements are the authors' position; no systematic literature statistics are given (the whole evidence base is 10 references). — [Verified from cited text] The characterizations of refs. 4, 6 (decomposition-then-assignment; NP-hard matching; SMA; coalition-size bound) are accurate against the originals as archived in this KB (P037/P024, P103); see Sec. 5. One printed reference has a year error (ref. 4: "2020" — the TAC paper is Vol. 66, No. 5, **May 2021**, pp. 2345–2350, DOI 10.1109/TAC.2020.3003840; per P037) and the p. 2 sentence defining g is truncated in the pre-proof ("a continuous function such that Ωgoal." — the defining property of Ω_goal is cut off).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **(Position/taxonomy)** A compact taxonomy of reach-avoid differential-game technologies organized for the anti-UAV application: games of kind/degree → numerical (HJI) vs. geometric (dominance regions: Voronoi / Apollonius / potential function) solvers; decomposition of multiplayer games into coalition-vs-one-evader subgames with winning-condition-based coalition formation; matching-based task allocation with NP-hardness and the SMA approximation; capture-style taxonomy (laser / collision / net) tied to the capture radius r_i (Secs. 2.1–2.3). This is a curated reading list of 10 anchors, several of which are the authors' own papers.
2. **(Modeling/mapping)** An explicit element-by-element mapping between the reach-avoid formulation (Eq. (1), Ω_goal, P/E, r_i) and AUAV scenario elements (infrastructure, malicious UAVs, defense UAVs, capture equipment), positioning reach-avoid games as the "rigorous game-theoretic framework for modeling AUAVs scenarios by defense UAVs" (Sec. 2, p. 3), and slotting the technology into the decision (task allocation) and action (control-strategy generation) phases of the IOODA loop (Sec. 1, p. 2).
3. **(Methodology assessment)** Short qualitative assessments of the two solver families: HJI = broad applicability to complex dynamics but computationally heavy; geometric dominance-region methods = "popular and powerful" under simple motion where optimal trajectories are straight lines (Sec. 2.1, p. 3); matching-based allocation = provably hard (NP-hard), hence approximation (SMA with a capture-count lower bound) for real-time large-scale play (Sec. 2.2, p. 3).
4. **(Open problems)** Four fundamental challenges with concrete future directions: higher-fidelity models (Dubins-car, damped double integrator, multi-phase games, collision-avoidance strategies, obstacles, irregular goal regions); principled task-allocation mechanisms (analytical matching algorithms and/or LLM-assisted matching with formal guarantees as the research question); systematic integration of physical capture styles into game analysis; reach-avoid games under an imperfect-information framework (Sec. 3, p. 4; Sec. 4, p. 4).
5. **(No new theory/experiments)** The paper contains no theorems, proofs, algorithms, or simulations of its own — its contributions are exclusively curatorial and programmatic.

### 5.0 The review's own formulation (Sec. 2, pp. 2–3, Eq. (1))

- **Game space**: R³ split by a closed surface T into Ω_goal = {y ∈ R³ | g(y) ≤ 0}, T = {y ∈ R³ | g(y) = 0}, Ω_play = {y ∈ R³ | g(y) > 0}, with g: R³ → R "a continuous function such that Ωgoal." — **the sentence is truncated in the pre-proof**; the intended regularity/shape condition on Ω_goal is unprinted (p. 2).
- **States**: x_Pi(t) = [x_Pi, y_Pi, z_Pi, ψ_Pi]ᵀ ∈ Rⁿ (n ≥ 3), x_Ej(t) = [x_Ej, y_Ej, z_Ej, ψ_Ej]ᵀ ∈ R^m (m ≥ 3); positions in R³; ψ ∈ R^(n−3) resp. R^(m−3) are "remaining elements (maybe empty)" (pp. 2–3). So the formulation is dimension-generic: 3-D simple motion is the minimal case, and Dubins/attitude states are accommodated.
- **Dynamics (Eq. (1), p. 3)**: ẋ_Pi = f_Pi(x_Pi, u_Pi), x_Pi(0) = x⁰_Pi, Pi ∈ P; ẋ_Ej = f_Ej(x_Ej, u_Ej), x_Ej(0) = x⁰_Ej, Ej ∈ E; f_Pi: Rⁿ×R^kp → Rⁿ and f_Ej: R^m×R^ke → R^m **continuously differentiable**.
- **Controls**: u_Pi ∈ U_p = {u: [0,∞) → S^kp | u piecewise smooth}, u_Ej ∈ U_e analogously, with S^kp = {u ∈ R^kp | ‖u‖ ≤ 1} the unit disk (p. 3). Note: for 3-D simple motion this disk is actually the ball/disk mismatch — as printed, "S^kp is the unit disk in R^kp"; no further regularity is imposed.
- **Capture**: evader Ej is captured by Pi iff the Euclidean distance between their positions ≤ the **capture radius r_i ≥ 0** "and P_i is pursuing the evader" (p. 3) — the pursuit-assignment qualifier matters for the coalition-based reading (a pursuer only captures its matched evader).
- **Objectives/winning conditions**: evasion team E maximizes the number of evaders entering Ω_goal before capture; pursuit team P "strives to prevent E from reaching Ωgoal"; research focus = "strategies for players and the winning conditions to determine the game winner" (p. 3). This is the canonical team reach-avoid objective used by the Beihang line (identical to P103's objective).
- **Assessment**: [authors' claim] the formulation "naturally match[es] the AUAVs" via the mapping infrastructure↔Ω_goal, malicious UAVs↔E, defense UAVs↔P, capture styles↔r_i (p. 3).

### 5.1 Dynamics models cataloged (Sec. 2, p. 3)

- **Simple motion**: bounded speed, instantaneous heading change; matches "mobile robots or UAVs which have speed limitations and high maneuverability"; "due to its simplicity, such a model has been extensively studied with fruitful results in reach-avoid differential games" [authors' claim]. This is the model underlying refs. 4–7.
- **Dubins car**: bounded speed + minimum turning radius; describes "fixed-wing UAVs or UAVs with low maneuverability" [authors' claim]. ### 5.2 Games of kind and degree + HJI reachable sets (Sec. 2.1, p. 3)

- **Games of kind** = qualitative outcome (winning conditions); **games of degree** = quantitative payoff optimization, e.g., terminal distance between evader and goal region. Standard Isaacs terminology (ref. 2) [authors' claim, faithful to Isaacs].
- **HJI method** (ref. 3: Chen M., Herbert S.L., Vashishtha M.S., et al., "Decomposition of reachable sets and tubes for a class of nonlinear systems", *IEEE TAC* 2018;63(11):3675-88): presented as "one predominant numerical method ... to compute reachable sets using solutions of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs (HJI) equations"; strengths: "broader applicability to complex dynamical systems currently"; limits: "practical implementation is constrained by heavy computation demands" (p. 3) [authors' claim]. Note the choice of ref. 3 — a decomposition paper for reachable sets/tubes of cascade-type nonlinear systems — rather than a canonical level-set reference; the review gives no HJI survey citation and no dimension-limit figures.
- **Dominance regions** (geometric branch): defined via domination of points (earliest-arrival property "regardless of the other players' best effort"); solvability of kind and degree "is closely related to constructing the dominance regions" when optimal trajectories are straight lines, "which is common under the simple motion" (p. 3). Three construction techniques named with refs. 4–6: **Voronoi Cell** (ref. 4 context), **Apollonius Circle**, **Potential Function** (ref. 6). — [verified vs. KB: P103 (ref. 6) constructs the generalized, capture-radius-bearing Apollonius surface as the zero level set of the potential f_ij(x) = ‖x − x_Pi‖₂ − α_ij‖x − x_Ej‖₂ − r_i (its eq. (2)), with the evasion space as superlevel set {f_ij > 0} and strictly convex bounded closure (its Lemma 3.1); P106 (ref. 5) uses expanded Apollonius circles and convex goal-covering polygons with Euclidean shortest paths in polygonal obstacle fields; P037/P024 (ref. 4) build Apollonius-circle-based dominance/assigned regions for the border-defense game.] The review itself does not give any of these formulas — it only names the techniques.

### 5.3 Subgame decomposition and winning-condition coalitions (Sec. 2.2, p. 3; refs. 3, 4)

- **Prevailing method**: decompose the original Np-vs-Ne game into subgames, "where each subgame consists of a pursuit coalition (comprising multiple pursuers) and a single evader" (refs. 3, 4). — [verified vs. KB: P037 (ref. 4; Garcia, Casbeer, Von Moll, Pachter, *IEEE TAC* 66(5), May 2021, pp. 2345–2350, DOI 10.1109/TAC.2020.3003840) solves the border-defense differential game by decomposing into pursuer-to-evader assignments with Apollonius-based 1-vs-1 capture regions plus a discrete assignment layer; P103 (ref. 3/6 context) formalizes the coalition-vs-one-evader subgame via the evasion space E(s,j) = ∩_{i∈s} E(i,j).] Note: the review attaches ref. 3 (Chen et al. 2018, HJI decomposition) to this decomposition principle as well — in P103's pipeline the decomposition-into-subgames is grounded in reachable-set decomposition arguments, consistent with the review's pairing.
- **Coalition criterion**: membership decided by whether the coalition "can guarantee to win against an evader by using the winning conditions from games of kind, instead of intuitive distance-based criteria" (p. 3). This is the review's clearest methodological message: game-of-kind guarantees, not heuristics, define coalitions. [authors' claim; verified as faithful to P103's coalitional game-of-kind trichotomy.]
- **Coalition reduction** (ref. 6): "if a coalition can ensure the capture of an evader, there exists a nontrivial upper bound of the number of pursuers in the coalition that are required to guarantee this capture, which largely reduces the computational burdens" (p. 3). — [verified vs. KB: P103, Corollary 1/Lemma 3.3: **at most three pursuers** are ever necessary to defend the goal against one evader in 3-D (coalition size ≤ 3); P105 similarly performs coalition reduction for Dubins-car pursuers.] The review states the bound exists but never prints its value (3).

### 5.4 Task allocation complexity and SMA (Sec. 2.2, p. 3; ref. 6)

- **NP-hardness**: "The complexity of the task allocation for capturing the most evaders is provably NP-hard" (p. 3). — [verified vs. KB: P103, Theorem 4.1: the Maximum Bipartite Matching with Conflict graph (MBMC) — the exact whole-game allocation problem with size-≤3 coalitions — is NP-hard by reduction from 3-dimensional matching, and APX-complete (its Corollary 2).] Caveat: the review phrases this as a general statement about "the task allocation for capturing the most evaders"; the proven result covers the specific MBMC formulation of P103 — a mild overgeneralization.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated open problems (Sec. 3, p. 4 — the four "fundamental challenges")**:

1. **Game-model fidelity gap**: winning conditions/strategies exist only for simplified models — "Current research mainly focuses on simple-motion players, while more complex dynamics receive limited attention due to higher-dimensional state spaces and the underlying nonlinearity. The game region is simplified, such as obstacle-free play regions, a single goal region of regular shape, and even the goal region that is a point" (p. 4). Future: Dubins-car, damped double integrator, multi-phase games (refs. 8–10), collision-avoidance strategy (ref. 5).
2. **Task allocation lacks a clear mechanism**: the standard two-step decompose-then-assign solution "basically is still heuristic" (ref. 9 cited here), with "limited attention ... paid to the algorithm for solving the resulting matching problems" (p. 4). Future: analytical algorithms (fast, high-quality); LLM-based matching (possibly superior outcomes, no formal guarantees); hybrid analytical+LLM integration.
3. **Capture styles in initial stage**: lasers/collision/nets "are mainly used in the simulation, requiring to prove their capture stability and precision in practical applications"; "the integration of these capture styles into reach-avoid differential games ... has not been systematically investigated" (p. 4).
4. **Imperfect information**: AUAV scenarios are mostly imperfect-information games, but "the existing research assumes omniscience regarding the environments and opponents, such as the opponents' accurate positions, velocities and even the control inputs" (p. 4). Future: reach-avoid games under an imperfect-information framework.
