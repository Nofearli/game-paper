## 2. Research Background

Reach-avoid games model adversarial scenarios where one team (defenders) tries to stop the other team (attackers) from entering a target region, with applications to collision avoidance, surveillance, and satellite maneuvering. When the defense or attack team has more than one member, cooperation becomes essential; the literature has concentrated on cooperative *defense* (Voronoi-cell, Apollonius-circle, and matching-based strategies), while cooperative *attack* strategies are scarce. The minimal setting forcing attacker cooperation is one defender versus two attackers: the defender can only chase one attacker at a time, so the two attackers must coordinate (one may even sacrifice itself) for either to reach the target. Prior works on such games (border defense, turret–runner–penetrator, coastline defense) either fix the defender's assignment at the start or lack rigorous sufficiency proofs of the proposed value functions. A classical but often ignored fact is that the "equilibrium controls are constant / trajectories are straight lines" conclusion for simple-motion games holds only away from singular surfaces; for multi-agent games singular surfaces (dispersal surfaces, switch envelopes, focal surfaces) are generic and curved equilibrium motion can occur (cf. Breakwell–Hagedorn's one-pursuer–two-evader focal surface). This paper provides the missing rigorous treatment: a fully proved value function and a systematic singular-surface analysis for one defender against two cooperating attackers in the plane.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** Zero-sum reach-avoid differential game in $\mathbb{R}^2$: defender $D$ (speed 1) protects the half-plane target $\Omega=\{x=[x,y]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^2\mid y\le 0\}$ against two cooperative attackers $A_1, A_2$ (speeds $\alpha_1,\alpha_2<1$), simple motion, point capture. Attackers win if either enters $\Omega$ before capture; the game of degree has terminal payoff $J=\min_i y_{A_i}(T_i)$ (eq. (5)). The goal is the value function, equilibrium strategies, and a complete description of the singular surfaces, for (i) a *constrained* scenario where $D$ must capture $A_1$ first, and (ii) the *general* scenario with no capture-order restriction.

**Gap — authors' claims:**
1. [authors' claim, p. 559] "rigorous proofs of the sufficiency of the value function are lacking in these works, for which a thorough discussion of singular surfaces is required" — referring to the one-defender–two-attacker literature ([15] Salmon et al. border defense, [16] Yan et al. two-evader–one-pursuer cooperation, [17] turret–runner–penetrator with role selection, [18] Garcia et al. coastline defense). *Verified as plausible against the cited works' framing*: [15]–[18] derive candidate values geometrically (Apollonius-based, fixed roles chosen at start) and at most "consider the existence of singular surfaces in an example" ([18]); none proves viscosity-solution sufficiency on nondifferentiable sets. (I could not re-verify each cited paper's content here; the claim is consistent with their abstracts as characterized by this paper.)
2. [authors' claim, p. 559] Existing viscosity-condition tools for singular surfaces ([21] Melikyan) handle only two differentiable branches ($m=2$). *Verified*: Lemma 1 of this paper explicitly generalizes [21] to $m>2$ branches, so the gap within the singular-surface theory itself is real.
3. [authors' claim, p. 566] Proving that equilibrium trajectories leave a surface from both sides is *not sufficient* for a dispersal surface; the viscosity condition $\tilde H(\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_i^*)\ge 0$ is also necessary. *Verified internally*: Section V-A constructs $\Upsilon_2$ — points where the naive $\bar V(X)=\max\{J_1,J_2\}$ has trajectories leaving on both sides yet violates the viscosity condition (Fig. 4), so $\bar V$ is not the value function. This is a genuine, demonstrated insufficiency of the common "dispersal-surface-by-picture" practice.

**Core gap addressed**: no complete value function (with proof) existed for any one-defender–two-attacker reach-avoid game with attacker cooperation, and the singular-surface structure (dispersal + switch envelope + focal surface interplay) of such games was uncharacterized.

## 4. Core Contributions

Stated by the authors (p. 559) and verified against the body:

1. **Theory (Lemma 1, Sec. II, p. 560)**: A necessary and sufficient viscosity condition on a singular surface for a function with $m>2$ differentiable branches, $u=\min_{i\in I}u_i$: $F(x_0,\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla u_i(x_0))\ge 0$ for all convex weights $\lambda_i\ge0$, $\sum\lambda_i=1$ (eq. (2)); generalizes Melikyan's $m=2$ result [21]. Companion Lemma 2 handles the mixed max–min representation (3) (condition holds automatically).
2. **Theory (Theorem 1, Sec. IV, p. 565)**: Full-state-space value function $J_1(X)$ for the constrained scenario (defender must capture $A_1$ first), constructed by geometric Apollonius-circle reasoning and proved to be the *unique viscosity solution* of the HJI equation — including the viscosity condition on the dispersal surface, not merely the HJI equation in the smooth region. The attackers' cooperative equilibrium strategy is read off from the value.
3. **Theory (Sec. V, Theorems 2–4)**: Singular-surface analysis of the general scenario: the set $\{J_1=J_2\}$ is *not* wholly a dispersal surface ($\bar V=\max\{J_1,J_2\}$ fails the viscosity condition on $\Upsilon_2$); only its subset $\Upsilon_1$ is a (defender's) dispersal surface. Three typical boundary behaviors of $\Upsilon_1$ are classified — bifurcation into two switch envelopes (Th. 2), bifurcation into a switch envelope plus a dispersal surface (Th. 3), attachment of a switch envelope (Th. 4) — with local construction of switch envelopes by integrating an ODE system (35)–(36) in inverse time and local proof that the resulting $V=\max\{V_0,\bar V\}$ is the value.
4. **Numerical illustration (Sec. V-B, Figs. 6–12)**: Numerical construction of the switch envelopes and equilibrium trajectories confirming $V>\bar V$ on the singular region.

**Modeling/experiment**: none beyond the numerical constructions; the paper is analytic.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

### 5.0 Setting, information structure, cost, termination

- **Dynamics (eq. (4), p. 561)**: $\dot X=F$, $F=[u_D^\top,\ \alpha_1 u_{A_1}^\top,\ \alpha_2 u_{A_2}^\top]^\top$, state $X=[x_D^\top,x_{A_1}^\top,x_{A_2}^\top]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^6$, controls $u_D,u_{A_i}\in\mathbb{S}^1$; defender speed normalized to 1, $A_i$ speed $\alpha_i<1$. Simple motion; point capture ($A_i$ captured iff $\|x_D-x_{A_i}\|_2=0$); $T_i$ = capture time of $A_i$; game ends at $t_e=\max_i T_i$.
- **Target / winning**: $\Omega=\{y\le 0\}$; attack team wins iff $x_{A_i}(T_i)\in\Omega$ for $i=1$ or $2$.
- **Cost (game of degree, eq. (5))**: $J(X(0);F(t))=\min_i y_{A_i}(T_i)$; attackers minimize, defender maximizes; $J\le0\iff$ attack team wins, so the game of kind is solved by the degree value.
- **Two-phase reduction (eqs. (6)–(8))**: after the first capture at $t_f=\min_i T_i$ the game reduces to one-defender–one-attacker; equivalent terminal payoff $J=\min\{y_{A_{i1}}(t_f),\,V(x_D(t_f),x_{A_{i2}}(t_f))\}$ (eq. (7)), terminal set $\mathcal T=\{X\mid\|x_D-x_{A_i}\|_2=0,\ i=1\text{ or }2\}$ (eq. (8)). Only the first phase is analyzed.
- **Information structure**: perfect-information feedback strategies; every player knows $X$ and (4); decisions depend on current state only; the two attackers choose controls cooperatively (p. 561).
- **HJI/Isaacs structure (eqs. (13)–(15), p. 562)**: Hamiltonian $H(F,q)=u_D^\top q_D+\alpha_1u_{A_1}^\top q_{A_1}+\alpha_2u_{A_2}^\top q_{A_2}$ (14); minimax Hamiltonian $\tilde H(q)=\|q_D\|_2-\alpha_1\|q_{A_1}\|_2-\alpha_2\|q_{A_2}\|_2$; minimax (equilibrium) control field $F^*(q)=\nabla\tilde H(q)=[\langle q_D\rangle^\top,-\alpha_1\langle q_{A_1}\rangle^\top,-\alpha_2\langle q_{A_2}\rangle^\top]^\top$ (15). HJI equation (13): $\min_{u_{A_1},u_{A_2}}\max_{u_D}H=\max_{u_D}\min H=0$, equivalently $\tilde H(\nabla J_1)=0$ (21).

### 5.1 Lemma 1 (p. 560, eq. (2)) — viscosity condition on an $m$-branch min surface

**Statement.** Let $x_0$ lie on a singular surface near which $u(x)=\min_{i\in I}u_i(x)$, $I=\{1,\dots,m\}$, $u_i$ differentiable with pairwise distinct gradients $\nabla u_i(x_0)\ne\nabla u_j(x_0)$. Then the viscosity condition at $x_0$ is equivalent to
$$F\Big(x_0,\ \sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\nabla u_i(x_0)\Big)\ge 0 \quad (2)$$
for all $\lambda_i\ge0$, $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$; for $u=\max_i u_i$ the inequality reverses.
**Assumptions**: continuity of $u$; $m$ differentiable branches with distinct gradients at $x_0$; $F$ the HJ Hamiltonian of the terminal value problem.
**Proof (3–5 steps)**: (i) No test function can attain a local *minimum* of $u-\varphi$ at $x_0$: otherwise for each pair $i<j$ the min-of-two $\tilde u_{ij}=\min\{u_i,u_j\}$ also has a local min there, and monotonicity of $\tilde u_{ij}-\varphi$ on each side of the branch-cut direction $\nabla u_i-\nabla u_j$ forces two inequalities whose product yields $\|\nabla u_i-\nabla u_j\|^2\le 0$, contradicting distinctness. (ii) Hence only local *maxima* of $u-\varphi$ matter; by shifting the test function one may take $\tilde\varphi(x_0)=u(x_0)$ and $u\le\tilde\varphi$ near $x_0$. (iii) $[x_0^\top,u(x_0)]^\top$ is then a minimum of $\min_{x,z}\tilde\varphi(x)-z$ s.t. $z\le u_i(x)\ \forall i$; the KKT condition (citing Kuhn–Tucker [29]) gives $\nabla\varphi(x_0)=\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla u_i(x_0)$ with $\lambda_i\ge0$, $\sum\lambda_i=1$. (iv) Substituting into the definition (1) yields exactly (2). (v) Conversely, $\varphi=\sum_i\lambda_iu_i$ is itself a valid test function attaining a local max of $u-\varphi$ at $x_0$, so (2) must hold for *all* convex weights — necessity.
**Decisive equation**: the KKT stationarity $\nabla\varphi(x_0)=\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla u_i(x_0)$.
**Scope**: local, analytic. **Meaning**: on a kink where $m$ optimal "choices" meet, smoothness of the viscosity condition is tested against the entire convex hull of the branch gradients. **Extensibility**: explicitly generalizes [21] (which had $m=2$) to any $m$; used later at every nondifferentiable set of this paper.

### 5.2 Lemma 2 (p. 560, eq. (3)) — mixed max–min branches

Near $x_0$ let $u(x)=\max\{\min_{1\le i\le m}u_i(x),\ \min_{m<j\le n}u_j(x)\}$ with distinct branch gradients. Then the viscosity condition holds at $x_0$ **automatically** (no inequality to check). Proof: same analysis as Lemma 1 shows neither a local min nor a local max of $u-\varphi$ can be attained at $x_0$, so Definition 1 is vacuous there. Local, analytic. Used in Remark 4 to skip points of $\Upsilon$ where one $J_i$ is nondifferentiable.

### 5.3 Lemma 3 (p. 561, eq. (6)) — one-defender–one-attacker value (= Theorem 1 of [18], imported)

For the single-defender–single-attacker reach-avoid game with terminal set $\{\|x_D-x_{A_i}\|_2=0\}$ and terminal payoff $y_{A_i}(t_f)$:
$$V(x_D,x_{A_i})=\frac{y_{A_i}-\alpha_i^2 y_D-\alpha_i\|x_{A_i}-x_D\|_2}{1-\alpha_i^2}. \quad (6)$$
Proof is cited, not repeated. Global, analytic; the building block for everything downstream (second-phase payoff, boundary values of Theorem 1).

### 5.4 Geometric construction of the constrained-game candidate (Sec. IV-A, pp. 561–562, eqs. (9)–(11))

Apollonius circle of $(A_i,D)$: $r_{C_i}(X)=\frac{\alpha_i\|x_{A_i}-x_D\|_2}{1-\alpha_i^2}$, $x_{C_i}(X)=\frac{x_{A_i}-\alpha_i^2x_D}{1-\alpha_i^2}$; point $\tilde x_i(\theta,X)=x_{C_i}+r_{C_i}e_\theta$; distance $\| \tilde x_i-x_{A_i}\|_2=\beta_i(\theta,X)r_{C_i}$ with $\beta_i(\theta,X)=\sqrt{1+\alpha_i^2-2\alpha_i\cos(\theta-\gamma_i(X))}$ and $e_{\gamma_i}=-\langle x_{A_i}-x_D\rangle$ (verified against rendered p. 561). $A_i$ reaches any point inside $C_i$ no later than $D$, and vice versa outside. With $A_1$ heading to $\tilde x_1(\theta,X)$, $A_2$ playing constant $e_\phi$, and $D$ intercepting $A_1$ at $t_f=s_1(\theta,X(0))/\alpha_1$ (so $x_{A_2}(t_f)=x_2(\phi,\theta,X)=x_{A_2}+\frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha_1}s_1(\theta,X)e_\phi$, direction $\omega(\theta,\phi,X)$ of $x_2-\tilde x_1$), the constant-control payoff is
$$\eta_1(\theta,\phi,X(0))=\min\{\tilde y_1(\theta,X(0)),\ \zeta_1(\theta,\phi,X(0))\}\ (9),\qquad \zeta_1(\theta,\phi,X)=V(\tilde x_1(\theta,X),x_2(\phi,\theta,X))\ (10)$$
and the candidate value is
$$J_1(X)=\min_{\theta,\phi}\eta_1=\min\{J_{11}(X),J_{12}(X)\}\ (11),\qquad J_{11}=\min_\theta\tilde y_1=y_{C_1}-r_{C_1},\quad J_{12}=\min_{\theta,\phi}\zeta_1 .$$
Interpretation: $J_{11}$ is $A_1$'s selfish one-to-one value; $J_{12}$ is the best team value with $A_1$ sacrificing. $J_{11}<J_{12}$: no sacrifice needed; $J_{11}>J_{12}$: $A_1$ must sacrifice individual payoff to drag $D$ away from $A_2$; $J_{11}=J_{12}$: singularity.

### 5.5 Lemma 4 (p. 562, eq. (12)) — first-order conditions for the second-phase optimum

If $\zeta_1(\theta^*,\phi^*,X)=\min_{\theta,\phi}\zeta_1$, then
$$\alpha_2\cos\theta^*+\sin(\theta^*-\omega^*)=\big(\sin\phi^*-\alpha_2\cos(\phi^*-\omega^*)\big)\,\xi_1(\theta^*,X),\qquad \cos\phi^*+\alpha_2\sin(\phi^*-\omega^*)=0 \quad (12)$$
with $\xi_i(\theta,X)=\sin(\theta-\gamma_i(X))/\beta_i(\theta,X)$, $\omega^*=\omega(\theta^*,\phi^*,X)$. **Proof**: direct differentiation of $\zeta_1$ w.r.t. $\theta$ and $\phi$ (chain rule through $V$, $\tilde x_1$, $x_2$; displayed derivatives $\frac{\partial\zeta_1}{\partial\theta}\propto \xi_1\sin\phi^*-\alpha_2\cos(\phi^*-\omega^*)\xi_1-(\alpha_2\cos\theta^*+\sin(\theta^*-\omega^*))$ and $\frac{\partial\zeta_1}{\partial\phi^*}\propto\cos\phi^*+\alpha_2\sin(\phi^*-\omega^*)$), then setting both to zero. Local, analytic. Meaning: on a regular optimum, $A_2$ runs along the tangent of a circle around the eventual second capture geometry, and $A_1$'s heading balances direct evasion against second-phase value.

### 5.6 Lemma 5 (p. 562, eq. (16)) — $J_{11}$ solves the HJI equation

$J_{11}(X)=V(x_D,x_{A_1})$ is differentiable and $\tilde H(\nabla J_{11})=0$ everywhere outside $\mathcal T$. **Proof**: from (6),
$$\frac{\partial J_{11}}{\partial x_D}=\frac{\alpha_1}{1-\alpha_1^2}\big(\alpha_1e_{-\pi/2}-e_{\gamma_1}\big),\qquad \frac{\partial J_{11}}{\partial x_{A_1}}=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha_1^2}\big(e_{-\pi/2}-\alpha_1e_{\gamma_1}\big),\quad \frac{\partial J_{11}}{\partial x_{A_2}}=0 \ (16)$$
so $\big\|\frac{\partial J_{11}}{\partial x_D}\big\|_2=\alpha_1\big\|\frac{\partial J_{11}}{\partial x_{A_1}}\big\|_2=\frac{\alpha_1\beta_1(-\pi/2)}{1-\alpha_1^2}$, which makes $\tilde H(\nabla J_{11})=\|q_D\|-\alpha_1\|q_{A_1}\|=0$. Decisive identity: the norm matching $\|q_D\|_2=\alpha_1\|q_{A_1}\|_2$ (the "equal speed-ratio" signature of simple-motion values). Global, analytic.

### 5.7 Lemma 6 (p. 563, eqs. (17)–(20)) — $J_{12}$ solves the HJI equation where the minimizing angles are unique

Outside $\mathcal T$, if the pair $(\theta^*,\phi^*)$ minimizing $\zeta_1$ is unique, then $J_{12}$ is differentiable and $\tilde H(\nabla J_{12})=0$. **Proof (5 steps)**: (i) $\nabla J_{12}=\frac{\partial}{\partial X}V(\tilde x_1(\theta^*,X),x_2(\phi^*,\theta^*,X))$ by the envelope/chain rule. (ii) Explicit player-wise gradients (17)–(19), e.g. $\frac{\partial J_{12}}{\partial x_{A_2}}=\frac{e_{\pi/2}-\alpha_2e_{\omega^*}}{1-\alpha_2^2}$, with $\bar\alpha_2=\frac{\alpha_2}{(1-\alpha_1^2)(1-\alpha_2^2)}$ and $\sigma_i(\theta,X)=\frac{1+\alpha_i^2-\alpha_i\cos(\theta-\gamma_i(X))}{\beta_i(\theta,X)}$. (iii) Candidate equilibrium field $\bar F=[\langle\alpha_1e_{\theta^*}-e_{\gamma_1}\rangle^\top,\ \alpha_1\langle e_{\theta^*}-\alpha_1e_{\gamma_1}\rangle^\top,\ \alpha_2e_{\phi^*}^\top]^\top$ (20) (geometric meaning: $D$ and $A_1$ run straight to the Apollonius point $\tilde x_1(\theta^*,X)$, $A_2$ runs along $e_{\phi^*}$); direct computation gives $H(\bar F,\nabla J_{12})=0$. (iv) Show $\bar F=F^*(\nabla J_{12})$: exterior-product identities like $\frac{e_{\theta^*}-\alpha_1e_{\gamma_1}}{\bar\alpha_2}\wedge\frac{\partial J_{12}}{\partial x_{A_1}}=0$ (from (12), (18)) establish parallelism — *the detailed derivation of this identity is delegated to the supplementary material* (proof gap: not checkable from the printed paper alone). (v) Orient the signs by contradiction: if $\frac{\partial J_{12}}{\partial x_{A_1}}$ pointed along $\langle\tilde x_1-x_{A_1}\rangle$ instead of against it, moving $A_1$ a small $\epsilon$ backward toward $\tilde x_1$ would strictly decrease $J_{12}$, yet the attacker from $x_{A_1}$ could still reach $\tilde x_1(\theta'^*,X')$ before $D$ (since $\|x_{A_1}-\tilde x_1(\theta'^*,X')\|_2\le\|x'_{A_1}-\tilde x_1(\theta'^*,X')\|_2$), yielding $\zeta_1\le J_{12}(X')<J_{12}(X)$ — contradiction. Hence $\langle\tilde x_1(\theta^*,X)-x_{A_1}\rangle=-\langle\frac{\partial J_{12}}{\partial x_{A_1}}\rangle$, and similarly for $D$ and $A_2$; therefore $\tilde H(\nabla J_{12})=H(\bar F,\nabla J_{12})=0$. Local, analytic; decisive step is the parallelism + orientation argument identifying the geometric field $\bar F$ with $F^*(\nabla J_{12})$.

**Corollary 1 (p. 563, eq. (21))**: outside $\mathcal T$, $J_1$ satisfies the HJI equation $\tilde H(\nabla J_1)=0$ wherever $J_1$ is differentiable (immediate from Lemmas 5–6 since at differentiability points $\nabla J_1=\nabla J_{1i}$ for the active branch).

### 5.8 Lemma 7 (pp. 563–564, eqs. (23)–(26)) — viscosity condition on the singular surface $\Gamma$

Let $\Gamma=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^6\mid J_1(X)\text{ non-differentiable}\}$. Near $X\in\Gamma$, $J_1$ has the branch representation (22): $J_1(X')=\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,\bar m\}}v_i(X')$, $v_i=\zeta_1(\theta_i(X'),\phi_i(X'),X')$ for $i\le m$ (continuous branches of minimizers of $\zeta_1$, existing on an open $U\ni X$ by continuity of $\zeta_1$), plus $v_{m+1}=J_{11}$ with $\theta^*_{m+1}=-\pi/2$ when $J_{11}(X)=J_{12}(X)$; $\bar m=m$ or $m+1$ accordingly; $\partial_i^*(X)=\nabla v_i(X)$ given by (17)–(19) (or (16) for $i=m+1$).

**Statement.** At every $X\in\Gamma$: $\tilde H\big(\sum_{i=1}^{\bar m}\lambda_i\partial_i^*(X)\big)\ge0$ for all $\lambda_i\ge0$, $\sum\lambda_i=1$.

**Proof (5 steps, tools: geometry + convexity)**: (i) Define angles $\theta_\lambda,\phi_\lambda$ by $\langle e_{\theta_\lambda}-\alpha_1e_{\gamma_1}\rangle=-\big\langle\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_{i,A_1}^*(X)\big\rangle$, $e_{\phi_\lambda}=-\big\langle\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_{i,A_2}^*(X)\big\rangle$, and the associated control field $F_\lambda$ as in (20); let $l_i^*=\|x_2(\phi_i^*,\theta_i^*,X)-\tilde x_1(\theta_i^*,X)\|_2$, $l_\lambda$ analogously. (ii) Two decompositions of $x_{A_2}$ relative to $\tilde x_1(\theta_i^*,X)$ and $\tilde x_1(\theta_\lambda,X)$ are equated and projected on $e_{\omega_i^*}$, giving the geometric identity (23) (a cosine-relation among $\theta,\phi,\omega$ angles and lengths). (iii) Substituting (17)–(19) and (23) into $H(F_\lambda,\partial_i^*(X))$ collapses it to $\frac{\alpha_2}{(1-\alpha_2^2)\beta_1(\theta_\lambda)}\big[\alpha_1\alpha_2(\sin\theta_i^*-\sin\theta_\lambda)-\alpha_1\frac{l_\lambda\cos(\omega_\lambda-\omega_i^*)-l_i^*}{r_{C_1}}+\beta_1(\theta_\lambda)\sin\phi_\lambda-\beta_1(\theta_i^*)\sin\phi_i^*\big]$. (iv) The optimality $\zeta_1(\theta_i^*,\phi_i^*,X)\le\zeta_1(\theta_\lambda,\phi_\lambda,X)$ is equivalent to inequality (24), which after substitution yields the decisive bound
$$H(F_\lambda,\partial_i^*(X))\ \ge\ \frac{\alpha_2}{(1-\alpha_2^2)r_{C_1}\beta_1(\theta_\lambda,X)}\Big[\alpha_1(l_\lambda-l_i^*)-\alpha_1\big(l_\lambda\cos(\omega_\lambda-\omega_i^*)-l_i^*\big)\Big]\ \ge\ 0 \quad (25)$$
(the second inequality because $1-\cos\ge0$ and the geometry forces $l_\lambda\ge l_i^*$ via (24)); and when $J_{11}=J_{12}$, $H(F_\lambda,\partial^*_{m+1}(X))=\frac{\alpha_1}{\beta_1(\theta_\lambda,X)}(1+\sin\theta_\lambda)\ge0$ (26). (v) Convexity: $\tilde H(\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_i^*)\ \ge\ u_{\lambda,D}^\top(\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_{i,D}^*)-\alpha_1\|\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_{i,A_1}^*\|_2-\alpha_2\|\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_{i,A_2}^*\|_2\ =\ H(F_\lambda,\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_i^*)\ =\ \sum_i\lambda_i H(F_\lambda,\partial_i^*)\ \ge\ 0$, using $\|v\|\ge u_\lambda^\top v$ for the unit vectors built in (i). Local, analytic. **Meaning**: the convex hull of branch gradients never violates the viscosity inequality — this is exactly what Theorem 1 needs on $\Gamma$.

### 5.9 Theorem 1 (p. 565) — value function of the constrained scenario (main result 1)

**Statement.** The optimal outcome $J_1(X)$ defined by (11) is the unique viscosity solution of the HJI equation (21), $\tilde H(\nabla J_1)=0$, with boundary values on $\{X\in\mathbb{R}^6\mid\|x_D-x_{A_1}\|_2=0\}$:
$$J_1(X)=\min\Big\{\frac{y_{A_2}-\alpha_2^2y_{A_1}-\alpha_2\|x_{A_2}-x_{A_1}\|_2}{1-\alpha_2^2},\ y_{A_1}\Big\}.$$
Hence $J_1$ is the value function of the game where $D$ must capture $A_1$ first. **Assumptions**: dynamics (4), $\alpha_i<1$, payoff (5)/(7), Friedman's value-existence hypotheses (Remark 1: $F$ bounded, continuous, separable in the opposing controls; terminal payoff continuous; termination forced by $D$ in finite time proportional to initial distance to $\mathcal T$; citing Friedman [30]).
**Proof (4 steps)**: (i) boundary values checked directly from (6) at $\|x_D-x_{A_1}\|=0$; (ii) off $\Gamma$ the viscosity condition is Corollary 1; (iii) near $\Gamma$ use representation (22) with Lemmas 1 and 7 (so the $m$-branch convex-hull condition holds); (iv) uniqueness of the viscosity solution by Crandall–Lions [27, Th. 3.1]. **Scope**: global over $\mathbb{R}^6\setminus\mathcal T$, analytic construction (the minimizations over $\theta,\phi$ are low-dimensional and implicitly defined via (12)). **Physical meaning**: the constrained game is completely solved; the attackers' cooperation consists in $A_1$ choosing between its selfish heading ($\theta^*=-\pi/2$) and a sacrificing heading when $J_{12}<J_{11}$.

### 5.10 Corollary 2 (p. 565) — $\Gamma$ is an attackers' dispersal surface

At any $X\in\Gamma$ with two distinct minimizer pairs, taking $\lambda_j=1$ in (25) gives $H(F^*(\partial_j^*(X)),\partial_i^*(X))\ge0$ with equality only if $\omega_i^*=\omega_j^*$; since $\phi_k^*\in(\pi,2\pi)$, equation (12) then forces $\phi_i^*=\phi_j^*$ and $\theta_i^*=\theta_j^*$. Hence $H(F^*(\partial_j^*),\partial_i^*)>0$ for $i\ne j$: if the attackers commit to one optimal choice, the other choice becomes strictly worse — trajectories leave $\Gamma$ transversely, and the attack team (who owns the choice) dominates which side is taken. The degenerate case $J_{11}=J_{12}$ with $\theta_i^*=-\pi/2$ is excluded by a Fig. 2 counterexample: if $\theta_i^*=-\pi/2$ were optimal among the $\zeta_1$-minimizers, $A_1$ could deviate slightly from $-\pi/2$ forcing $D$ to take longer to reach the second-capture point $x_e$, giving $J_{12}<\zeta_1(-\pi/2,\phi^*_{m+1},X)=J_{11}$ — contradiction. **Equilibrium strategies** (Sec. IV-B): regular region — $F^*(\nabla J_1)$; explicitly $u_{A_1}=\langle e_{\theta^*}-\alpha_1e_{\gamma_1}\rangle$, $u_{A_2}=e_{\phi^*}$, $u_D=\langle\alpha_1e_{\theta^*}-e_{\gamma_1}\rangle$, straight-line trajectories since along equilibrium $\dot X=F^*(q),\ \dot q=0$ (27) (state-independent Hamiltonian). On $\Gamma$ the attack team may pick any minimizer pair; the defender without knowledge of the attackers' current controls can only suffer an infinitesimally brief loss and may play a mixed strategy on $\Gamma$, switching to the unique equilibrium control after exit. **Remark 3 (important methodological point)**: to certify a dispersal surface it is *not* enough that "optimal" trajectories leave it ($H(F^*(\partial_i^*),\partial_j^*)>0$); the viscosity condition $\tilde H(\sum_i\lambda_i\partial_i^*)\ge0$ of Lemma 7 is also necessary — and indeed fails on $\Upsilon_2$ below.

### 5.11 Sec. V-A (pp. 566–567) — why $\bar V=\max\{J_1,J_2\}$ fails; Lemma 8; $\Upsilon_1,\Upsilon_2$, $B_0,B_1,B_2$

For the unconstrained scenario the naive candidate is $\bar V(X)=\max\{J_1(X),J_2(X)\}$ ($J_2$: value when $D$ must capture $A_2$ first). At $X\in\Upsilon=\{J_1=J_2\}$ where both are differentiable, Lemma 1 (max-case) requires $f(\lambda,X):=\tilde H(q_\lambda(X))=\tilde H((1-\lambda)q_1+\lambda q_2)\le0$ on $[0,1]$, where $q_i=\nabla J_i$; Corollary 1 gives $f(0,X)=f(1,X)=0$, so (28) is necessary:
$$f_\lambda(0,X)=(q_2-q_1)^\top F^*(q_1)\le0,\qquad f_\lambda(1,X)=(q_2-q_1)^\top F^*(q_2)\ge0. \quad (28)$$
- **Numerical counterexample set $\Upsilon_2$**: $\Upsilon_2=\{X\in\bar\Upsilon\mid\max_{\lambda\in[0,1]}f(\lambda,X)>0\}$ ($\bar\Upsilon$: both differentiable). Fig. 3 shows $f_\lambda(0,X)>0$ at points of $\Upsilon$ for $\alpha_2\in[0.01,0.9]$ with $\alpha_1=\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_1\in\{0.3,0.5,0.8\}$; hence $\Upsilon_2\ne\emptyset$ on a nonzero-measure parameter subset (authors state it is "more likely" true for all $\alpha_i\in(0,1)$ but "difficult to prove rigorously" — an admitted proof gap). Consequently $\bar V$ is **not** the value function. Fig. 4 additionally shows a point where (28) holds yet $\max f=0.0054>0$ at $\lambda_m=0.15$ — (28) is necessary, not sufficient.
- **Remark 5**: $J_{11}(X)>J_2(X)$, so near $\Upsilon$ the cooperative strategy beats the "selfish" one.
- **Remark 6**: the failure of $\bar V$ demonstrates why HJI-satisfaction on the smooth region alone never proves a value function — Lemma 7-type checks are indispensable.

**Lemma 8 (pp. 566–567, eq. (29))**: if $X\in\Upsilon$ and $\lambda_m\in(0,1)$ maximizes $f(\lambda,X)$, then
$$\max_{u_D}\min_{u_{A_1},u_{A_2}}\max\Big\{\frac{dJ_1(X)}{dt},\frac{dJ_2(X)}{dt}\Big\}=f(\lambda_m,X). \quad (29)$$
**Proof**: defender's optimal control has the form $u_D^\lambda=\big\langle(1-\lambda)\frac{\partial J_1}{\partial x_D}+\lambda\frac{\partial J_2}{\partial x_D}\big\rangle$; playing $u_D^{\lambda_m}$ gives $(1-\lambda_m)\frac{dJ_1}{dt}+\lambda_m\frac{dJ_2}{dt}\ge f(\lambda_m,X)$ (equality iff attackers play $u_{A_i}=-\big\langle(1-\lambda_m)\frac{\partial J_1}{\partial x_{A_i}}+\lambda_m\frac{\partial J_2}{\partial x_{A_i}}\big\rangle$), so the max over $D$ of the min over attackers of $\max\{\dot J_1,\dot J_2\}\ge f(\lambda_m,X)$; conversely attackers playing the $\lambda$-mixture against $u_D^\lambda$ holds both derivatives' convex combination at $f(\lambda,X)\le f(\lambda_m,X)$, giving $\le$. Two-sided minimax argument, local, analytic. **Meaning**: the defender can strictly increase *both* branch values on $\Upsilon_2$ — the true value satisfies $V\ge\bar V$ with strict inequality near $\Upsilon_2$, and $\Upsilon_2\subset\mathcal D_0:=\{V>\bar V\}$ (singular region), while $\mathcal D_i=\{V=J_i\}$ are the regular regions.

**Dispersal subset and its boundary**: $\Upsilon_1=\mathrm{cl}\{X\in\bar\Upsilon\mid\max_{\lambda\in[0,1]}f(\lambda,X)=0\}$ carries the viscosity condition; since $q_2-q_1$ is normal to $\Upsilon_1$ pointing to the $\bar V=J_2$ side, equilibrium trajectories leave it on both sides and the *defender* dominates the side choice — a defender's dispersal surface. Its boundary $B$ splits into
$$\mathcal B_0=\{X\in B\mid f(\lambda_m,X)=0,\ \lambda_m\in(0,1)\},\quad \mathcal B_1=\{X\in B\mid f_\lambda(0,X)=0,\ f(\lambda,X)<0\ \forall\lambda\in(0,1)\},\quad \mathcal B_2=\{X\in B\mid f_\lambda(1,X)=0,\ f(\lambda,X)<0\ \forall\lambda\in(0,1)\}.$$

### 5.12 Boundary analysis at $\mathcal B_0$ (pp. 567–568, eqs. (30)–(34))

At $X_0\in\mathcal B_0$ with $J_1,J_2$ twice continuously differentiable, three controls are equally optimal: $F^*(q_1),F^*(q_2),F^*(q_{\lambda_m})$; $\tilde H(q_{\lambda_m}(X_0))=f(\lambda_m,X_0)=0$, so $q_{\lambda_m}$ is a candidate singular-side gradient. Key relations: maximum condition (30) $f_\lambda(\lambda_m,X_0)=(q_2-q_1)^\top F^*(q_{\lambda_m})=0$ ⇒ $F^*(q_{\lambda_m})$ is tangent to $\Upsilon_1$; second-order necessary condition (31) $(q_{\lambda_m}-q_i)^\top\frac{\partial F^*}{\partial q_{\lambda_m}^\top}(q_{\lambda_m})(q_{\lambda_m}-q_i)\le0$, $i=1,2$; staying-on-$\Upsilon_1$ condition (32) $\frac{df(\lambda_m,X_0)}{dt}=(1-\lambda_m)F^{*\top}\nabla^2J_1F^*+\lambda_mF^{*\top}\nabla^2J_2F^*\le0$, whence at least one of
$$F^{*\top}(q_{\lambda_m})\nabla^2J_1(X_0)F^*(q_{\lambda_m})\le0\ (33),\qquad F^{*\top}(q_{\lambda_m})\nabla^2J_2(X_0)F^*(q_{\lambda_m})\le0\ (34).$$

### 5.13 Singular-arc ODE system (pp. 568, eqs. (35)–(36))

Along a switch envelope (= equivocal/corner surface [20]–[22]) the singular arcs obey
$$\dot X=F^*(p),\qquad \dot p=a(X,p)\,(p-q(X)) \quad (35)$$
($p$: singular-side gradient; $q(X)$: regular-side gradient), with $a$ fixed by requiring $(p-q)^\top F^*(p)=0$ along the arc; differentiating that constraint in time gives
$$a(X,p)=\frac{F^{*\top}(p)\,\frac{\partial q^\top(X)}{\partial X}\,F^*(p)}{(p-q)^\top\frac{\partial F^{*\top}(p)}{\partial p}(p-q)}. \quad (36)$$
At $\mathcal B_0$, $a(X_0,q_{\lambda_m}(X_0))\ge0$ for both candidate envelopes, from (31), (33), (34). Construction: integrate (35) in inverse time from $p=q_{\lambda_m}$ at $\mathcal B_0$ with $q=q_i$; no analytic solution (authors state the global analysis is therefore difficult).

### 5.14 Theorem 2 (p. 568) — bifurcation of the dispersal surface into two switch envelopes

**Statement.** If at $X_0\in\mathcal B_0$: (1) $J_1,J_2$ are twice continuously differentiable; (2) strict inequalities hold in (28), (31), (33), (34); (3) $f(\lambda,X_0)<0$ for all $\lambda\in(0,1)$, $\lambda\ne\lambda_m$ — then: (i) near $X_0$ there exist two switch envelopes $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1},\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ tangent to $\Upsilon_1$ at $\mathcal B_0$, each consisting of singular arcs (35) with terminal value $p=q_{\lambda_m}$ at $\mathcal B_0$ and $q(X)=q_i(X)$; (ii) $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1},\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2},\Upsilon_1$ partition the neighborhood into $\bar{\mathcal D}_i\subset\mathcal D_i$; in $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$ (between the envelopes) there is a $C^1$ solution $V_0$ of $\tilde H(\nabla V_0)=0$ with $V_0=J_i$ on $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,i}$, and locally the value is $V=\max\{V_0,\bar V\}$ with $V=V_0$ on $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$.
**Proof (6 steps, tools: Melikyan's generalized-characteristics theory [21])**: (i) strict (32) ⇒ the $F^*(q_{\lambda_m})$-trajectory leaves $\mathcal B_0$ transversely; invoke [21, Th. 1.3] for existence/uniqueness of each $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,i}$ with fields satisfying (37): $v_i=J_i$, $\nabla v_i=p_i$, $\tilde H(p_i)=0$, $(p_i-q_i)^\top F^*(p_i)=0$, $p_i=q_{\lambda_m}$ at $\mathcal B_0$; (ii) tangency: normal vectors coincide at $\mathcal B_0$ ($p_i-q_i=q_{\lambda_m}-q_i$); (iii) [21, Th. 1.6] gives a unique $C^1$ solution $V_{0,i}$ with Cauchy data $(J_i,p_i)$ on $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,i}$ whose regular trajectories (38) $\dot X=\nabla\tilde H(p_i),\dot p_i=0$ fill a half-neighborhood directed by $p_i-q_i$; the surface $\mathcal S_0$ of such trajectories through $\mathcal B_0$ carries $V_{0,1}=V_{0,2}$ (same initial gradient $q_{\lambda_m}$), forcing both solutions to live in the same strip $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$ between $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1}$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$; (iv) glue $V_0=V_{0,i}$ on $\bar{\mathcal D}_{0,i}$; $V=\max\{V_0,\bar V\}$; (v) viscosity check on $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,i}$ via $\bar f(\lambda,X)=\tilde H((1-\lambda)p_i+\lambda q_i)$: $\bar f(0)=\bar f(1)=0$, $\bar f_\lambda(0,X)=0$ by (37), $\bar f_\lambda(1,X_0)=(q_i-q_{\lambda_m})^\top F^*(q_i)>0$ by strict (28), $\frac{\partial^2\bar f}{\partial\lambda^2}(0,X)=(p_i-q_i)^\top\frac{\partial F^*}{\partial p_i^\top}(p_i-q_i)<0$ by strict (31), and assumption 3 gives $\bar f(\lambda,X_0)<0$ on $(0,1)$; continuity extends $\bar f\le0$ near $X_0$ ⇒ $V$ is a viscosity solution, hence the value near $X_0$ (uniqueness as before); (vi) since $(q_i-p_i)^\top F^*(q_i)>0$ and $(q_i-p_i)^\top F^*(p_i)=0$, trajectories approach $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,i}$ tangentially with $F(p_i)$ and leave transversely with $F(q_i)$, and $\tilde H(p_i)=p_i^\top F^*(p_i)=0$ ⇒ $q_i^\top F^*(p_i)=0$ ⇒ $V=J_i$ constant along the arc — the defining property of a switch envelope. **Scope**: local near $X_0$; analytic + ODE construction. **Remark 7**: $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1},\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2},V_0$ are uniquely determined by $J_i,\nabla J_i$ at $\mathcal B_0$, and the conclusions extend along connected subsets of $\mathcal B_0$ where the hypotheses hold. $V_0$ may be seen as the value of a reach-avoid game with terminal value $\bar V$ over terminal set $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1}\cup\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$; computationally, integrate (38) in inverse time from $\nabla V_0=p_i$ on the envelopes. Two ends of the construction: (a) negative feedback in (35) under inverse time ($a\ge0$) can shrink $p-q$ to zero so the singular arc degenerates into a regular straight trajectory (35)→(27) — observed for $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1}$ in Fig. 6; (b) both $\bar f_\lambda(0,X)$ and $\bar f_\lambda(1,X)$ can vanish, where continuing the envelope would violate the viscosity condition and the necessary condition for a **focal surface** appears (observed for $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$, Fig. 8). Focal-surface construction is explicitly left open (and generally not ODE-solvable, [31]).

### 5.15 Theorem 3 (pp. 570–571) — bifurcation into a switch envelope plus a dispersal surface

**Statement.** If at $X_0\in\mathcal B_0$: (1) $J_1,J_2$ twice continuously differentiable; (2) strict inequalities in (28), (32), (31), and (39) $F^{*\top}(q_{\lambda_m})\nabla^2J_1(X_0)F^*(q_{\lambda_m})>0$; (3) $f(\lambda,X_0)<0$ for $\lambda\in(0,1)$, $\lambda\ne\lambda_m$ — then: (i) near $X_0$ there exist a dispersal surface $\Upsilon_0$ and a switch envelope $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ tangent to $\Upsilon_1$ at $\mathcal B_0$ ($\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$: singular arcs (35), $p=q_{\lambda_m}$ at $\mathcal B_0$, $q=q_2$); (ii) in $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$ between $\Upsilon_0$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ there is a $C^1$ solution $V_0$ of the HJI equation with $V_0=J_1$ on $\Upsilon_0$ and $V_0=J_2$ on $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$; locally $V=\max\{V_0,\bar V\}$, $V=V_0$ on $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$.
**Proof (5 steps)**: (i) $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ and $\mathcal S_0$ exist as in Theorem 2; on $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ in inverse time $\tau$ ($d/d\tau=-d/dt$): $\frac{dJ_1}{d\tau}\big|_{\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}}=F^{*\top}(q_{\lambda_m})(q_2-q_1)=0$ and, differentiating using (35),
$$\frac{d^2J_1(X_0)}{d\tau^2}\Big|_{\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}}=F^{*\top}(q_{\lambda_m})\Big(\nabla^2J_1(X_0)+\frac{\lambda_m}{1-\lambda_m}\nabla^2J_2(X_0)\Big)F^*(q_{\lambda_m})<0 \quad (41)$$
by strict (32); (ii) hence $V_{0,2}=J_2\ge J_1$ on $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ near $\mathcal B_0$ while $V_{0,2}\le J_1$ on $\mathcal S_0$ near $X_0$ (from (40) $\frac{d^2J_1}{d\tau^2}\big|_{\mathcal S_0}=F^{*\top}\nabla^2J_1F^*>0$); by continuity and $\nabla V_{0,2}(X_0)\ne\nabla J_1(X_0)$ there is a unique surface $\Upsilon_0$ between $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$ and $\mathcal S_0$ with $V_{0,2}=J_1$, tangent to $\Upsilon_1$; (iii) viscosity on $\Upsilon_0$ via $\bar f(\lambda,X)=\tilde H((1-\lambda)q_1+\lambda p)$, $p=\nabla V_0$: at $\mathcal B_0$, $\bar f(\lambda,X)=f(\lambda_m\lambda,X)$, so $\bar f(0)=\bar f(1)=0$, $\bar f_\lambda(0)<0$, $\bar f_\lambda(1)=0$; (iv) the delicate step is the sign of $\bar f_\lambda(1,\cdot)$ *along* $\Upsilon_0$: following a trajectory from $X_1\in\Upsilon_0$ back to $X_0$ (straight segment in $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$ + singular arc in $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}$) and writing $J_1(X_1)-J_1(X_0)=0$ as a second-order expansion with $\kappa_0=\frac{dJ_1}{d\tau}\big|_{\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}},\ \kappa_1=\frac{d^2J_1}{d\tau^2}\big|_{\tilde\Upsilon_{0,2}},\ \kappa_2=\frac{d^2J_1}{d\tau^2}\big|_{\mathcal S_0}$, the fraction of inverse time spent on the singular arc obeys the decisive limit
$$\lim_{\Delta\tau\to0}\frac{\Delta\tau_1}{\Delta\tau}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{\kappa_1}{\kappa_2}+\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_1^2}{\kappa_2^2}-\frac{\kappa_1}{\kappa_2}}} \quad (42)$$
(verified against the rendered page); combining with $\dot p=a(X_0,q_{\lambda_m})\Delta\tau_1/\Delta\tau\,(q_2-q_{\lambda_m})$ from (35)–(36) gives $\lim_{\Delta\tau\to0}\frac{\bar f_\lambda(1,X_1)-\bar f_\lambda(1,X_0)}{\Delta\tau}=\kappa_2+\lim\frac{\Delta\tau_1}{\Delta\tau}(\kappa_1-\kappa_2)>0$, so $\bar f_\lambda(1,X)>0$ on $\Upsilon_0$ near $X_0$ and the viscosity condition holds there; (v) $\bar f_\lambda(0)<0<\bar f_\lambda(1)$ ⇒ trajectories leave $\Upsilon_0$ transversely on both sides ⇒ dispersal surface. **Scope**: local; analytic. **Caveat printed by the authors**: "According to a number of numerical simulations, this possibility may not appear in the problem under consideration. However, the results in Theorem 3 still have theoretical importance" — i.e., the $\Upsilon_0$-bifurcation is predicted but not observed in this game's parameter scans (may occur in related games).

### 5.16 Theorem 4 (p. 572) — switch envelope attached at $\mathcal B_1$

At $X_0\in\mathcal B_1$: $(q_2-q_1)^\top F^*(q_1)=0$ and $(q_2-q_1)^\top F^*(q_2)\ge0$ — the special case of $\mathcal B_0$ with $\lambda_m=0$; the switch-envelope necessary conditions hold with $q_2$ the regular-side and $q_1$ the singular-side gradient. The other formal envelope $\tilde\Upsilon_{0,1}$ degenerates to $\mathcal S_0$ because $q_{\lambda_m}=q_1$ at $\mathcal B_1$. Supporting inequalities: from the equilibrium control $F^*(q_1(X_0))$ keeping $J_1$ constant while $J_2$ does not increase,
$$\frac{d^2J_2(X_0)}{dt^2}=F^{*\top}(q_1)\nabla^2J_2(X_0)F^*(q_1)\le0\ (43),\qquad \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\lambda^2}(0,X_0)=(q_1-q_2)^\top\frac{\partial F^*(q_1)}{\partial q_1^\top}(q_1-q_2)\le0\ (44)$$
(analogous to (31)).
**Statement.** If at $X_0\in\mathcal B_1$: (1) $J_1,J_2$ twice continuously differentiable; (2) (43), (44) strict; (3) $f_\lambda(1,X_0)=(q_2-q_1)^\top F^*(q_2)>0$ — then: (i) near $X_0$ there exist a switch envelope $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ and a surface $\mathcal S_0$ tangent to $\Upsilon_1$ at $\mathcal B_1$; $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ consists of singular arcs (35) with $q(X)=q_2(X)$ and terminal $p=\nabla J_1$ at $\mathcal B_1$; $\mathcal S_0$ consists of regular trajectories (27) of $J_1$ with terminal points in $\mathcal B_1$; (ii) in $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$ between $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ and $\mathcal S_0$ there is a $C^1$ solution $V_1$ of $\tilde H(\nabla V_1)=0$ with $V_1=J_2$ on $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ and $V_1=J_1$ on $\mathcal S_0$; locally $V=\max\{V_1,\bar V\}$, $V=V_1$ on $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$; $V$ is continuously differentiable across $\mathcal S_0$. **Proof**: "in the same way as Theorem 2" (authors' statement; the tangential/transversal structure and viscosity check mirror Th. 2 with roles of $J_1$/$q_1$ swapped). A symmetric envelope $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ grows from $\mathcal B_2$ (trajectories approach tangentially from $\mathcal D_0$ and exit into $\mathcal D_1$); an analogous theorem is stated to hold. The constructions of $\tilde\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2$ must terminate near $\mathcal B_1\cap\mathcal B_2$, where trajectories are tangent to $\Upsilon_1$ from both sides — focal-surface conditions; the matching focal surfaces are left as future work.

### 5.17 Summary of the complete solution structure

Constrained scenario (Sec. IV): value $J_1$ global and explicit up to a 2-D minimization; only dispersal surfaces ($\Gamma$). General scenario (Sec. V): $V=\bar V$ on regular regions $\mathcal D_1,\mathcal D_2$ (near the terminal set) and $V=V_0$ (resp. $V_1$) on the singular region $\bar{\mathcal D}_0$ generated locally from $\mathcal B_0$ (resp. $\mathcal B_1,\mathcal B_2$) by the switch-envelope ODE (35)–(36); the defender strictly benefits from *not* committing to a predefined capture order near $\Upsilon_2$ ($V>\max\{J_1,J_2\}$), confirming the headline claim that "the defender can perform better than to capture the attackers in any predefined order". Focal surfaces closing the construction remain open.

**Proof gaps / delegated steps flagged**: (a) the exterior-product identity in Lemma 6's step (iv) is proven only in the supplementary material (p. 563); (b) $\Upsilon_2\ne\emptyset$ for *all* $\alpha_i\in(0,1)$ is asserted from numerics, "difficult to prove rigorously" (p. 566); (c) Theorems 2–4 are local ($C^2$ assumptions, strict inequalities at a boundary point) — the global assembly of $V$ over $\mathcal D_0$ is not completed; (d) Lemma 1 assumes pairwise distinct branch gradients at the singular point.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated:**
1. Focal surfaces are not constructed: "the construction of the focal surface is left as future work" (pp. 569, 571, 572); generally focal surfaces "cannot be constructed by solving an ODE system" [31]. The general game is therefore *not* solved globally — the value is only assembled locally around $\mathcal B_0,\mathcal B_1,\mathcal B_2$.
2. Global analysis of switch envelopes is difficult because (35) "cannot be solved analytically" (p. 568); termination of the envelope construction (degeneracy to straight trajectories, or focal-surface condition at the far end) is handled only by numerical observation.
3. $\Upsilon_2\ne\emptyset$ for all $\alpha_i\in(0,1)$ is supported numerically but "difficult to prove rigorously" (p. 566).
4. Theorem 3's bifurcation type (dispersal + switch envelope) "may not appear in the problem under consideration" per simulations (p. 571) — retained for theoretical generality.
5. Part of the Lemma 6 proof (the exterior-product identity) resides in supplementary material (p. 563).

**Critical observations (mine, with locators):**
6. The game-of-kind (barrier) characterization is never written explicitly; only the remark that $J\le0\iff$ attack win connects degree to kind (p. 561). Practitioners must threshold the value function themselves.
7. All numerical examples use symmetric speeds $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=0.5$ (Figs. 4, 6–12); robustness of the switch-envelope construction across asymmetric speeds is not illustrated.
8. Theorems 2–4 assume $J_i$ twice continuously differentiable at the boundary point and *strict* inequalities ((31), (33), (34), (39), (43), (44)); degenerate boundary points (equalities) are not covered, and no existence argument for such strict points is given beyond the examples.
9. The value in the singular region is characterized as $V=\max\{V_0,\bar V\}$ only in a *neighborhood* of $X_0$ (Ths. 2–4); how the patches glue globally, and whether $\mathcal D_0$ is connected, are open.
10. The information structure on $\Gamma$: the defender "can guarantee the optimal payoff only if he has access to the attacker's current controls" (p. 565); without it the loss is claimed infinitesimally small and a mixed strategy is suggested, but no formal non-anticipativity/robust-strategy analysis is provided.
11. Scope confined to 2-D half-plane target, simple motion, point capture, faster defender; extension to convex/general targets, 3-D, or slower defenders is not addressed (cf. sibling works in the library, e.g., Deng et al. 2024 ellipses tool, P-adjacent entries).

## 8. Citation Value

**Role in the literature**: the rigorous backbone for one-defender–two-attacker reach-avoid games with cooperative attackers, and a methodological exemplar of the modern viscosity-solution treatment of singular surfaces in simple-motion differential games. Three distinct citation payloads: (a) the $m$-branch viscosity Lemma 1 as a reusable certification tool for kinks of value functions; (b) Theorem 1 as the *only* fully proved value function in this game class — the canonical counterexample to the habit of proving HJI satisfaction on smooth regions and stopping (Remarks 3 and 6, $\Upsilon_2$); (c) Theorems 2–4 as the standard reference for how a dispersal surface terminates (bifurcating into switch envelopes, into switch envelope + dispersal surface, or attaching a switch envelope) with the explicit singular-arc ODE (35)–(36) for constructing switch envelopes in inverse time.

**Suggested citation sentences**: "For one-defender–two-attacker reach-avoid games, Deng et al. [P019] proved the value function of the capture-order-constrained game as the unique viscosity solution of the HJI equation, certifying the dispersal surface through the $m$-branch viscosity condition rather than trajectory arguments alone." Or: "Singular surfaces beyond dispersal — switch envelopes growing from the boundary of a defender's dispersal surface via the singular-arc ODE $\dot X=F^*(p),\dot p=a(X,p)(p-q(X))$ — were classified and locally constructed by Deng et al. [P019], who also showed that the naive value $\max\{J_1,J_2\}$ violates the viscosity condition on a nonempty set $\Upsilon_2$."
