## 2. Research Background

The paper studies global geometric features of capture-type pursuit ("Lion & Man") problems, a problem class attributed to Rado in the 1930s whose celebrated resolution for the planar disc (evader wins against the radius-tracking pursuer) is due to Besicovitch (1952), with geometric proofs by Littlewood [21] and Croft [6] and speed-ratio generalizations by Flynn [8]; more recent treatments are [20, 2, 33, 17]. Pursuit games have been studied on graphs [26], spheres [18, 32], surfaces of revolution [22, 10], Euclidean half-spaces [15], hypersurfaces [31], and compact CAT(0) spaces [1], and domain geometry has been used as a complexity parameter [27, 19]. The authors note that "necessary and sufficient capture criteria are rare, sufficiency being more common in the literature" (p. 104). On a compact domain the greedy (move-along-the-geodesic) strategy always captures, so all difficulty is created by unboundedness: the possibility of escape to infinity. The paper's declared perspective is "to incorporate the boundary of the domain as a type of stationary pursuer, whose geometry affects the success of a given pursuit strategy" (p. 106) — the boundary can corner the evader, and conversely a badly shaped boundary can defeat an otherwise winning pursuit algorithm. The enabling observation is that boundary constraints (normals set $\mathcal{N}$), escape directions (recession set $\mathcal{R}$), and evader–pursuer sightlines $[EP_j]$ all live in the same object: the unit tangent (visibility) sphere $S^{n-1}$ at the evader, where they interact through elementary spherical convexity and duality [7, 29].

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** Fix a closed, unbounded, convex Euclidean domain $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbf{E}^n$ with boundary ($\mathcal{D}$ lies in no hyperplane), one evader $E$ and $N$ pursuers $\{P_j\}_{1}^{N}$, all points, at distinct initial positions (Assumptions §1.2, p. 104). Play is in discrete time $t \in \mathbb{N}$: first the evader jumps from $E^t$ to $E^{t+1}$ within unit distance; then each pursuer jumps from $P^t_j$ to $P^{t+1}_j$ by at most a unit distance; each pursuer has perfect information about $\mathcal{D}$, $P^t_j$ and $E^{t+1}$. The evader wins iff $P^t_j \neq E^t$ for all $t$ and all $j$ (point capture, equal unit speeds, no capture radius). The goal: a necessary and sufficient condition, as a function of $(E_0, \{P^0_j\}, \mathcal{D})$ only, for the pursuers to win regardless of evader strategy (p. 105).

**Gap, as stated by the authors, and verification.**
1. [Authors' claim] For the boundaryless case $\mathcal{D} = \mathbf{E}^n$ the classical criterion — $E$ in the interior of $\mathrm{conv}\{P_j\}$ — "has been discovered and rediscovered in various contexts" [6, 11, 36, 17, 28] (p. 106). [Verified as an accurate literature summary of the cited claim; the paper does not re-prove it.]
2. [Authors' claim] Sgall [33] solved the one-pursuer quadrant problem under condition (⋆) (p. 106), and remarked that the result generalizes to any planar wedge of angle $< \pi$ and to higher-dimensional convex cones. The authors state Sgall "fails to notice" the distinction between wedges of angle $\geq \pi/2$ (where (⋆) forces the center $O \in \mathbf{Q}$ and the proof is sound) and wedges of angle $< \pi/2$ (where the center may leave the domain and the algorithm "may fail ... because it may require the pursuer to move outside the domain", p. 106). [Verified within this paper: the thin-cone counterexample of Figure 3 (p. 112) exhibits exactly such a failure.]
3. [Authors' claim] Kopparty–Ravishankar [17] extended the Radius proof to convex domains bounded by finitely many hyperplanes, claiming (Theorem 3 of [17]) capture under condition (⋆) of p. 107. The authors assert: "The application of Radius is invalid on general convex Euclidean domains (even 2-d cones), contradicting the claims of [33] and [17]" (p. 108, §2.5 item 2). [The contradiction is substantiated internally by Figure 3; it concerns the claimed generalizations — Sgall's quadrant theorem itself is not disputed.] They also provide the repair: an additional geometric hypothesis (Theorem 12) making the [17] proof valid, and a single-pursuer cone-width condition (Theorem 16).
4. [Authors' claim] "Necessary and sufficient capture criteria are rare" (p. 104). [Plausible and consistent with the cited literature: prior criteria covered only $\mathbf{E}^n$ and piecewise-linear (hyperplane-bounded) domains; no criterion existed for arbitrary convex boundaries (curved, smooth) and none was both necessary and sufficient. This is the gap the paper fills with the Boundedness Condition + RotatingRadius.]

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Theory — a dimension- and pursuit-count-independent feasibility criterion.** The *Boundedness Condition* (Def. 1, eq. (1), p. 108): the intersection $\big(\bigcap_{j=1}^N \mathbf{H}_j\big) \cap \mathcal{D}$ is bounded, where $\mathbf{H}_j$ is the closed half-space containing $E$ whose boundary hyperplane passes through $P_j$ orthogonal to $EP_j$. Four equivalent spherical formulations (Theorem 9) and necessity for *any* winning strategy (Proposition 10).
2. **Theory — correction of the prior art.** Identification (Figure 3, §4.1, pp. 111–112) of the exact failure mode of the Radius algorithm of [33, 17] on cones with angle $< \pi/2$: the required next position $P^{t+1}_j$ can lie outside $\mathcal{D}$; the "discard pursuers whose sightline leaves $\mathcal{R}$" preprocessing can destroy the Boundedness Condition or discard all pursuers. Corrected sufficient conditions (Theorems 12, 15, 16).
3. **Theory — the main theorem.** Theorem 17 (p. 115): the Boundedness Condition is necessary *and* sufficient for discrete-time equal-speed capture on any convex unbounded $\mathcal{D}$ (not merely polyhedral domains). Sufficiency is by a new algorithm, RotatingRadius (Algorithm 2, p. 116), which repairs Radius's boundary collisions by nearest-point projection onto $\mathcal{D}$ and by *rotating* (recomputing) the blocking center while keeping blocking radii bounded, proved via a set-valued Lyapunov function: a strictly decreasing nested family of "playing fields" $C^t$ (Lemma 19, eqs. (3)–(6)) plus a radial progress lemma (Lemma 18, eq. (2)). A quadratic capture-time estimate (Corollary 20, p. 119) accompanies the proof.
4. **Modeling/theory — non-convex extension.** For domains $\mathcal{D} = \bigcup_\alpha \mathcal{D}_\alpha$ that are finite unions of convex domains (Def. 21, p. 120), the *Extended Boundedness Condition* — a partition of pursuers over components such that each non-compact component satisfies the Boundedness Condition for the projected "ghost" evader $\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha(E)$ — is sufficient for capture (Theorem 22), with an accessibility-graph pruning refinement (Remark 23). The converse fails (§6.2).
5. **Tooling.** A transfer of elementary spherical convexity/duality (Danzer–Grünbaum–Klee [7]; Rockafellar [29]) into pursuit theory: recession sets, normals sets, and sightlines as dual objects on $S^{n-1}$ (Remarks 25, 24). No algorithms beyond the two pseudocode blocks; no experiments.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.1 Model, dynamics, winning conditions (§1.2–1.3, pp. 104–105)

State: $E^t, P^t_j \in \mathcal{D}$, $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbf{E}^n$ closed, unbounded, convex, with nonempty interior. Each turn: (i) evader moves $E^t \to E^{t+1}$, $|E^tE^{t+1}| \le 1$; (ii) pursuers respond, $|P^t_j P^{t+1}_j| \le 1$ (evader moves first; pursuers see $E^{t+1}$ before moving — a Stackelberg-within-period structure). Winning: evader wins iff $P^t_j \neq E^t\ \forall t, j$; pursuers win iff exact coincidence occurs at some finite $t$. Capture = point coincidence, no capture radius; speeds equal (=1). Notation (§1.3): $[PQ] := \overrightarrow{PQ}/|PQ| \in S^{n-1}$; $B(O;r)$ closed ball; a *cone with central angle* $\theta$ = union of all rays from a point making angle $\le \theta$ with a fixed ray. In §6 the motion rule changes for non-convex $\mathcal{D}$: each move must follow a rectifiable path of length $\le 1$ *in the interior geometry of* $\mathcal{D}$ (no jumping across corners).

### 5.2 Geometric machinery on the visibility sphere (§3, pp. 108–111)

- **Definition 1 (Boundedness Condition, p. 108, eq. (1))**: with $\mathbf{H}_j$ the closed half-space containing $E$ bounded by the hyperplane through $P_j$ orthogonal to $EP_j$,
  $$\text{(1)}\qquad \Big(\bigcap_{j=1}^{N} \mathbf{H}_j\Big) \cap \mathcal{D}\ \ \text{is bounded}.$$
  Physical meaning: the evader cannot be "shaded" from infinity by the family of planes through the pursuers perpendicular to the sightlines — she cannot simultaneously move away from all pursuers *and* from the boundary. No regularity beyond convexity is assumed on $\partial\mathcal{D}$.
- **Definition 2 (spherical convexity, p. 109)**: $\mathcal{A} \subset S^{n-1}$ is convex iff the cone over it, $C(\mathcal{A}) = \{v \in \mathbf{E}^n : v = \lambda x,\ x \in \mathcal{A},\ \lambda \in [0,\infty)\}$, is a convex subset of $\mathbf{E}^n$. Remark 3: great $k$-spheres (hence antipodal pairs) are convex.
- **Definition 4 (normals set, p. 109)**: $\mathcal{N} \subset S^{n-1}$ = all outer unit normals to support hyperplanes meeting $\mathcal{D}$ but not its interior. Warning preserved from [38]: $\mathcal{N}$ itself need not be convex, though $\overline{\mathcal{N}}$ is.
- **Definition 5 (duality, p. 109)**: the dual $\mathcal{A}^0$ of convex $\mathcal{A} \subset S^{n-1}$ is the set of unit vectors making angle $\geq \pi/2$ with every vector of $\mathcal{A}$; $\mathcal{A}^0$ is closed convex and $\mathcal{A}^{00} = \overline{\mathcal{A}}$ (biduality). [Note the $\geq \pi/2$ convention — verified against the printed page; using $\le \pi/2$ would break biduality here.]
- **Definition 6 (recession set, p. 109)**: $\mathcal{R} \subset S^{n-1}$ = unit directions of half-lines contained in $\mathcal{D}$ (escape directions).
- **Lemma 7 (p. 109)**: for convex $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbf{E}^n$, $(\overline{\mathcal{N}})^0 = \mathcal{R}$; equivalently $x \in \mathcal{R}$ iff $\mathcal{H}_{-x} \supset \mathcal{N}$, where $\mathcal{H}_x$ is the closed hemisphere with pole $x$. Cited to [29, p. 123, Corollary 14.2.1] ("well known and easy to prove"); global, analytic. This is the duality between boundary constraint and escape routes: $\mathcal{N} = \emptyset$ ($\mathcal{D} = \mathbf{E}^n$) $\Leftrightarrow \mathcal{R} = S^{n-1}$; $\mathcal{D}$ compact $\Leftrightarrow \mathcal{R} = \emptyset,\ \mathcal{N} = S^{n-1}$ (p. 110).
- **Lemma 8 (p. 110)**: $\mathcal{N}$ lies in a closed hemisphere of $S^{n-1}$ iff $\mathcal{D}$ is unbounded. Proof: $\overline{\mathcal{N}}$ convex nonempty lies in a closed hemisphere or equals $S^{n-1}$; the latter iff $\mathcal{R} = \emptyset$ (Lemma 7) iff $\mathcal{D}$ bounded. Decisive step: Lemma 7.
- **Theorem 9 (p. 110)** — the reformulation theorem. The following are equivalent: (1) $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbf{E}^n$ satisfies the Boundedness Condition; (2) no closed hemisphere of $S^{n-1}$ contains $\mathcal{N} \cup \{[EP_j]\}_{1}^{N}$; (3) $\mathcal{R} \subset \bigcup_{j=1}^N \mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{H}_{[EP_j]}$ (the union of open hemispheres with poles the sightlines covers the recession set); (4) the spherical convex hull of $\mathcal{N} \cup \{[EP_j]\}_{1}^N$ equals $S^{n-1}$. Proof: (1)⟺(2) by Lemma 8 applied to $\mathcal{D}$ intersected with the half-spaces of Def. 1; (2)⟺(3) by Lemma 7 (a point $x \in \mathcal{R}$ lies in some $\mathrm{int}\,\mathcal{H}_{[EP_j]}$ iff every hemisphere $\mathcal{H}_{-x} \supset \mathcal{N}$ contains some $[EP_j]$ in its interior); (2)⟺(4) by Definition 2. Specialization $\mathcal{D} = \mathbf{E}^n$: $\mathcal{N} = \emptyset$, recovering "$E \in \mathrm{int}\,\mathrm{conv}\{P_j\}$". Global, analytic, dimension-independent. This is the paper's geometric-convexity engine: **capture feasibility = the sightlines $[EP_j]$ plus the boundary normals $\mathcal{N}$ are not contained in any closed hemisphere of the visibility sphere** (equivalently: every escape direction is "covered" by some sightline).
- **Proposition 10 (p. 111)** — necessity. The Boundedness Condition is necessary for the existence of any successful pursuit strategy. Proof: if it fails, $\mathcal{N} \cup \{[EP_j]\}$ lies in a single closed hemisphere $\mathcal{H}$ (Theorem 9(2)); let $v$ be the vector dual to $\mathcal{H}$; then $v \in \mathcal{R}$ (Lemma 7, since $\mathcal{H}_{-v} \supset \mathcal{N}$), and the evader moving in direction $v$ yields an infinite trajectory that "never decreases the distance to any $P_j$ (as a trivial calculation shows)" — indeed $[EP_j] \in \mathcal{H}_{-v}$ gives $\langle [EP_j], v\rangle \le 0$, so $\frac{d}{dt}|E^0 + tv - P_j|^2 = 2\langle E^0 - P_j, v\rangle \ge 0$. Decisive fact: $v \in \mathcal{R}$ makes the ray $E^0 + tv$ remain in $\mathcal{D}$ (standard for closed convex sets, via [29]; not spelled out). Interpretation: one escape direction common to the boundary and all sightlines = evader win. Local-to-global: the condition is checked once, at $t=0$.

### 5.3 The Radius algorithm: failure and repair (§4, pp. 111–114)

- **Algorithm 1 (Radius, p. 106)** — from [33, 17]: given a fixed *center* $O$ (a point on the ray from $E$ through $P$, beyond $P$, such that the component of $\mathcal{D} - B(O;|OP|)$ containing $E$ is bounded — its existence follows from (⋆)), set $P' \leftarrow$ the point of $\overline{OE'} \cap B(P,1)$ closest to $E'$; Require $|PE'| > 1$. Under Radius the colinearity and order of the triple $(E^t, P^t_j, O_j)$ are preserved and $|O_jP^t_j|$ grows, the expanding ball $B(O_j;|O_jP^t_j|)$ squeezing the bounded component containing the evader.
- **Failure mode (§4.1, Figure 3, p. 112)**: for a thin (angle $< \pi/2$) cone the recession set $\mathcal{R}$ and the set $\mathcal{U}$ of Def. 14 do not partition $S^{n-1}$; Radius can demand $P^{t+1} \notin \mathcal{D}$. Also, the preprocessing of [17, p. 120] (discard all $P_j$ with $[EP_j] \notin \mathcal{R}$) may destroy the Boundedness Condition or discard all pursuers. Remark 11 (pp. 111–112): in high dimension with a thin $\mathcal{R}$ (minimal cone angle $\approx 0$, maximal $\approx \pi$), an adversarial evader can "zig-zag" and force boundary collisions at many/all time steps; whether induced errors accumulate is left hedged ("could presumably accumulate").
- **Theorem 12 (p. 113) — corrected version of Theorem 3 of [17]**: for any convex unbounded $\mathcal{D}$, the pursuers win if (1) the Boundedness Condition holds and (2) $[EP_j] \in \mathcal{R}$ for all $j$. Proof: (2) implies each center $O_j \in \mathcal{D}$ and $P^{t+1}_j = \mathsf{Radius}(P^t_j, E^{t+1}, O_j) \in \mathcal{D}$ (it lies on segment $\overline{O_jE^{t+1}} \subset \mathcal{D}$); maintained colinearity/order of $(E^t, P^t_j, O_j)$ keeps the evader inside the bounded set of eq. (1); the angle $\angle O_jP^t_jP^{t+1}_j$ is obtuse and $|P^t_jP^{t+1}_j| = 1$, so the Law of Cosines gives the decisive unnumbered inequality (p. 113):
  $$|O_jP^{t+1}_j|^2 > |O_jP^t_j|^2 + 1,$$
  forcing capture in finite time. Global; analytic; exact. Extensibility: this is the template argument reused for RotatingRadius. *Proof gap (minor)*: the containment "the evader must remain for all $t$ within the (bounded!) domain of (1) in Definition 1" is asserted from colinearity/order without a written argument (the half-space $\mathbf{H}_j$ of the *initial* configuration and the moving support sphere of $B(O_j;\cdot)$ are only tangent at the current $P^t_j$; the rigorous bridge — nested bounded components $C^t$ of $\mathcal{D} - \bigcup_j B^t_j$ — is developed only in §5.3 for RotatingRadius). Likewise the colinearity/order maintenance itself is inherited from [17] without reproduction.
- **Remark 13 (p. 113)**: pursuers may be discarded *ab initio* provided the Boundedness Condition still holds for the remainder.
- **Definition 14 (p. 114)**: $\mathcal{U}$ = union of all closed hemispheres of $S^{n-1}$ containing $\mathcal{N}$. By Theorem 9, the single-pursuer Boundedness Condition reads $[EP] \notin \mathcal{U}$.
- **Lemma 15 (p. 114)**: single pursuer; if $\mathcal{R} \cup \mathcal{U} = S^{n-1}$, the pursuer wins following Radius whenever the Boundedness Condition holds. Proof: $[EP] \notin \mathcal{U} \Rightarrow [EP] \in \mathcal{R} \Rightarrow$ Theorem 12. One line; the content is the covering hypothesis.
- **Theorem 16 (p. 114) — cone-width guarantee**: single pursuer; if $\mathcal{D}$ contains a cone of central angle at least $\pi/4$, the Boundedness Condition guarantees capture via Radius. Proof: a cone of central angle $\pi/4$ in $\mathcal{D}$ gives $\mathcal{R} \supset$ spherical disk of radius $\pi/4$ about some $v$; by duality $\mathcal{N} \subset$ disk of radius $\pi/4$ about $-v$; hence $\mathcal{U} \supset$ disk of radius $3\pi/4$ about $-v$ (any $y$ with $\angle(y,-v) \le 3\pi/4$ admits a valid hemisphere pole within $\pi/4$ of $-v$); since every point of $S^{n-1}$ is then within $\pi/4$ of $v$ or within $3\pi/4$ of $-v$, $\mathcal{R} \cup \mathcal{U} = S^{n-1}$, and Lemma 15 finishes. The constant $\pi/4$ is exactly the covering threshold of this argument; analytic, global, sharp within this method. Geometric-convexity tools recorded: **cones (central angle), spherical disks and hemispheres, duality $\mathcal{N} \leftrightarrow \mathcal{R}$, and hemisphere-union set $\mathcal{U}$** — not Apollonius/eccentricity machinery.

### 5.4 Main theorem and RotatingRadius (§5, pp. 115–119)

- **Theorem 17 (MAIN THEOREM, p. 115)**: *Discrete-time equal-speed capture on a convex domain $\mathcal{D}$ is achievable if and only if the initial positions of the pursuers and evader satisfy the Boundedness Condition.* Necessity: Proposition 10. Sufficiency: Algorithm RotatingRadius (discarding first those $P_j$ with $[EP_j] \in \mathcal{N}$ — Boundedness-preserving by Theorem 9, since such sightlines lie in $\mathcal{N}$ and contribute nothing to the hull; at least one pursuer always survives because $\mathcal{N}$ lies in a closed hemisphere, Lemma 8). Applies to arbitrary convex $\mathcal{D}$ (any boundary shape), any $N$, any dimension. This is the characterization promised in the abstract.
- **Algorithm 2 (RotatingRadius, p. 116)**, signature $(P',O') = \mathsf{RotatingRadius}(P, E', O, \mathcal{D})$, Require $|PE'| > 1$: (1) $P'' \leftarrow \mathsf{Radius}(P,E',O)$; (2) if $P'' \in \mathcal{D}$, set $P' \leftarrow P''$, $O' \leftarrow O$ (no boundary contact: fixed center, concentric growing blocking ball); (3) else $P^* \leftarrow$ nearest point of $\mathcal{D}$ to $P''$ (so $P^* \in \partial\mathcal{D}$, $|PP^*| < 1$), $P' \leftarrow$ the point of $\overline{P^*E'} \cap B(P,1)$ closest to $E'$, and $O' \leftarrow$ the point on the ray $\overrightarrow{E'P'}$ with $|E'O'| = |E'O|$ — the *center rotates* to keep the new blocking ball containing the old intersection with $\mathcal{D}$ while its radius stays controlled. Per turn the update is $(P^{t+1}_j, O^{t+1}_j) = \mathsf{RotatingRadius}(P^t_j, E^{t}, O^t_j, \mathcal{D})$ as printed in §5.1 (p. 115) — note $E^t$ is printed where $E^{t+1}$ is clearly meant (the evader has already moved; Algorithm 2's Require $|PE'|>1$ and the proof of Lemma 18 both use $E' = E^{t+1}$): a typo.
- **Lemma 18 (radial progress, p. 115)**: if $P^{t+1}_j \neq E^{t+1}$ then
  $$|O^t_jP^t_j|^2 + 1 < |O^{t+1}_jP^{t+1}_j|^2 .$$
  Proof (p. 116): with $P = P^t_j$, $|EE'| \le 1$; the point $P'' \in \overline{E'O}$ at distance 1 from $P$ nearest $E'$ has $\angle OPP''$ obtuse, so Law of Cosines: $|OP|^2 + 1 < |OP''|^2$. If $P'' \in \mathcal{D}$, done. Else let $P^*$ = nearest point of $\mathcal{D}$ to $P''$ and $\mathbf{H}^*$ the half-space containing $\mathcal{D}$ bounded by the support hyperplane at $P^*$ orthogonal to $P''P^*$; then $E' \in \mathbf{H}^*$, $\angle E'P^*P'' \ge \pi/2$, hence $|P^*E'| < |P''E'|$; with $|E'O'| = |E'O|$ the decisive chain eq. (2) (p. 116):
  $$\text{(2)}\qquad |O'P'|^2 > |O'P^*|^2 > |OP''|^2 > |OP|^2 + 1 .$$
  Local (per pursuer, per step); analytic; exact. *Proof gap (minor)*: the first inequality $|O'P'|^2 > |O'P^*|^2$ is justified only implicitly (all of $E',P',P^*,O'$ are colinear with $P'$ between $E'$ and $P^*$ and $O'$ on the ray $\overrightarrow{E'P'}$ at distance $|E'O|$; the step additionally requires $|E'P^*| \le |E'O'|$, which is not stated or proven).
- **Lemma 19 (decreasing playing field, p. 116)**: with $B^t_j = B(O^t_j, |O^t_jP^t_j|)$ and $C^t$ the component of $\big(\mathcal{D} - \bigcup_j B^t_j\big)$ containing $E^t$,
  $$\mathrm{cl}\,C^{t+1} \subset C^t \quad \text{for every } t.$$
  $C^t$ is a **set-valued Lyapunov function** for the pursuit. Proof (pp. 116–118): concentric case ($O = O'$) is Lemma 18; otherwise prove the nestedness eq. (3): $(B^{t+1} \cap \mathcal{D}) \supset (B^t \cap \mathcal{D})$, reduced via eq. (4) $(B \cap \mathcal{D}) \supset (B'' \cap \mathcal{D})$, eq. (5) $(B'' \cap \mathcal{D}) \subset \mathbf{H}'$, and eq. (6) $(B'' \cap \mathbf{H}^*) \subset \mathbf{H}'$ ($\mathbf{H}'$ = half-space containing $O'$ bounded by the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{OO'}$). Verification of (6): reduce to the 2-plane $T$ through $O, O', E'$ orthogonal to the bounding hyperplanes of $\mathbf{H}'$ and $\mathbf{H}^*$ (membership in $\mathbf{H}',\mathbf{H}^*$ depends only on the $T$-projection, and balls are centered on $T$); in the plane, $S \cap S''$ ($S = \partial B$, $S'' = \partial B''$, equal radii $|OP''|$, centers equidistant from $E'$) is two points on the bisecting line; $\angle E'P^*P''$ obtuse gives $|E'P^*| < |E'P''|$, so $|O'P^*| > |OP''|$, i.e. $P^* \notin B$; $P^* \in \mathbf{H}'$ and $(B\cap\mathbf{H}') \supset (B''\cap\mathbf{H}')$ put $P^*$ outside $B \cup B''$, so the segment $P^*P''$ (except endpoint $P''$) leaves $B \cup B''$, whence $B'' \cap \mathbf{H}^*$ cannot cross the bounding line of $\mathbf{H}'$ ("the intersection point with the bounding line of $\mathbf{H}^*$ is separated from $S \cap S''$ by the intersection point with $\overline{P^*P''}$", p. 118; Figure 6). *Proof gap (compression, not error)*: the final planar separation argument and the symmetry reduction to $T$ are stated in one breath; both are reconstructible but neither is written out in full.
- **Proof of Theorem 17 (p. 118), eq. (7)**: pick $Q_j \in (B^0_j \cap \mathcal{D})$; by eq. (3) $Q_j \in (B^t_j \cap \mathcal{D})$ for all $t$; since $Q_j \in \mathcal{D}$ and $\mathbf{H}^{t*}_j$ is a supporting half-space, $Q_j \in \mathbf{H}^{t*}_j$, and eq. (6) gives $Q_j \in \mathbf{H}^{t+1}_j$ for all $t$, so $|Q_jO^t_j|$ is nonincreasing in $t$; Lemma 19 bounds $|Q_jE^t|$ uniformly; hence $|O^t_jE^t|$ is uniformly bounded. But Lemma 18 iterated gives the decisive double inequality:
  $$\text{(7)}\qquad |O^t_jE^t|^2 \ \ge\ |O^t_jP^t_j|^2 \ \ge\ |O^0_jP^0_j|^2 + t ,$$
  (first inequality: maintained colinearity puts $P^t_j$ on segment $\overline{O^t_jE^t}$; second: telescoping Lemma 18). The right side diverges while the left is bounded — contradiction unless capture occurs. Global; exact; constructive (algorithmic proof).
- **Corollary 20 (quadratic estimate, p. 119)**: under the Boundedness Condition, if $Q_j \in (B^0_j \cap \mathcal{D})$, capture occurs by time
  $$t \;<\; \min_j\Big[\big(|Q_jO^0_j| + \max\{|Q_jx| : x \in C^0_j\}\big)^2 - |O^0_jP^0_j|^2\Big],$$
  where $C^0_j$ is printed with subscript $j$ although §5.3 defines a single component $C^0$ (minor notational inconsistency; the intended set is the bounded initial component). Proof chain (p. 119): $|O^0_jP^0_j|^2 + t < |O^t_jP^t_j|^2 \le (|O^t_jQ_j| + |Q_jP^t_j|)^2 \le \big(|Q_jO^0_j| + \max_{x \in C^0_j}|Q_jx|\big)^2$ — first from eq. (7), last from the nonincreasing $|Q_jO^t_j|$ and Lemma 19. This mirrors Sgall's sharp quadratic bound in $|OP|$ for the quadrant [33] (cited p. 106) and Kopparty–Ravishankar's setting [17]. The bound is analytic, global, and of order (initial field diameter)$^2$.

### 5.5 Non-convex domains (§6, pp. 119–121)

- **Model change (§6.1, p. 119)**: $\mathcal{D} = \bigcup_\alpha \mathcal{D}_\alpha$, each $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ convex with boundary; the union need be neither disjoint, nor non-compact, nor full-dimensional per component; moves are paths of interior length $\le 1$ (no jumping across corners).
- **Definition 21 (p. 120)**: $\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha : \mathbf{E}^n \to \mathcal{D}_\alpha$ nearest-point projection (well-defined, $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ convex). *Extended Boundedness Condition*: pursuers partition into nonempty collections $\{P_{\alpha j_\alpha} \in \mathcal{D}_\alpha,\ 1 \le j_\alpha \le N_\alpha\}$ such that for each non-compact $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ the configuration $(\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha(E), \{P_{\alpha j_\alpha}\})$ satisfies the Boundedness Condition; for compact $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ it merely demands one designated pursuer inside.
- **Theorem 22 (p. 120)**: the Extended Boundedness Condition is *sufficient* to ensure discrete-time equal-speed capture on $\mathcal{D}$. Proof: $\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha$ is distance non-increasing, so the ghost $\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha(E)$ moves at most unit steps; each group plays RotatingRadius on $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ against the ghost (compact components: fix $O^0_{j_\alpha} = P^0_{j_\alpha}$ and "continue as in Radius" — radial progress eq. (7) plus compactness of $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ yields capture of the ghost); a group that has captured its ghost but with $\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha(E) \neq E$ thereafter shadows it, $P^t_{\alpha j_\alpha} = \mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha(E^t)$ (legal: $\mathcal{D}_\alpha$ convex makes the segment interior-feasible). All ghosts are eventually captured; since $E \in \mathcal{D} = \bigcup_\alpha \mathcal{D}_\alpha$, at least one $\alpha$ has $\mathrm{PROJ}_\alpha(E) = E$, and that ghost's captor has captured $E$. Sufficiency only; the convex-decomposition structure substitutes for convexity of the whole domain.
- **Remark 23 (graph pruning, p. 121)**: define $C^t_\alpha$ per component and call $C^t_\alpha, C^t_{\alpha'}$ *accessible* iff interior distance in $\mathcal{D}$ between them is $\le 1$; the graph $\Gamma_t$ (vertices $V_\alpha$ ↔ components, edges ↔ accessible pairs) loses edges monotonically by Lemma 19, so designated pursuers on components disconnected in $\Gamma_t$ from every component containing $E^t$ can be discarded online.
- **§6.2 (fewer pursuers, p. 121)**: the Extended Boundedness Condition depends on the chosen decomposition ("an infelicitous choice (too many components) leads to an excessive lower bound on the number of pursuers"); and — in sharp contrast with the convex case — the analogue of Proposition 10 fails: failure of the Extended Boundedness Condition for one decomposition (or even for all convex decompositions) does *not* imply capture is impossible.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated:**
1. **Discrete time, point capture, perfect information, initial all-to-all communication** (Remark 27, pp. 122–123): the necessary and sufficient conditions "have the stringent assumptions of perfect evader location and domain information, as well as initial all-to-all communication between pursuers (to initialize centers $O_j$)". The proposed relaxation to a compact convex location uncertainty set $\mathfrak{E}$ with non-expanding uncertainty ("it is *perhaps possible* to reprove the Main Theorem by ... having each pursuer chase after the point of $\mathfrak{E}$ closest to the pursuer's center") is explicitly conjectural; limited-communication variants are left as challenges connected to distributed consensus [35].
2. **Multiple evaders** (Remark 26, p. 122): only the obvious necessary condition (Boundedness Condition per evader w.r.t. the full pursuer set, via Prop. 10) and obvious sufficient condition (a pursuer partition making each pair satisfy the condition, played in parallel) are given; strategies with trading of "ownership" of an evader "remain an important and interesting challenge".
3. **General (non-convex, non-decomposable) domains** (§6 intro, p. 119; Remark 24, p. 121): "the prospects for extending Theorem 17 to arbitrary Euclidean domains seem dim"; the core difficulty is "a potentially intricate, high-dimensional boundary"; "there is seemingly no hope of adapting differential game-theoretic methods to such problems ... and we are left with geometry as a recourse".
4. **Non-convex case is one-sided** (§6.2, p. 121): Theorem 22 is sufficiency only; the Extended Boundedness Condition is decomposition-dependent and not necessary for capture.
5. **Robustness of Radius near boundaries is hedged** (Remark 11, pp. 111–112): boundary collisions forced by a zig-zagging evader "could presumably accumulate" — a conjecture, not a theorem, about approximate/noisy implementations.
6. **Efficiency** (p. 105): the underlying greedy philosophy is "efficacious if not efficient"; only worst-case quadratic bounds are given, no optimality of capture time.

**Critical observations (this archive, with page/eq. anchors):**
- The model is discrete-time with the evader moving first and point capture; no capture radius and no continuous-time limit is discussed, so the results do not transfer verbatim to classical continuous-time Lion & Man (where on the disc the evader wins) or to HJI/barrier-based reach-avoid analysis; the paper's own comparison set is [6, 8, 17, 20, 21, 33].
- *Proof gap (minor)* in Theorem 12 (p. 113): the assertion that the evader remains for all $t$ inside the bounded set of eq. (1) is made from maintained colinearity/order of $(E^t, P^t_j, O_j)$ without a written argument; the rigorous mechanism (nested bounded components $C^t$) appears only later, for RotatingRadius, in §5.3.
- *Proof gap (minor)* in Lemma 18: chain (2) relies on the unstated bound $|E'P^*| \le |E'O'|$ for its first inequality (p. 116).
- Lemma 19's proof (pp. 117–118, eqs. (3)–(6)) compresses the symmetry reduction to the 2-plane $T$ and the final line-separation argument into single sentences; correct in reconstruction but far below journal-standard explicitness.
- Typographical: §5.1 (p. 115) prints the update $\mathsf{RotatingRadius}(P^t_j, E^t, O^t_j, \mathcal{D})$ where $E^{t+1}$ is meant; Corollary 20 writes $C^0_j$ vs. the $C^t$ of §5.3.
- The Boundedness Condition is checked only at $t = 0$; the paper never claims (nor needs) that the condition is preserved along the RotatingRadius trajectory, but readers equating it with a state-constraint invariant should note the live object along play is the nested family $C^t$ (Lemma 19), not eq. (1).
- Theorem 16's hypothesis ($\mathcal{D}$ *contains* a $\pi/4$-cone) is about the domain's widest opening, orthogonal in spirit to Sgall's $\pi/2$ wedge dichotomy about the corner geometry at the center; the two thresholds are easily conflated.

## 8. Citation Value

**Role**: the mathematical-foundations citation for pursuit/capture feasibility on *unbounded* domains. It contributes (i) the recession-set/visibility-sphere formalism that unifies boundary normals, escape directions, and pursuer sightlines; (ii) the sharp (necessary *and* sufficient) Boundedness Condition for equal-speed discrete capture on arbitrary unbounded convex domains (Thms. 9 + 17); (iii) the canonical counterexample showing that naive radius/lifeline-style pursuit fails near non-polyhedral or narrow boundaries (Fig. 3), with the corrected sufficient conditions (Thms. 12, 15, 16); and (iv) the RotatingRadius algorithm with a set-valued Lyapunov (nested blocking balls) proof and a quadratic capture-time bound. For the reach-avoid/lifeline/perimeter-defense literature in this library it is the natural authority for statements of the form "on unbounded domains, capture feasibility is a recession-set geometry question, not merely a speed-ratio question", and for the caution that boundary-collision handling is where naive geometric pursuit algorithms break. Suggested citation sentences:

- "Feasibility of equal-speed capture on an unbounded convex domain is characterized exactly by the recession-set condition of Alexander, Bishop and Ghrist [P001]: capture is possible if and only if the boundary normals and the evader–pursuer sightlines are not contained in any closed hemisphere of the visibility sphere (their Thms. 9 and 17), i.e. if and only if the evader cannot move away from the domain boundary and all pursuers simultaneously."
- "Because pursuit strategies inherited from bounded domains can fail on unbounded ones — the Radius algorithm may commandeer positions outside the playing field near narrow boundaries [P001, Fig. 3] — we verify our barrier construction against the Boundedness Condition of [P001] before invoking capture."
