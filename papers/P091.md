## 2. Research Background

Reach–avoid games are a canonical subclass of two-team differential games in which attackers try to reach a protected target line/region while defenders try to capture them first. The HJI approach solves such games exactly only in low dimension and becomes computationally intractable as dimension grows, so the community has developed geometric alternatives — Apollonius circles, Voronoi partitions, dominance regions — that partition the state space into a defensive advantage region (defenders guarantee capture) and an offensive advantage region (attacker guarantees T-reach), separated by a barrier. Most existing analytical results assume point capture (zero capture radius) and/or homogeneous, equal speeds. This paper works out the geometric barrier theory for the practically important case in which defenders carry positive, heterogeneous capture radii ρ_i and all players have heterogeneous maximum speeds, so that the game splits into three regimes indexed by the speed ratio α_ji = v*_{Aj}/v*_{Di} (<1, >1, =1), each requiring a different curve family (offset-Apollonius curves, Cartesian ovals, hyperbola branches).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** N defenders (capture radii ρ_i, max speeds v*_{Di}) versus M ≤ N attackers (max speeds v*_{Aj}) in a compact domain Ω ⊂ R² whose top edge is the target line T = {(x,y) ∈ Ω | y = n} with corner vertices E(0,n), F(m,n). Attackers win if at least l (0 < l ≤ M) of them reach T uncaptured; defenders win by capturing M−l+1 attackers. The paper seeks (i) closed-form barriers partitioning Ω into winning regions for all three speed-ratio regimes under nonpoint capture, and (ii) optimal target points and optimal (straight-line) strategies for both teams under payoff functions defined on each region.

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Theory (α_{ji} ≤ 1, Scenario 1 & 3).** An advantage-region method that constructs fully analytical barriers for reach–avoid games with defender capture radii: a three-curve barrier for one faster defender vs. one slower attacker (Theorem 1) and a five-curve barrier for two heterogeneous cooperating defenders vs. one attacker (Theorem 2); the equal-speed case collapses to a two-curve hyperbola-branch barrier (Theorem 6). Compared to [30], which only handles faster defenders, all speed ratios are covered.
2. **Theory (α_{ji} > 1, Scenario 2).** For a faster attacker, winning regions are built from per-defender *defensive advantage angles* γ_i obtained from a Cartesian oval of simultaneous arrival (Theorem 3), a case classification of the attacker's optimal target point against one defender (Theorem 4), and an *absolute advantage angle* Φ_j for a coalition of slower defenders with a sufficient coverage condition (Theorem 5, eq. (19)) guaranteeing cooperative interception despite the speed disadvantage.
3. **Modeling + strategies (nonpoint capture).** A termination-set/winning-condition formalization with an l-out-of-M threshold (eqs. (2)–(4)) and position-dependent payoffs J_{j,1} (5)/J_{j,2} (7) that generalize the distance-based payoff of [19] along three axes (radii, heterogeneous ratios α_{ji} and β_{pq}, multidefender cooperation — Remark 3). Optimal target points and straight-line optimal strategies are derived in the defensive region (Lemma 1) and existence/uniqueness of the offensive optimal target point is proved (Theorem 7), reducible to a quartic solvable by Ferrari's method (Remark 14). Integration with the assignment algorithm of [2] yields a complete N-vs-M pipeline.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

### 5.0 Model, information, payoffs, termination (Sec. II, pp. 2–3)

- **Dynamics, eq. (1)**: simple motion, ẋ_{Di} = v_{Di} cos φ_{Di}, ẏ_{Di} = v_{Di} sin φ_{Di} (identically for attackers), heading φ ∈ [−π, π) changeable at any time (no turn-rate limit); controls u_{Di} = {v_{Di}, φ_{Di}}. Both sides play maximum speed v*_{Di}, v*_{Aj} (standard assumption, citing [33], [34]); speed-dependent costs acknowledged as out of scope. State x̃ = (x_{D1},…,x_{DN}, x_{A1},…,x_{AM}) ∈ R^{2(N+M)}; N ≥ M (Remark 1: if N < M the problem becomes attacker prioritization, not barrier construction).
- **Information structure**: perfect, instantaneous knowledge of own and all others' positions/speeds and of Ω; no disturbances; strategies are state feedback (u_{Aj}(·), u_{Di}(·) in eqs. (6), (8)). Assignment: binary χ_{ij}, Σ_{j=1}^M χ_{ij} ≤ 1 (each defender pursues ≤ 1 attacker; cooperative 2-vs-1 pursuit allowed); captured defenders become stationary.
- **Ratios**: attacker-to-defender α_{ji} = v*_{Aj}/v*_{Di} (Scenarios 1/2/3: <1, >1, =1); defender-to-defender β_{pq} = v*_{Dp}/v*_{Dq}. Constant ratios assumed "to permit analytical barrier solutions" (p. 3).
- **Termination set, eq. (2)**: T = {∩_{C(M,M−l+1)} T_{D_j}} ∪ {∩_{C(M,l)} T_{A_j}}, with T_{D_j} (eq. (3)) = {x̃ | ∃i, τ_i = 1 s.t. ∥x_{Di} − x_{Aj}∥₂ = ρ_i and y_{Aj} ≠ n} (capture before T-reach) and T_{A_j} (eq. (4)) = {x̃ | x_{Aj} ∈ [0,m], y_{Aj} = n} (safe T-reach); C(M,·) are combinations encoding "at least l reach / at least M−l+1 captured".
- **Payoffs and values**: offensive region — J_{j,1} (eq. (5)) = ∥x_{Di}(t_{j,1}) − x_{Aj}(t_{j,1})∥₂ − ρ_i (defender–attacker distance when A_j hits T, minus own radius), value V_{j,1} (eq. (6)) = max_{u_{Aj}} min_{u_{Di}} J_{j,1}; defensive region — J_{j,2} (eq. (7)) = ∥x_{Aj}(t_{j,2}) − T_j∥₂ (distance of the capture point from T), value V_{j,2} (eq. (8)) = max_{u_{Di}} min_{u_{Aj}} J_{j,2}. Remark 3: when ρ_i = 0 and one defender, (5) reduces to the payoff of [19]; the extensions are −ρ_i, heterogeneous α_{ji}/β_{pq}, and multidefender cooperation.
- **Simultaneous-arrival curves (p. 4)**: without radii, ∥S^c_j − x⁰_{Aj}∥₂ = v*_{Aj}t_f = α_{ji}v*_{Di}t_f = α_{ji}∥S^c_j − x⁰_{Di}∥₂ (Apollonius); with radius ρ_i > 0, the interception-tie point S′_j satisfies (1/α_{ji})∥S′_j − x⁰_{Aj}∥₂ = ∥S′_j − x⁰_{Di}∥₂ − ρ_i. The locus is the curve C(D_i, A_j); its interior is where A_j reaches freely; for two defenders the attackable region is C_{A_j,inner}(D_p, D_q, A_j) = intersection of the two curves. This (1/α)·(distance) − distance = ρ structure — a weighted difference of distances equal to a constant — is the paper's workhorse; all barriers below are its consequences.

### 5.1 Theorem 1 (pp. 4–5): barrier, one faster defender vs. one slower attacker

**Statement.** If v*_{Aj} < v*_{Di} and x⁰_{Di} ∈ Ω, the barrier B(x⁰_{Di}) consists of three adjacent curves (eq. (11)):
- B₁(x⁰_{Di}) = {x=(x,y) | ∥x⁰_{Di} − E∥₂ = ∥x−E∥₂/α_{ji} + ρ_i, x ∈ [0, c₁]};
- B₂(x⁰_{Di}) = {x | (1/α²_{ji})(f−x)² + (1/α²_{ji})(n−y)² + (2ρ_i/α_{ji})√((f−x)²+(n−y)²) + ρ²_i − (f−x⁰_{Di})² − (n−y⁰_{Di})² = 0, x ∈ (c₁, c₂)};
- B₃(x⁰_{Di}) = {x | ∥x⁰_{Di} − F∥₂ = ∥x−F∥₂/α_{ji} + ρ_i, x ∈ [c₂, m]},

where f denotes the abscissa s*_j of the optimal target point S*_j = (s*_j, n) (a function f(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Di}, y⁰_{Di}, α_{ji}, n, ρ_i) of six variables) and c₁, c₂ are determined by curve intersections. Convention (p. 4): x⁰_{Aj} ⩾ x⁰_{Di} and s*_j ⩾ x⁰_{Aj} (the optimal target lies on the attacker's side).

**Proof approach.** (1) Reverse method (Remark 4): assume the attacker reaches T and characterize the barrier as attacker positions for which the attackable region meets T in exactly one point (tangency at S*_j). (2) Tangency/simultaneous-arrival condition eq. (12): ∥x⁰_{Di} − S*_j∥₂ = ∥x⁰_{Aj} − S*_j∥₂/α_{ji} + ρ_i. (3) First-order condition on the payoff surrogate F(s_j) (eq. (9)) = ∥S_j − x⁰_{Di}∥₂ − ∥S_j − x⁰_{Aj}∥₂/α_{ji} − ρ_i, i.e., F′(s*_j)=0 gives eq. (10): (s*_j − x⁰_{Di})/∥S_j − x⁰_{Di}∥₂ = (s*_j − x⁰_{Aj})/(α_{ji}∥S_j − x⁰_{Aj}∥₂). (4) Decisive intermediate equation: combining (10) with (12), (s*_j − x⁰_{Di})(α²_{ji}∥x⁰_{Di} − S*_j∥₂² − α²_{ji}ρ²_i) = (s*_j − x⁰_{Aj})∥x⁰_{Di} − S*_j∥₂², whence s*_j = f(·) and substitution into (12) squared yields the implicit B₂. (5) Endpoint segments by contradiction: if ∥x⁰_{Di} − E∥₂ > ∥x⁰_{Aj} − E∥₂/α_{ji} + ρ_i the attackable region cuts T in infinitely many points (attacker wins); if <, in none; equality is B₁; B₃ symmetric at F. Local construction, global partition (analytic, closed form).

**Physical meaning / heterogeneity effect.** Each barrier arc is an "Apollonius circle inflated by ρ_i": the defender must only get within ρ_i of the attacker, so the offensive region Ω_A (above the barrier, adjacent to T) shrinks exactly by the radius effect. Remark 6 (quantitative reduction): Theorem 1 extends [18, Lemma 1] (Wu et al., TCNS 2024, point capture); for ρ_i = 0 the extra terms (2ρ_i/α_{ji})√((f−x)²+(n−y)²) + ρ²_i vanish and the hyperbolic segments of [18] are recovered; for ρ_i > 0 the segments are "irregular curves". Proof gap (minor, p. 5): the contradiction step for B₁ ("this implies s*_j < 0, which is not possible") is terse and the phrase "f is a polynomial constant with six variables" is loose — s*_j is in fact only implicitly defined via a quartic (see (21), Remark 14), not a polynomial constant.

### 5.2 Theorem 2 (pp. 5–6): barrier, two heterogeneous faster defenders vs. one attacker

**Statement.** Given x⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq} ∈ Ω, the barrier B(x⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq}) consists of five adjacent curves: B₁ = {∥x⁰_{Dp} − E∥₂ = ∥x−E∥₂/α_{jp} + ρ_p, x ∈ [0, c₃)}; B₂ = same implicit family as Theorem 1's B₂ with (f₁, ρ_p, α_{jp}, D_p), x ∈ (c₃, c₄); B₃ = {∥x⁰_{Dp} − S_k∥₂ = ∥x−S_k∥₂/α_{jp} + ρ_p, x ∈ [c₄, c₅]}; B₄ = B₂-family with (f₂, ρ_q, α_{jq}, D_q), x ∈ (c₅, c₆); B₅ = {∥x⁰_{Dq} − F∥₂ = ∥x−F∥₂/α_{jq} + ρ_q, x ∈ [c₆, m]}; c₃–c₆ from curve intersections.

**Key cooperative equation, eq. (13)**: if D_p and D_q depart simultaneously to reach the target-line point S_k = (s_k, n), ∥x⁰_{Dp} − S_k∥₂ = β_{pq}∥x⁰_{Dq} − S_k∥₂ + ρ_p − β_{pq}ρ_q; dividing by v*_{Dp} this reads (∥x⁰_{Dp} − S_k∥ − ρ_p)/v*_{Dp} = (∥x⁰_{Dq} − S_k∥ − ρ_q)/v*_{Dq} — equal *effective capture times* (travel minus own radius, over speed), the exact heterogeneous generalization of the rendezvous condition. s_k = g(x⁰_{Dp}, y⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq}, y⁰_{Dq}, n, β_{pq}, ρ_p, ρ_q) ("polynomial constant with eight variables", again loosely stated — actually the root of a quadratic).

**Proof approach.** (1) For x⁰_{Aj} on the barrier, C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq}) ∩ T = {S*_j} and the optimal attacker path is straight. (2) The critical middle segment B₃: both defenders capture simultaneously at S*_j, giving eq. (14): ∥x⁰_{Aj} − S*_j∥₂ = α_{jp}∥x⁰_{Dp} − S*_j∥₂ − α_{jp}ρ_p = α_{jq}∥x⁰_{Dq} − S*_j∥₂ − α_{jq}ρ_q (eq. (12) multiplied through by α per defender). (3) Hence S*_j = S_k and ∥x⁰_{Aj} − S_k∥₂ = α_{jp}∥x⁰_{Dp} − S_k∥₂ − α_{jp}ρ_p — the attacker positions whose critical target is the defenders' rendezvous point. (4) Partition points: intersections of B₃ with B(x⁰_{Dp}) (giving c₄, where s_k = s*_j = f(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}, y⁰_{Dp}, α_{jp}, n, ρ_p)) and with B(x⁰_{Dq}) (c₅); remaining segments by the Theorem 1 argument. Analytic, local-to-global as in Theorem 1.

**Heterogeneity effect.** The radii difference ρ_p − β_{pq}ρ_q in (13) shifts the rendezvous point S_k toward the weaker defender (slower or smaller-radius); Remark 7: with ρ_p = ρ_q = 0 the construction reduces to the two-defender barrier of [18] (straight-line and hyperbolic segments). No proof gap beyond the same loose "polynomial constant" phrasing; implicit assumption that D_p is the left-assigned and D_q the right-assigned defender (B₁–B₃ tied to D_p, B₄–B₅ to D_q) is not stated explicitly.

### 5.3 Theorem 3 (pp. 6–7): Cartesian oval and defensive advantage angle (α_{ji} > 1)

**Statement.** When the attacker is faster, the defender–attacker simultaneous-arrival set is the Cartesian oval O_i (eq. (15)): x = x_{Aj} + η_j cos φ_{ji}, y = y_{Aj} + η_j sin φ_{ji}, with φ_{ji} = ω_{ji} + θ, θ ∈ [−θ_{ji}, θ_{ji}], ω_{ji} = arctan((y_{Di} − y_{Aj})/(x_{Di} − x_{Aj})) ∈ [0, 2π) the line-of-sight angle, ς_{ji} = ∥x_{Di} − x_{Aj}∥₂, µ_{ji} = 1/α_{ji} (defender-to-attacker speed ratio), ϖ_{ji} = 1 − µ²_{ji},
- θ_{ji} = arccos( (√(ϖ_{ji}(ς²_{ji} − ρ²_i)) − µ_{ji}ρ_i) / ς_{ji} ),
- η_j = (µ_{ji}ρ_i + ς_{ji}cos θ ± √((µ_{ji}ρ_i + ς_{ji}cos θ)² − ϖ_{ji}(ς²_{ji} − ρ²_i))) / ϖ_{ji}.

The oval has two arcs: O¹_i (between the players, the minus root, eq. (16)) and O²_i (far arc, plus root). The **defensive advantage angle** is γ_i = 2θ_{ji}, determined by the arc O¹_i.

**Proof approach.** (1) Simultaneous arrival with radius: η′_j = µ_{ji}η_j + ρ_i (defender covers µη_j of ground but only needs to reach the ρ_i-ball). (2) Law of cosines at A_j: η′²_j = η²_j + ς²_{ji} − 2ς_{ji}η_j cos θ. (3) Decisive quadratic, eq. (17): (1 − µ²_{ji})η²_j − 2(µ_{ji}ρ_i + ς_{ji}cos θ)η_j + ς²_{ji} − ρ²_i = 0. (4) The two roots are the two arcs; since points on O²_i are reachable only through O¹_i, the defender always intercepts on O¹_i first, so O¹_i governs the advantage region. (5) Equal-root (zero-discriminant) condition gives the half-angle θ_{ji} (tangency directions from A_j's position), hence γ_i = 2θ_{ji}; η_j on O¹_i satisfies (16). Analytic; local (per pair).

**Physical meaning / checks.** Remark 8: the defendable region of pair (D_i, A_j) has the oval as boundary; interior points give strict temporal advantage for the defender. Sanity check (mine): ρ_i = 0 gives θ_{ji} = arccos(√(1−µ²_{ji})) = arcsin(µ_{ji}), so γ_i = 2 arcsin(v*_{D}/v*_{A}) — exactly the containment angle of the cooperative-containment result [26] that the paper cites for the elliptical region; the capture radius thus *widens* each defender's angular coverage beyond 2arcsin(µ). Terminology is slightly inconsistent across the paper (Fig. 5 caption "Cartesian ellipse", Theorem 3 "Cartesian oval", Remark 8 "ellipse for α_{ji} > 1").

### 5.4 Theorem 4 (pp. 7–8): optimal target point, one defender vs. one faster attacker

**Statement.** Given initial positions with ∥x⁰_{Di} − x⁰_{Aj}∥₂ > ρ_i, first convert ω_{ji} to ω̄_{ji} ∈ (−π/2, π/2) by eq. (18) (ω̄ = ω if x⁰_{Di} ≥ x⁰_{Aj} ∧ y⁰_{Di} ≥ y⁰_{Aj}; ω − π if x⁰_{Di} < x⁰_{Aj}; ω − 2π if x⁰_{Di} ≥ x⁰_{Aj} ∧ y⁰_{Di} < y⁰_{Aj}). Then, per case:
1. x⁰_{Di} < x⁰_{Aj}: if θ_{ji} − ω̄_{ji} < π/2, S*_j = (x⁰_{Aj}, n) (go straight up); if θ_{ji} − ω̄_{ji} ≥ π/2, S*_j = (x⁰_{Aj} + ℓ, n) with ℓ = (n − y⁰_{Aj}) tan(θ_{ji} − ω̄_{ji} − π/2 + ϵ), ϵ > 0 arbitrarily small (graze the cone boundary on the right).
2. x⁰_{Di} > x⁰_{Aj}: if θ_{ji} + ω̄_{ji} < π/2, S*_j = (x⁰_{Aj}, n); if θ_{ji} + ω̄_{ji} ≥ π/2, S*_j = (x⁰_{Aj} − ℓ, n), ℓ = (n − y⁰_{Aj}) tan(θ_{ji} + ω̄_{ji} − π/2 + ϵ).
3. x⁰_{Di} = x⁰_{Aj}: if θ_{ji} < π/2, S*_j = (x⁰_{Aj} ± ℓ, n), ℓ = (n − y⁰_{Aj}) tan(θ_{ji} + ϵ); if θ_{ji} ≥ π/2 the attacker cannot reach T at all and S*_j = (x⁰_{Aj}, y⁰_{Aj} + b), b > 0 (closest point of the oval to T).

**Proof approach.** Geometric case analysis on the cone boundary: γ_i = 2θ_{ji} (Theorem 3) fixes two boundary rays; if the vertical ray from A_j to (x⁰_{Aj}, n) lies outside the defensive cone, straight-up motion is optimal; otherwise the attacker's path must keep an arbitrarily small angle ϵ clear of the cone boundary to remain safe while minimizing path length, giving the tangent-of-angle ℓ formulas; case 3 = complete coverage of T by the cone, payoff switches to (7) and the attacker aims at the oval point nearest T. Proof gap (p. 8): the claim that the closest point of O¹_i to T is "the midpoint of O¹_i, located directly above the attacker" is asserted; by oval symmetry this holds for a horizontal line of sight, but no argument is given for the general configuration. Remark 9: the optimal strategy switches from direct pursuit to circumnavigation at the thresholds θ_{ji} ± ω̄_{ji} = π/2; θ_{ji} ≥ π/2 is full defensive coverage.

### 5.5 Theorem 5 (pp. 8–9): cooperative interception condition for N_j slower defenders vs. one faster attacker

**Setup.** For adjacent defenders D_p, D_q pursuing the same A_j, the **intersecting angle** is δ^pq_j = |ω^p_j − ω^q_j| − ½γ_p − ½γ_q. δ^pq_j > 0: an angular gap exists and the attacker can slip through it (Fig. 8); δ^pq_j < 0: advantage regions overlap and A_j cannot escape the pair — the optimal attacker point is then the intersection of the two ovals closest to both attacker and T. For a coalition N_j (with each defender's coverage not contained in another's), the **absolute advantage angle** is Φ_j = Σ_{i∈N_j} γ_i + Σ_{p∼q ∈ N_j} δ^{pq−}_j, where p∼q means neighbors and δ^{pq−} collects only nonpositive δ's.

**Statement.** If all adjacent intersecting angles are < 0, the boundary of the defenders' absolute advantage angle is [Φ^I_j, Φ^S_j] = [min_i(ω̄_{ji} − θ_{ji}), max_i(ω_{ji} + θ_{ji})] if some defender starts bottom-right of the attacker (∃ x⁰_{Di} > x⁰_{Aj} ∧ y⁰_{Di} < y⁰_{Aj}); otherwise [min_i(ω_{ji} − θ_{ji}), max_i(ω_{ji} + θ_{ji})]. If the attacker's cone onto T, eq. (19): [arctan((n − y⁰_A)/(m − x⁰_A)), arctan((y⁰_A − n)/x⁰_A)] ⊂ [Φ^I_j, Φ^S_j], then A_j's interception is guaranteed.

**Proof approach.** Per-defender boundary rays γ_{i,l}, γ_{i,s} of the cone γ_i = [ω_{ji} − θ_{ji}, ω_{ji} + θ_{ji}] (shifted by −2π for a defender bottom-right, using (18)); the coalition's angular interval is the envelope of the shifted rays; if this interval covers the cone subtended by T at A_j, every attack ray exits through a covered sector, so no escape route exists (pigeonhole on rays, informal). Remark 10: the Fig. 9 example has Φ₁ = γ₁ + γ₂ + γ₃ + δ¹²₁ + δ²³₁ satisfying (19); Remark 11: slower defenders win "by eliminating escape routes rather than out-running the attacker" — strategic positioning beats speed. **Imprecision (verified against p. 9):** the interval in (19) is printed with a positive lower endpoint (angle toward F) and a negative upper endpoint (angle toward E), i.e., unordered as an interval; the intended meaning is the angular cone subtended by T, and the containment must be read sector-wise. The proof is informal (no rigorous covering lemma), the main gap of the paper.

### 5.6 Theorem 6 (p. 9): barrier, equal speeds (α_{ji} = 1)

**Statement.** If v*_{Aj} = v*_{Di} and x⁰_{Di} ∈ Ω, the barrier B^e(x⁰_{Di}) consists of two adjacent curves: B^e₁(x⁰_{Di}) = {x | ∥x⁰_{Di} − E∥₂ = ∥x − E∥₂ + ρ_i, x ∈ [0, c₇)} and B^e₂(x⁰_{Di}) = {x | ∥x⁰_{Di} − F∥₂ = ∥x − F∥₂ + ρ_i, x ∈ [c₇, m]}, c₇ from the curves' intersection. Each curve is one branch of a hyperbola (difference of distances to two foci = ρ_i), matching the scenario statement that at equal speeds the simultaneous-arrival set is the attacker-side hyperbola branch (Fig. 10).

**Proof approach.** Endpoint contradiction: if ∥x⁰_{Aj} − E∥₂ < ∥x⁰_{Di} − E∥₂ − ρ_i, then C_{A_j,inner}(D_i, A_j) ∩ T is infinite (successful attack); if >, empty; equality gives B^e₁; symmetric at F; also valid if the defender starts at E or F. **Proof gap (mine, p. 9):** the argument only treats the endpoints; the needed fact that with α = 1 the maximum over S ∈ T of (∥S − x⁰_{Di}∥₂ − ∥S − x⁰_{Aj}∥₂) is attained at an endpoint of T (any interior stationary point of the distance difference is a collinear configuration that is a minimum, so no middle barrier segment exists, unlike Theorem 1) is used implicitly. Remark 12 supplies the conceptual justification: at α = 1 the case is a special instance of Scenario 1 whose relevant barriers are exactly the endpoint-anchored arcs B₁, B₃, B₅ of Theorem 2 (the middle segments B₂/B₄ degenerate).

### 5.7 Lemma 1 (p. 10): optimal strategy in the defensive advantage region (α < 1)

**Statement.** If x⁰_{Aj} ∈ Ω_D(x⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq}), with payoff reformulated as eq. (20): J_{j,2} = ∥S*_j − T_j∥₂ (T_j ∈ T), then by (8) the optimal target point S*_j is the point of C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq}) (the boundary of the attackable region determined by all three initial positions) closest to T, and the optimal strategy of A_j is the straight line from x⁰_{Aj} to S*_j.

**Discussion (no formal proof; descriptive).** Since C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}, x⁰_{Dq}) consists of arcs for α < 1, one must check whether the peaks of C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}) and C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dq}) fall inside the joint region (Fig. 11). Defender strategies: if S*_j ∈ C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}) ∩ C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dq}) both defenders go straight to S*_j; if S*_j ∈ C(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dp}) only, D_q's strategy is payoff-irrelevant (S*_j already lies in C_{A_j,inner}(x⁰_{Aj}, x⁰_{Dq})), and for consistency both are told to head to S*_j. Physical meaning: the game value is the gap between the joint attackable region's closest approach and the target line.

### 5.8 Theorem 7 (pp. 10–11): existence and uniqueness of the offensive optimal target point (α < 1, ρ_i > 0)

**Statement.** Considering α_{ji} < 1, ρ_i > 0, if x⁰_{Aj} ∈ Ω_A(x⁰_{Di}, x⁰_{Aj}) and m is sufficiently large, there exists a unique optimal target point S*_j = (s*_j, n) ∈ T with s*_j > x⁰_{Aj}; on [x⁰_{Aj}, m] the payoff (5) first increases then decreases, attaining its maximum at S*_j. (Restricted to x⁰_{Aj} > x⁰_{Di}; if x⁰_{Aj} = x⁰_{Di} then s*_j = x⁰_{Aj} = x⁰_{Di}.)

**Proof approach (the paper's most technical proof, Fig. 12).** (1) Construction: r₁ = ∥x⁰_{Aj} − L∥₂, r₂ = ∥x⁰_{Di} − I∥₂, r₃ = ∥I − L∥₂ with L, G on T and R, I auxiliary points; from F′(s*_j) = 0 (eq. (10) form) and the similarity of the configuration (R L ∥ x⁰_{Di} I), ∥I − S*_j∥/∥x⁰_{Di} − S*_j∥ = ∥L − S*_j∥/∥R − S_j∥ and ∥R − S*_j∥/∥x⁰_{Aj} − S*_j∥ = α_{ji}. (2) Boundary ratios: S*_j = L ⇒ α_{ji} = 0; S*_j = G ⇒ α_{ji} = 1; hence for α_{ji} ∈ (0,1), S*_j lies strictly between L and G, proving existence and s*_j > x⁰_{Aj}. (3) Uniqueness parametrization: r₄ = ∥L − G∥₂, r₄ = r₁r₃/(r₂ − r₁), and ∥L − S_j∥₂ = ε r₄, ε ∈ [0,1]. (4) Ratio function F = ∥R − S_j∥₂/∥x⁰_{Aj} − S*_j∥₂; in the right triangle R L S_j, ∥R − S_j∥² = ∥R − L∥² + ∥S_j − L∥² and ∥x⁰_{Aj} − S_j∥² = r₁² + ε²r₄², giving ∥x⁰_{Aj} − S_j∥² = r₄²(r₂² + ε²(r₃+r₄)²)/(r₃ + εr₄)² and F(ε) = (r₃ + r₄)√(H(ε)) with H(ε) = ((r₃ + εr₄)² + r₂²)ε² / ((r₃ + εr₄)²((r₃+r₄)²ε² + r₂²)). (5) Decisive monotonicity computation: H′(ε) = 2εr₃r₂²H₂(ε) / ((r₃+εr₄)³(r₄²ε² + 2r₃r₄ε² + r₃²ε² + r₂²)²) with H₂(ε) = r₂² + r₃² + 3r₃r₄ε + 3r₄²ε² − (r₃r₄ + 2r₄²)ε³; since H₂′(ε) = 3r₄(1−ε)(r₃(1+ε) + 2r₄ε) > 0 on (0,1) (verification mine — the paper asserts "we can prove that when ε ∈ [0,1], H₂′(ε) > 0"), H₂ is increasing, H₂(ε) > H₂(0) > 0, so H′(ε) > 0, H and F are strictly increasing, and S*_j is unique. (6) Sign pattern: on [x⁰_{Aj}, s*_j), F < α_{ji} ⇒ F′(s_j) > 0; on (s*_j, n), F′(s_j) < 0 — J_{j,1} rises then falls. (7) The case y⁰_{Di} > y⁰_{Aj} (Fig. 13) is "similar". (8) First-order condition squared, eq. (21): α²_{ji}(s*_j − x⁰_{Di})²((x⁰_{Aj} − s*_j)² + (y⁰_{Aj} − n)²) = (s*_j − x⁰_{Aj})²((x⁰_{Di} − s*_j)² + (y⁰_{Di} − n)²). Analytic, global on the stated interval.

**Remark 14.** By (9) the first-order condition is a quartic in s*_j with up to four solutions; the theorem guarantees a unique valid one; Ferrari's method computes it in closed form; task assignment uses [2]; each speed-range category maps to one scenario and hence to the appropriate optimal strategy.

### 5.9 How heterogeneous speed ratios reshape the answers (synthesis)

- **α < 1 (Scenario 1):** barriers are offset-Apollonius curves C(D_i, A_j): ∥S′ − x⁰_{Aj}∥/α_{ji} = ∥S′ − x⁰_{Di}∥ − ρ_i; heterogeneity enters through per-pair α_{ji} and ρ_i in every segment and through the rendezvous shift ρ_p − β_{pq}ρ_q in (13). More than two (faster) defenders on one slower attacker add nothing — the barrier "depends on at most two of them" (p. 4, citing [2]).
- **α > 1 (Scenario 2):** regions are angular, not areal: each slower defender covers a cone γ_i = 2θ_{ji} with θ_{ji} inflated by ρ_i relative to the radius-free 2arcsin(µ_{ji}); a faster attacker always reaches T if it is long enough unless a coalition's absolute angle Φ_j covers the attacker's cone (19); interception of a faster attacker is thus a pure coverage/positioning condition.
- **α = 1 (Scenario 3):** the middle barrier segments vanish; the barrier is two hyperbola branches anchored at E and F; the case is the degenerate limit of Scenario 1 (Remark 12), and the optimal-decision analysis of Sec. IV is accordingly conducted only for Scenario 1 (Remark 13).

No formal Isaacs/HJI verification (Hamiltonian analysis, barrier-surface PDE, or sufficiency theorem) is provided anywhere; "optimality" is argued geometrically (dominance + first-order conditions + payoff monotonicity), and strategies are open-loop straight lines whose feedback version is the implied position-dependent switching rule.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated:** (i) constant speed ratios assumed for analytic tractability; time-varying ratios "a promising avenue for future research" (p. 3); (ii) homogeneous attackers with equal threat levels; weighted-damage objectives Σ w_j left as a multiobjective assignment problem (Remark 2); (iii) no environmental disturbances/measurement noise (p. 3); (iv) speed-dependent payoff functions with real-time performance "an open direction" (p. 3); (v) conclusion: Dubins-type kinematic constraints would force numerical barrier construction; stochastic disturbances and their effect on winning regions; experimental validation on real robotic platforms (p. 12); (vi) N < M reduces the contribution of the barriers to pairwise outcomes and defers the real problem to attacker prioritization (Remark 1).

**Critical observations (with location):**
1. **Scenario 2/3 unvalidated**: both simulations use slower attackers, so the paper's most novel machinery (γ_i, δ^pq_j, Φ_j, eq. (19)) is never numerically tested (Sec. V).
2. **Informal coverage proof**: Theorem 5's sufficiency argument ("if the boundary … can cover this angular region, the attacker will not be able to attack successfully", p. 9) is a ray-pigeonhole argument without a formal lemma; eq. (19) is printed with unordered interval endpoints (positive lower, negative upper bound) and must be read as a sector inclusion.
3. **Unproven geometric assertion**: Theorem 4, case 3 — the closest point of O¹_i to T is claimed to be the oval's midpoint "directly above the attacker" (p. 8); true by symmetry only for a horizontal line of sight.
4. **Implicit endpoint-reduction**: Theorem 6's proof handles only E and F; the fact that at α = 1 no interior point of T can be the critical target (so the two branches suffice) is never demonstrated (p. 9).
5. **Loose analytic bookkeeping**: s*_j and s_k are called "polynomial constant(s) with six/eight variables" (pp. 5–6) while actually being implicitly defined quartic/quadratic roots (Remark 14); c₁–c₇ are "determined by the intersections of the curves" with no algorithm given.
6. **Optimality is geometric, not Isaacs-verified**: no Hamiltonian/sufficiency analysis; strategies are straight lines to S*_j with claimed state-feedback interpretation (eqs. (6), (8)); barrier non-crossing (that neither side can benefit by crossing) is argued via the dominance definitions rather than proved as a no-value-improvement lemma.
7. **Editorial defects**: duplicated sentence in contribution 3 (p. 2: "Furthermore, based on the players' winning regions…" appears twice); Scenario-1 opener "If v*A_j < v*D_j, then α_{ji} < 1" has mismatched subscripts (p. 4); Case 2 prints "ρ₃ = 1.6" for D6's radius (p. 12); Fig. 6 caption says "relative ordinate (x-coordinate)" conflating ordinate/abscissa.
8. **Modeling scope**: instant heading changes (unlimited turn rate), max-speed play always optimal (exceptions waved off, p. 3), stationary defenders after capture, and attackers that "approach T as closely as possible despite inevitable interception" (p. 3) are behavioral assumptions, not derived; the 2-vs-1 cooperation trigger ("when advantageous", p. 3) is delegated to the assignment algorithm of [2] rather than derived from (13).

## 8. Citation Value

This is the bridge paper between the point-capture analytical-barrier school (Yan–Shi–Zhong [2], [19], [30]; Wu et al. [18]) and nonpoint-capture heterogeneous reach–avoid games: it is the natural "related work" anchor for any paper that adds capture radii, mixed faster/slower defenders, or advantage-angle coalition coverage to a barrier-based multiplayer reach–avoid analysis, and its eq. (13) (heterogeneous rendezvous with radii) and Theorem 5's absolute-advantage-angle condition (19) are the two reusable technical primitives. Cite it as: "Wang et al. derived closed-form barriers and optimal target points for multiplayer reach–avoid games with heterogeneous defender capture radii across all three speed-ratio regimes, using offset-Apollonius curves for slower attackers, Cartesian-oval defensive advantage angles for faster attackers, and a coalition coverage condition guaranteeing that slower defenders can intercept a faster intruder." It is also the right baseline to cite when arguing real-time analytic barriers (sub-second computation) against HJI, numerical, or learning-based reach–avoid methods, and as the starting point for the capture-radius-aware task-assignment problem it leaves open.
