## 2. Research Background

Many robotics and control applications — aircraft piloting, ground vehicles, air traffic management, and team path planning — require agents to reach goals under adversarial opposition or moving obstacles, which the authors model through reach–avoid games: one agent must reach a sequence of target regions while avoiding the capture region of an opponent (p. 1014). Capture-the-flag (CTF) is proposed as an excellent proxy framework for such adversarial problems because it naturally combines two competing objectives (reach the opponent's flag, then return to one's own safe region while avoiding capture), and because it organically admits human agents, enabling study of automation for humans and mixed human–robot teams (p. 1014). Prior tractable treatments (RoboFlag-style model predictive control, MILP defense, geometric methods) imposed limiting assumptions on player actions (e.g., straight-line attacker motion or prescribed linear feedback laws) and gave no guarantees if the opponent deviated (pp. 1015–1016). The authors instead advocate the zero-sum differential game / HJI route: complete winning-region and feedback-strategy solutions computed offline numerically and recalled online as lookup tables or visual decision aids (pp. 1016, 1020). The work is the journal extension of the authors' ICRA 2011 paper [1] and the methodological foundation of the BEARCAT (Berkeley autonomy and robotics in capture-the-flag) testbed, which later became a general platform for human/automation decision research (p. 1023).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem (Section III, pp. 1016–1017).** A two-player CTF game in a compact planar domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ (possibly non-simply-connected; obstacles are holes in $\Omega$). The attacker $a$ must (1) reach the flag region $F \subset \Omega$ (closed, circular of radius $r_f$) within $[0,T_1]$, (2) reach the return region $R \subset \Omega$ (closed) within $[T_1,T_f]$, $T_2 = T_f - T_1$, while (3) staying in $\Omega$ and outside the capture set $C = \{x \mid \|x_a - x_d\|_2 \le r_c\}$ for all $t \in [0,T_f]$; the attacker also wins if the defender violates the rules by entering $F \cup R$; the defender wins by preventing all of this by $T_f$ while obeying $x_d \in \Omega \setminus (F \cup R)$ (p. 1016). The deliverable is the complete solution: attacker-winning region $W_A \subset \mathbb{R}^4$ with winning feedback strategies, and $W_D = \Omega^2 \setminus W_A$ for the defender, plus intermediate sets $F_A$ (flag-capture), $F_D = \Omega^2 \setminus F_A$ (stop-capture), $R_A$ (flag-return), $R_D = \Omega^2 \setminus R_A$ (stop-return) (p. 1017). The paper focuses on $T_f \to \infty$ because small $T_f$ merely induces trivial delaying strategies (pp. 1016–1017). Solutions are positioned at the guidance layer: explicit path synthesis for autonomous vehicles, decision-support visualizations for humans (p. 1016).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Theory (game-of-kind formulation of reach–avoid, Section IV)**: formal Elliott–Kalton nonanticipating-strategy formulation of the finite-horizon reach–avoid game (2)–(6); equivalent constrained terminal-cost minimax (7)–(11) via the time-dilation augmentation trick; characterization of the upper value as the constrained viscosity solution of the HJI equation (12)–(13), with $J^{\bar{}}_T(x^0) = \phi(x^0,-T)$ (15) "by slight modifications of the proof techniques in [20]" (Evans–Souganidis representation formulas); Isaacs condition (16) guarantees existence of value.
2. **Modeling (two-stage CTF composition, Section V)**: encoding of all CTF rule violations into reach–avoid target/undesired sets ($G_A$, $G_D$ fold "attacker leaves $\Omega$" and "defender enters $F\cup R\cup\Omega^C$" into the *other* player's winning conditions, p. 1020); backward composition — compute the flag-return set $R_A$ (24), intersect with the flag set to get the *feasible flag-capture configurations* $\tilde{R}_A = G_F \cap R_A$, then compute the overall attacker-winning region $W_A = \mathcal{R}^A_{T_1}(\tilde{F}_J, C_J)$ (26) so that stage-1 winning is conditioned on stage-2 feasibility. This is the paper's central structural device for multistage games.
3. **Algorithm (strategy synthesis, Sections IV-B, V-B)**: optimal feedback controls (17)–(19) from costate gradients of the value function where differentiable; for the separable kinematic Hamiltonian (23) the Isaacs condition holds and the closed forms (27)–(28) (max-speed motion along $\mp$ gradient blocks) follow; a switching attacker strategy (run $\mu_1$ until $x(\cdot) \in \tilde{R}_A$, then $\mu_2$) is claimed optimal on $W_A$; stationary infinite-horizon strategies (29)–(30) from the monotone limit $\phi^*$ (20).
4. **Experiment (BEARCAT, Section VII)**: first field validation of HJI differential-game solutions with human agents — three trial campaigns (small-area walking with WIFI phones; large-area running with obstacles over 3G; UAV-assisted search with simulated line-of-sight sensing) demonstrating (i) HJI feedback as an explicit path-planning tool, (ii) winning-region visualization as decision support enabling *deception* strategies (an aided agent in a losing configuration luring the unaided/uncertain opponent out of its winning region), and (iii) winning-region-guided UAV search under incomplete information.

### 5.1 Game model, information structure, winning conditions (pp. 1016–1017)

- Dynamics (1): $\dot{x}_a = u$, $u \in U$; $\dot{x}_d = d$, $d \in D$, with kinematic input sets $U \triangleq \{u \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \|u\|_2 \le v_{a,\max}\}$, $D \triangleq \{d \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \|d\|_2 \le v_{d,\max}\}$; joint state $x = (x_a, x_d) \in \mathbb{R}^4$, joint domain $\Omega^2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^4$; $x(0) = (x_a^0, x_d^0) = x^0$. Domain $\Omega$ compact (may have holes); $R, F \subset \Omega$ closed; capture set $C = \{x \mid \|x_a - x_d\|_2 \le r_c\}$ (p. 1016). Only guidance-level (position) dynamics — no heading/acceleration states.
- Termination/winning (p. 1016): attacker wins iff (i) *Flag Capture*: $\exists\, t \in [0,T_1],\, x_a(t) \in F$; (ii) *Flag Return*: $\exists\, t \in [T_1, T_f],\, x_a(t) \in R$; (iii) $x_a(t) \in \Omega \wedge x(t) \notin C\ \forall t \in [0,T_f]$; plus the rule-violation clause (defender entering $F\cup R$ loses). Defender wins by preventing (i)–(iii) until $T_f$ while $x_d \in \Omega \setminus (F\cup R)$. Time windows $T_1$, $T_2 = T_f - T_1$; emphasis on $T_f \to \infty$ (pp. 1016–1017).
- Information (p. 1017): positions fully observable; "the defender has knowledge of the past and present inputs of the attacker. More formally, this can be viewed as a Stackelberg game with attacker as the leader [16]." [Authors' claim] this pattern is conservative for the attacker under general dynamics, but "for the kinematic agents considered in (1), it turns out that a minimax condition holds, hence resulting in nonconservative solutions" — **proof gap**: no proof is given at this point; the substantiation arrives only via the separable Hamiltonian (23) satisfying Isaacs' condition (16) on p. 1020 (which equalizes the min–max and max–min orders for the *Hamiltonian*, the standard route to information-pattern irrelevance for such simple-motion games).
- Derived sets (p. 1017): $W_A \subset \mathbb{R}^4$ = configurations from which the attacker can force victory regardless of the defender; $W_D = \Omega^2 \setminus W_A$; $F_A$ / $F_D = \Omega^2 \setminus F_A$ / $R_A$ / $R_D = \Omega^2 \setminus R_A$ as single-stage analogues.

### 5.2 Reach–avoid game of kind and its game-of-degree reformulation (pp. 1017–1018)

- General dynamics (2): $\dot{x} = f(x,u,d)$, $x(0) = x^0$; admissible controls (3) measurable maps $[t_1,t_2] \to U/D$; nonanticipating strategies (4): $\Gamma^U_{[t_1,t_2]} = \{\gamma_U : \mathcal{D}_{[t_1,t_2]} \to \mathcal{U}_{[t_1,t_2]},\ d(s) = \tilde d(s)\ \forall s \le t \Rightarrow \gamma_U[d](s) = \gamma_U[\tilde d](s)\ \forall s \le t\}$ (and symmetrically $\Gamma^D$) — Elliott–Kalton [22] information pattern, conservatively favoring Player II (the defender).
- Winning condition of Player I — game of kind (5): $\exists t \in [0,T],\ x(t) \in \mathcal{T},\ x(s) \notin \mathcal{K}\ \forall s \le t$. Winning region — reach–avoid set (6):
$$\mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{K}) = \{x(0) = x^0 : \forall \gamma^D_{[0,T]} \in \Gamma^D_{[0,T]},\ \exists u \in \mathcal{U}_{[0,T]},\ (5)\ \text{holds}\}.$$
Zero-sum complement: Player II's winning set is $(\mathcal{R}^A_T)^c$ (p. 1018).
- Level-set game of degree [23],[24]: a set $G = \{x : \phi_G(x) \le 0\}$; set algebra $A \cap B = \max(\phi_A,\phi_B)$, $A \cup B = \min(\phi_A,\phi_B)$; $\phi_T, \phi_K$ bounded and Lipschitz continuous (assumption, p. 1018).
- Time-dilation augmentation (7)–(8): augmented input $\tilde u = [u,\ \dot u] \in \tilde U = U \times [0,1]$; augmented dynamics $\dot{\tilde x} = \tilde f(\tilde x, \tilde u, d) = \dot u\, f(\tilde x, u, d)$, $\tilde x(0) = x^0$. Physical meaning: the scalar $\dot u \in [0,1]$ rescales the traversal speed so that "reach $\mathcal{T}$ at *some* time in $[0,T]$" becomes a *terminal-time* condition of the augmented system — this is the device that converts the $\exists t$ disjunct of (5) into an optimization terminal cost.
- Constrained minimax — upper value (9):
$$J^{\bar{}}_T(x^0) = \sup_{\bar\gamma^D_{[0,T]}} \inf_{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{[0,T]}} \max\{\phi_T(\tilde x(T)), -\phi_K(\tilde x(T))\} \quad \text{s.t. } \phi_K(\tilde x(t)) > 0\ \forall t \in [0,T],$$
with trajectories violating the constraint assigned cost $+\infty$; reach–avoid set (10): $\mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{K}) = \{x^0 \in \mathbb{R}^n : J^{\bar{}}_T(x^0) \le 0\}$. Lower value (11): same payoff with $\inf_{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}} \sup_{\mathcal{D}}$. In general $J^{\_}_T \le J^{\bar{}}_T$; if $J^{\_}_T = J^{\bar{}}_T$ the problem "has a value" and Nash strategies are feedback strategies [21] (p. 1018).
- Assumptions for all of 5.2: compact control sets, measurable controls, nonanticipating strategies, bounded Lipschitz $\phi_T,\phi_K$; finite horizon $[0,T]$.

### 5.3 HJI characterization of the upper value (pp. 1018–1019) — the paper's main analytic pillar

- **Result (12)–(15) (representation of the upper value; the closest thing to a theorem in the paper, stated with attribution)**: let $\phi : \mathbb{R}^n \times [-T,0] \to \mathbb{R}$ be the *viscosity solution* [26] of the constrained terminal-value HJI equation
$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \min\!\left[0,\, H\!\left(x, \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}\right)\right] = 0, \qquad \phi(x,0) = \phi_{\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{K}}(x) \tag{12}$$
subject to the state-constraint side condition
$$\phi(x,t) \ge -\phi_K(x) \quad \forall t \in [-T,0], \tag{13}$$
with Hamiltonian (14) $H(x,p) = \min_{u \in U} \max_{d \in D}\, p^T f(x,u,d)$. Then for $x^0 \in \mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{K})$:
$$J^{\bar{}}_T(x^0) = \phi(x^0, -T). \tag{15}$$
- Note the terminal datum $\phi_{\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{K}}(x) = \max\{\phi_T(x), -\phi_K(x)\}$ (level-set of $\mathcal{T} \cap \mathcal{K}^c$ via the max-rule of 5.2), which matches the terminal payoff $\max\{\phi_T(\tilde x(T)), -\phi_K(\tilde x(T))\}$ in (9); and the $\min[0,\cdot]$ in (12) freezes the dynamics on the subzero region once the target has been reached (reach-then-stay semantics).
- **Assumptions**: bounded Lipschitz $\phi_T,\phi_K$; compact input sets; nonanticipating strategies; the result is claimed "by the results detailed in [17] and [25]" and "by slight modifications of the proof techniques in [20]" — i.e., proof approach (as attributed, 4 steps): (i) dynamic programming: the terminal-cost game over $[0,T]$ satisfies the semigroup property; (ii) the constrained problem is handled by projecting the value above the obstacle $-\phi_K$ (13) (the constraint $\phi_K(\tilde x(t)) > 0$ becomes a reflected/obstacle-type boundary condition); (iii) Evans–Souganidis-style representation formulas for viscosity solutions of the upper HJI equation identify the unique constrained viscosity solution with the upper value (15); (iv) comparison/uniqueness of viscosity solutions [26] pins down $\phi$. Local-vs-global: (15) is stated for $x^0 \in \mathcal{R}^A_T$ (global in the winning region); the complement is characterized only as $\{J^{\bar{}}_T > 0\}$ via (10). Decisive equation: (15) itself — it converts a set-membership game of kind into a scalar PDE solution.
- **Existence of value (16)**: Isaacs' condition $\min_u \max_d\, p^T f = \max_d \min_u\, p^T f$ (16) implies the reach–avoid problem has a value ($J^{\_}_T = J^{\bar{}}_T$) — [attributed to standard zero-sum theory [15],[16], not re-proven].
- Computational meaning: grid-based viscosity solution via the Level-Set Toolbox for MATLAB [27]; subgrid resolution of set boundaries through continuity/interpolation of $\phi$ — contrasted favorably with full discretization into graph games [2],[31] (p. 1019). Scalability caveat: grid size grows exponentially with dimension; 4-D routine, some 5-D systems solved [28] (p. 1018).

### 5.4 CTF Hamiltonian and Isaacs condition (p. 1020, eq. (23))

For dynamics (1) ($f = [u, d]^T$), with costate split $p = (p_a, p_d)$ into attacker/defender blocks:
$$H(x,p) = \min_{u \in U} \max_{d \in D}\, p_a^T u + p_d^T d = -v_{a,\max}\|p_a\|_2 + v_{d,\max}\|p_d\|_2. \tag{23}$$
- Proof logic (1 step, elementary): the Hamiltonian is *separable* in $(u,d)$ — the inner $\max_d p_d^T d = v_{d,\max}\|p_d\|_2$ (support-function/max-norm dual) and $\min_u p_a^T u = -v_{a,\max}\|p_a\|_2$; separability gives $\min_u \max_d = \max_d \min_u$, so **Isaacs' condition (16) is satisfied** (stated explicitly on p. 1020) and the game has a value. Decisive inequality structure: $\pm\|p\|$ duality. Physical meaning: the attacker always decreases the value at rate $-v_{a,\max}\|p_a\|$ (hence runs at full speed against its costate gradient), the defender always increases it at rate $+v_{d,\max}\|p_d\|$ — the origin of the bang-bang max-speed strategies (27)–(28). Extensibility: any dynamics with $f$ affine and additively separable in $u$ and $d$ admits the same argument (the paper notes such Hamiltonians are "saddlelike", p. 1019).

### 5.5 Two-stage construction of winning regions (p. 1020, eqs. (24)–(26)) — the paper's structural core

Rule-violation folding (p. 1020): with $\Omega^C$ the complement of $\Omega$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, define $G_A = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^4 : x_a \in \Omega^C\}$ (attacker left the domain), $G_D = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^4 : x_d \in F \cup R \cup \Omega^C\}$ (defender broke the rules), $G_R = \{x : x_a \in R\}$, $G_F = \{x : x_a \in F\}$. "The constraints for each agent are encoded as part of the winning conditions for the other, so that the attacker wins if the defender violates its constraints and vice versa."

- **Flag-return stage** (horizon $[0,T_2]$): attacker-winning condition $O_R \vee O_D$ with
$O_R = \{\exists t \in [0,T_2],\ x_a(t) \in R \wedge x(s) \notin C \cup G_A\ \forall s \in [0,t]\}$ and $O_D = \{\exists t \in [0,T_2],\ x_d(t) \in F \cup R \cup \Omega^C \wedge x(s) \notin C \cup G_A\ \forall s \in [0,t]\}$.
Target/undesired sets: attacker target $\mathcal{R}_J = G_R \cup G_D$ (union via min-rule), defender "target" (= attacker's undesired set) $\mathcal{C}_J = C \cup G_A$. Then
$$R_A = \mathcal{R}^A_{T_2}(\mathcal{R}_J, \mathcal{C}_J). \tag{24}$$
- **Flag-capture stage in isolation** (horizon $[0,T_1]$): condition $O_F \vee O_D$ ($O_D$ re-timed to $T_1$), target $\mathcal{F}_J = G_F \cup G_D$, giving
$$F_A = \mathcal{R}^A_{T_1}(\mathcal{F}_J, \mathcal{C}_J). \tag{25}$$
- **Coupled two-stage composition**: arrival at $F$ is insufficient if the arrival configuration lies outside $R_A$; the attacker "must reach the subset of the flag region for which flag return is possible", $\tilde{R}_A = G_F \cap R_A$. Modified objective $\tilde O_F \vee O_D$ with $\tilde O_F = \{\exists t \in [0,T_1],\ x(t) \in \tilde R_A \wedge x(s) \notin C \cup G_A\ \forall s \in [0,t]\}$; modified target $\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_J = \tilde{R}_A \cup G_D$; overall
$$W_A = \mathcal{R}^A_{T_1}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_J, \mathcal{C}_J). \tag{26}$$
- Proof approach: none needed beyond the definitions — the correctness of (26) rests on the *dynamic consistency* of the two reach–avoid computations, i.e., that targeting the level set $\tilde R_A$ during stage 1 exactly preserves stage-2 feasibility. **Proof gap (mild)**: the paper does not formally verify that $W_A$ from (26) coincides with the game-theoretic winning region of the *sequential* game (e.g., that no attacker strategy outside (26)'s construction wins by reaching configurations in $F \cap R_A$ reached at times not anticipated by the $T_2$-horizon computation, nor how the defender's stage-1 presence inside $F \cup R$-adjacent states interacts with $\tilde R_A$'s computation, since (24) assumed a fresh start). The empirical validations (Section VI) support it. Note also $W_D$ is obtained *only* by complementation, $\Omega^2 \setminus W_A$ — the defender's two-stage blocking behavior emerges from the composed construction rather than from an explicit defender-side HJI composition.
- Physical meaning: (26) says "win stage 1 by entering exactly those flag configurations from which stage 2 is still winnable" — the key design insight that later papers (e.g., P038's transition-phase treatment) refine analytically. Extensibility: the pattern (compute downstream stage first, intersect to get the upstream target set) is explicitly offered as the route to "general multiplayer capture-the-flag games" (p. 1015) and to symmetric role-switching games (p. 1027).

### 5.6 Feedback strategy synthesis (pp. 1018–1021, eqs. (17)–(19), (27)–(28))

- Generic synthesis where $\phi$ is differentiable [21],[32],[33] (dynamic programming + Pontryagin minimum principle): for $x \in \mathcal{R}^A_T(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{K})$,
$$\mu(x,t) \in \arg\min_{u \in U} \max_{d \in D} \left(\frac{\partial \phi(x, t-T)}{\partial x}\right)^{\!T} f(x,u,d), \tag{17}$$
for $x \notin \mathcal{R}^A_T$, Player II: $\gamma(x,u,t) \in \arg\max_{d \in D} (\partial\phi/\partial x)^T f$ (18); under Isaacs (16), the defender's feedback map $\tilde\gamma(x,t) \in \arg\max_{d} \min_{u} (\partial\phi/\partial x)^T f$ (19). Viscosity solutions are only Lipschitz: differentiable away from singular surfaces, which have Lebesgue measure zero and "commonly correspond to regions in the state space in which the optimal controls are not uniquely determined" [16] (p. 1019). [Authors' heuristic, p. 1019] the event of landing on a singular surface "is likely to occur with probability approaching zero", "validated with the numerical and experimental tests" — **assumption without formal proof** (dispersal surfaces in reach–avoid games are generic, e.g., at symmetric geometries).
- CTF instantiation (pp. 1020–1021): let $\phi_1, \phi_2$ be the viscosity solutions computing $W_A$ (26) and $R_A$ (24); $p_{a,i} = \partial\phi_i/\partial x_a$, $p_{d,i} = \partial\phi_i/\partial x_d$. From (17) and (19) with Hamiltonian (23):
$$\mu_i(x,t) = -v_{a,\max} \frac{p_{a,i}(x, t-T_i)}{\|p_{a,i}(x, t-T_i)\|_2}, \qquad \tilde\gamma_i(x,t) = v_{d,\max} \frac{p_{d,i}(x, t-T_i)}{\|p_{d,i}(x, t-T_i)\|_2}, \tag{27} \tag{28}$$
for $t \in [0,T_i]$ — unit-norm gradient descent/ascent, i.e., **always maximum speed**, attacker along $-p_a$, defender along $+p_d$; if $\|p\| = 0$ (gradients vanish, e.g., deep inside/outside winning regions) the argmin/argmax is set-valued (unhandled in closed form; subsumed by the singular-surface discussion).
- **Switching optimality claim (p. 1021)**: the attacker strategy $\mu$ that plays $\mu_1$ and "switches to $\mu_2$ at the first time instant such that the trajectory $x(\cdot)$ enters the set $\tilde{R}_A$" — "It can be verified that $\mu$ is an optimal strategy for the attacker on the winning region $W_A$." **Proof gap**: no verification argument is given (one must show the stage-1 value $\phi_1$ remains a certificate along the switched trajectory and that the entry time into $\tilde R_A$ is compatible with the residual stage-2 horizon — nontrivial because $T_1/T_2$ windows are decoupled). Similarly "In a similar manner, one can derive an optimal strategy for the defender" (p. 1021) is asserted without construction. These are the paper's weakest formal spots; the simulation/experiment sections serve as the de facto validation.

### 5.7 Infinite-horizon limit and stationary strategies (pp. 1019, 1021, eqs. (20)–(22), (29)–(30))

- **Monotone-convergence result (20)**: since $\phi_T, \phi_K$ are bounded, $\phi$ is bounded [20]; by the form of (12), $\phi(\cdot,-T)$ is monotonically decreasing in $T$ [25]; hence (standard analysis [34], i.e., monotone-bounded convergence)
$$\phi^*(x) = \lim_{T \to \infty} \phi(x,-T) \tag{20}$$
exists pointwise. **Authors' explicit caveat (p. 1019)**: the limiting function of a finite-horizon DP iteration "may not always equal the value of an infinite horizon problem" [35],[36]; a stationary strategy may fail to exist; and the reach–avoid problem adds two obstacles to closing this gap: (1) the cost (9) is **not discounted**, and (2) the nonstandard constraint (13). "To the best of our knowledge, a rigorous analysis of the properties of the function $\phi^*$ in (20) is not available in literature." Possible route named: ergodic analysis of optimal stopping / discounted limits [21]. **This is an honest open problem, not a theorem.**
- Stationary strategies used empirically: $\mu^*(x) \in \arg\min_u \max_d (\partial\phi^*(x)/\partial x)^T f$ (21), $\gamma^*(x,u) \in \arg\max_d (\partial\phi^*(x)/\partial x)^T f$ (22); for CTF with $\phi_i^* = \lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_i(x,-t)$, $p^*_{a,i} = \partial\phi_i^*/\partial x_a$:
$$\mu_i^*(x) = -v_{a,\max} \frac{p^*_{a,i}(x)}{\|p^*_{a,i}(x)\|_2}, \qquad \tilde\gamma_i^*(x) = v_{d,\max} \frac{p^*_{d,i}(x)}{\|p^*_{d,i}(x)\|_2}. \tag{29} \tag{30}$$
"In various numerical and experimental tests, the strategies (21) and (22) are shown to provide satisfactory performance" — empirical, no convergence-rate or performance guarantees.
- Why not stationary HJI directly (p. 1019): fast marching [37]/fast sweeping [38] need convex Hamiltonians (Eikonal-type); differential games with $f$ affine in $u,d$ have saddle-like (nonconvex) Hamiltonians; and stationary solutions "typically yield little information for the synthesis of optimal strategies outside the respective player winning regions", whereas the time-dependent formulation (12) does — important when a player starts outside its winning region but can win if the opponent plays suboptimally (relevant to the deception experiments, Section VII).

- **Fig. 3** (p. 1022): two closed-loop simulations with both players on infinite-horizon strategies (29)–(30) — flag-capture-only and flag-return-only; defender starting inside its winning set intercepts the attacker in both. Validates (29)–(30) and the single-stage sets (24)–(25).
- **Fig. 4** (p. 1022): (a)–(b) defender starts in $W_D \setminus F_D$: it does *not* contest flag capture but repositions during the attacker's flag approach and prevents return — direct behavioral confirmation of the Fig. 2 finding; (c)–(d) defender starts outside $W_D$: attacker captures and returns unimpeded; notably the attacker "did not take the shortest direct path to $F$, but rather reached $F$ at a point that is closer to $R$" — qualitative evidence that the value-function gradient encodes the *two-stage* objective, not just stage-1 reachability.

- No quantitative accuracy tables, no baselines (e.g., against MPC or Apollonius-style geometric policies), no Monte-Carlo statistics: validation is by scenario visualization.

### 6.2 BEARCAT field experiments (Section VII, pp. 1021–1026)

- **Small-area trials (p. 1024, Fig. 7)** — validates phone guidance hardware and the HJI feedback as a path-planning tool; then, with asymmetric information (aided attacker vs unaided defender, attacker in a *losing* configuration): trial 1 defender misjudged the intercept angle; trial 2 defender adjusted and intercepted (deterministic optimal headings are learnable); the attacker then innovated **deception**: deliberately move opposite to guidance, pulling the defender out of $R_D$ (visible in real time on the phone), then revert to the optimal heading and win — "Two more trials were conducted, and in each case the attacker was able to successfully maneuver past the defender using this strategy. Note, however, that this maneuver was only successful with asymmetric guidance information." Validates (i) winning-region visualizations as decision support and (ii) the theoretical caveat of 5.7 that strategies synthesized from $\phi^*$ also inform play *outside* winning regions.
- **Large-area trials (pp. 1024–1025, Figs. 8–9)** — with buildings, running speeds, unreliable 3G, "gameplay much more dependent on deception and managing opponent information, with the HJI guidance information used as a decision support tool rather than the primary path-planning tool". Fig. 9 game: initial condition was in fact attacker-winning, but agents were out of line-of-sight; both maneuvered for visibility; the attacker hugged the cluttered southwest corner of the central building; the defender moved south to localize the attacker and thereby exited $R_D(x_a)$; the attacker visually confirmed the defender "well outside of $R_D(x_a)$", then went the opposite way and reached $R$ unintercepted (Fig. 9(d)–(f)). Validates winning-region displays under partial information; also demonstrates that with *both* agents aided, deception exploits position uncertainty rather than information asymmetry.
- **Automation-assisted UAV search (pp. 1025–1026, Fig. 10)** — quadrotor searching for an initially unobserved defender *for* the attacker; sensing simulated, state transmitted only in line of sight. Key idea: instead of a probabilistic prior over adversary locations ("difficult to create a distribution... without a model of the adversary's likely strategies"), use the HJI winning region as the worst-case adversary capability set: "the adversary only poses a danger to the agent if it is found within this set" — restrict search to $W_D$; possible defender positions $P_D$ shown as an expanding pink circle, visual regions $V_A$ (teal) / $V_Q$ (white). Outcome: UAV "able to quickly locate the defender, allowing the attacker to maneuver into a winning position". Explicitly qualitative ("not used in a quantitative fashion for agent path planning"). Validates a *new use modality* of reach–avoid sets: information-gathering guidance under incomplete observation.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (pp. 1019, 1021, 1027):**
1. Curse of dimensionality: uniform-grid value functions grow exponentially in joint-state dimension; 4-D practical, 5-D marginal [28]; multiagent CTF "problematic" — extending to higher dimensions and multiplayer games "currently a topic of active research" (p. 1027).
2. Solutions not real-time: precomputed lookup tables; ~1 h per 4-D set at 45 pts/dim (p. 1021); re-computation needed whenever $\Omega$, $F$, $R$, or parameters change.
3. Grid-induced underapproximation of sharp set boundaries (e.g., near $F_D \cap F$) with concrete failure mode: defenders starting below-grid-resolution outside $F_D$ still won; $\epsilon$-inflation proposed as mitigation (p. 1021).
4. Infinite-horizon theory unresolved: $\phi^*$ (20) proven to exist by monotone convergence, but not proven to be the infinite-horizon upper value (undiscounted cost + nonstandard constraint (13)); "a rigorous analysis... is not available in literature" (p. 1019); stationary strategies only empirically "satisfactory".
5. Symmetric two-sided CTF (roles switchable) requires a value function per role combination; role selection from those tables sketched but not implemented (p. 1027).

**Critical observations (mine, with page/eq anchors):**
6. **Switching-strategy optimality asserted, not proven** — "It can be verified that $\mu$ is an optimal strategy for the attacker on the winning region $W_A$" (p. 1021); no verification theorem for the two-stage switch, and the defender's overall optimal strategy is only gestured at ("In a similar manner...", p. 1021). The composition (26) itself is argued by construction, not by a dynamic-consistency proof for the sequential game (p. 1020).
7. **Minimax/information claim unproven in-paper**: the non-conservatism of the defender-knows-attacker-inputs pattern for kinematic agents (p. 1017) is justified only post hoc by separability of (23); no formal Elliott–Kalton value argument is carried out for the two-stage composite.
8. **Singular-surface handling is heuristic**: "likely to occur with probability approaching zero... validated with the numerical and experimental tests" (p. 1019) — dispersal surfaces (e.g., symmetric geometries where $\|p\|=0$ and (27)–(28) are undefined) are not characterized for the CTF game, unlike classical treatments [15],[16].
9. **Model scope**: point-mass kinematics with constant capture radius; the guidance-layer output assumes perfect GPS-grade state estimation (position errors acknowledged only as experimental nuisance factors, p. 1025); fixed time allocation $T_1$/$T_2$ windows even as $T_f \to \infty$ (the "infinite horizon" is two long fixed windows, so trivial delaying strategies reappear if $T_i$ is mis-set); no dynamics for the flag handoff.
10. **Experimental evidence is anecdotal**: single-digit trial counts, no success-rate statistics, only the flag-return stage played (p. 1023), simulated sensing in the UAV campaign (p. 1025), and no baseline comparison (e.g., unaided-vs-aided win rates are reported narratively, not tabulated).

## 8. Citation Value

Role in a future Introduction/Related Work: this is the **foundational numerical-HJI reference for capture-the-flag and multistage reach–avoid games** — the paper to cite (i) when motivating differential-game (worst-case-guarantee) treatment of multiobjective adversarial planning against assumption-laden MPC/geometric methods, (ii) when introducing the reach–avoid HJI/level-set machinery and its winning-region/feedback-strategy outputs, and (iii) whenever human-in-the-loop or automation-assisted adversarial decision making (deception, decision support, winning-region-guided search) needs an experimental anchor. It is the journal twin of the ICRA 2011 conference version [1] and the natural numerical counterpart to closed-form CTF treatments (e.g., the Garcia–Casbeer–Pachter line, P038), which cite it as "the numerical-approximation" baseline. Ready-to-adapt citation sentences: "Huang et al. formulated one-versus-one capture-the-flag as a two-stage zero-sum reach–avoid differential game and computed complete winning regions and feedback strategies as viscosity solutions of constrained Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs equations, validating the solutions on the BEARCAT testbed with human agents and providing the first complete solution to the 1-v-1 CTF game [P040]." Or, in a methods paragraph: "Multistage reach–avoid winning regions can be composed by intersecting each stage's feasible target set with the downstream stage's reach–avoid set and solving the upstream HJI problem over the composed target — the construction introduced by Huang, Ding, Zhang and Tomlin in their automation-assisted capture-the-flag framework [P040]."
