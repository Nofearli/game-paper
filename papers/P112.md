## 2. Research Background
The lifeline game, introduced by Isaacs (Example 9.5.1 of [11] in the paper), is a qualitative two-player pursuit-evasion game in which a slower-or-equal evader must reach an infinite straight line (the "lifeline") before a faster pursuer captures it. This paper extends it to three players — two pursuers and one evader with simple motion in a half-plane — motivated by borderline patrol with UAVs (a reconnaissance UAV must slip back across a border line while enemy UAVs intercept) and by oil-pipeline protection with sensor networks. Multi-player pursuit-evasion games become intractable at three players because of the variety of terminal conditions, pursuer cooperation, and the evader's tradeoff between multiple goals (reaching the lifeline vs. avoiding capture). Two known remedy paradigms exist: decomposition of the game into sub-games on the terminal manifold (fishing game, game of three cars, three-player suicidal pedestrian game) and explicit-policy/geometric construction of winning behaviors (cooperative homicidal chauffeur). The paper proposes a hybrid: a decomposition method based on explicit policy, integrating the analytic barriers of two two-player sub-games and then partitioning the escape zone into regions, each with a dedicated evasion policy.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
**Problem** [authors' formulation, Sec. 2, p. 4]: two pursuers $P_1, P_2$ and one evader $E$ with simple motion in the half-plane $\mathscr{R} = \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2 : y\ge 0\}$ bounded by the lifeline $\mathscr{L} = \{y = 0\}$; pursuers win if $\min\{|EP_1|,|EP_2|\} < l$ (capture radius $l\ge 0$) while $y_e > 0$; evader wins if it reaches $\mathscr{L}$ ($y_e = 0$) with $\min\{d_1,d_2\}\ge l$. Two questions: (a) the initial position distribution guaranteeing evader victory (the escape zone); (b) the corresponding evasion strategies. Only $w := v_p/v_e \ge 1$ is treated, since $v_p < v_e$ is trivial (whole space is the escape zone) [authors' claim — correct: a faster evader can always run straight to the line].

**Gap** [authors' claim, Sec. 3, p. 4]: the classic game-of-kind approach — integrate the retrogressive path equations (RPEs) from the boundary of the usable part (BUP) of the terminal manifold — "cannot be applied directly in a three-player lifeline game because a component of the terminal manifold, $\min\{d_1,d_2\} = l$, is indeterminate," i.e., the capture terminal surface switches non-smoothly between the two pursuers. [Verified from the paper's own setup, eqs. (2), Sec. 3 termination conditions]: the terminal manifold is indeed the union of two capture conditions plus the evader's $y_e=0$, so a single BUP does not exist for the joint game; the paper's remedy — split by pursuer, solve each two-player lifeline sub-game exactly, and integrate the barriers — is consistent with the decomposition precedent it cites [13,27,29]. [Authors' claim, p. 3]: the methods of [25,27,29] "cannot be used in a three-player lifeline game directly" because of the lifeline and different game rules. [Verified as plausible]: those works target confinement/collision-avoidance payoffs without a target line; the paper offers no formal impossibility, so this is an assertion. [Authors' claim, p. 3]: the method "is more scalable than that of [25,27,29] for lifeline games with more than two pursuers" — supported only by the 3- and 4-pursuer examples of Fig. 7, not by a general construction (see Sec. 7).

## 4. Core Contributions
1. **Theory (barrier of the two-player sub-game)**: closed-form parametric barrier $\mathscr{B}_1$ (and by symmetry $\mathscr{B}_2$) of the $E$ vs. $P_i$ lifeline sub-game in the reduced state space, via Isaacs' main equation, optimal strategies, and RPE integration from the whole BUP (Theorem 1, p. 5, eq. (6); p. 6, eq. (17)).
2. **Theory (barrier of the three-player game)**: the three-player barrier $\mathscr{B}$ is the "bond" of $\mathscr{B}_1$ and $\mathscr{B}_2$ — with pursuers fixed at $(-u,h_1)$, $(u,h_2)$, the evader's barrier trajectory is the piecewise curve (20) switching at the intersection $(\tilde x,\tilde y)$ of the two sub-game curves (Theorem 2, p. 6, eqs. (18)–(20)); capture zone of the joint game = union of sub-game capture zones because "the pursuers play individually."
3. **Modeling (pursuer payoffs and optimal play)**: quadratic payoff $J_i = k_1 d_i^2 - k_2 y_e^2$ (eq. (21)) yielding an explicit line-of-sight (pure-pursuit) optimal strategy for each pursuer, eq. (23).
4. **Algorithm (evasion policies per region)**: explicit partition of the escape zone into regions A, B, C, D (Fig. 6) with (i) sub-game optimal strategy (10) in the outer regions A/D, (ii) vertical-dash (shortest-time) strategy certified by the feasibility condition (24)–(25) in region B, and (iii) a novel **resultant force method** — repulsive forces from the pursuers plus a gravitational pull of the whole lifeline, eqs. (26)–(29) — balancing the active goal (reach $\mathscr{L}$) and passive goal (avoid capture) in region C.
5. **Experiment (validation/scalability)**: numerical examples: two evasion strategies compared from identical initial positions (Fig. 5); escape-zone partition (Fig. 6); lifeline games with three and four pursuers won by the evader (Fig. 7); prediction that circle/polygon lifelines are handled by the same machinery.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (MOST IMPORTANT)

### 5.0 Game setup (exactly as printed)
- **Dynamics** (p. 3, eqs. (3)–(4)), simple motion in the half-plane, headings measured from the downward vertical:
$$\dot x_{p_i} = v_p\sin\phi_i,\quad \dot y_{p_i} = -v_p\cos\phi_i,\ i=1,2; \qquad \dot x_e = v_e\sin\phi,\quad \dot y_e = -v_e\cos\phi .$$
Both pursuers share the same speed $v_p$; evader speed $v_e$; $w = v_p/v_e \ge 1$ (p. 4).
- **Information structure** (p. 4): "the instantaneous positions and velocities of the players are available to their opponents" — perfect full-state information, perfect recall.
- **Termination/winning conditions** (p. 4, Sec. 3): (1) $y_e = 0$ and $\min\{d_1,d_2\}\ge l$ $\Rightarrow$ $E$ wins; (2) $y_e > 0$ and $\min\{d_1,d_2\} < l$ $\Rightarrow$ pursuers win; otherwise the game proceeds. Terminal manifold (Def. 1, eq. (2)): $\mathcal{D} = \{x,t \mid G(x(t),t)=0\}$; for the joint game one component $\min\{d_1,d_2\}=l$ is "indeterminate," which blocks the classic single-BUP RPE construction (p. 4).
- **Reduced state** for sub-game 1 (p. 4): $x_1 = x_{p_1}-x_e$, $y_1 = y_{p_1}$, so (eq. (5)):
$$\dot x_1 = v_p\sin\phi_1 - v_e\sin\phi,\quad \dot y_1 = -v_p\cos\phi_1,\quad \dot y_e = -v_e\cos\phi .$$
- **Roles**: pursuers are homogeneous (same speed, same payoff form); the lifeline is the evader's target set; the evader has a **two-goal** (two-irrelevant-goals) structure: $y_e = 0$ (its win) and $\min\{d_1,d_2\} < l$ (its loss), p. 6–7.

### 5.1 Theorem 1 (p. 5, eq. (6)) — barrier of the E–P1 sub-game
**Statement.** The barrier $\mathscr{B}_1$ of the two-player lifeline sub-game of $E$ and $P_1$, in the reduced state $(x_1,y_1,y_e)$, is
$$x_1(\tau) = \Big[l + \big(v_p - \tfrac{v_e}{w}\big)\tau\Big]\sin s,\qquad
y_1(\tau) = (l + v_p\tau)\cos s,\qquad
y_e(\tau) = \frac{v_e\tau}{w}\sqrt{w^2-\sin^2 s},\tag{6}$$
with retrogressive time $\tau = t_f - t \in [0,\infty]$ and parameter $s\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$. (Zoomed rendering confirms the speed term is $v_p - v_e/w$, i.e., $v_e(w^2-1)/w$, not $(v_p-v_e)/w$.)
**Assumptions**: simple motion; $w\ge 1$; half-plane play ($y_1\ge 0$, $y_e\ge 0$); capture radius $l$; perfect information.
**Proof approach** (Isaacs' classic recipe): main equation of the game of kind (eq. (7)–(8)):
$$\min_{\phi_1}\max_{\phi}\big[\lambda_1\dot x_1 + \lambda_2\dot y_1 + \lambda_3\dot y_e\big] = 0
\;\Leftrightarrow\; \min_{\phi_1}\max_{\phi}\big[v_p(\lambda_1\sin\phi_1-\lambda_2\cos\phi_1) - v_e(\lambda_1\sin\phi+\lambda_3\cos\phi)\big]=0 .$$
Optimal strategies from (8) (eqs. (9)–(10)): $\sin\phi_1^* = -\lambda_1/\rho_1$, $\cos\phi_1^* = \lambda_2/\rho_1$, $\rho_1=\sqrt{\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2}$; $\sin\phi^* = -\lambda_1/\rho_2$, $\cos\phi^* = -\lambda_3/\rho_2$, $\rho_2=\sqrt{\lambda_1^2+\lambda_3^2}$. Substitution gives the decisive algebraic condition (eq. (11)): $-w\rho_1 + \rho_2 = 0$, $w = v_p/v_e$ (i.e., $\rho_2 = w\rho_1$).
**BUP** (eqs. (13)–(16)): the evader's terminal manifold $\mathscr{D}_e=\{y_e=0, d_1\ge l\}$ and pursuer's $\mathscr{D}_p=\{y_e>0, d_1<l\}$ are separated by the boundary $\mathscr{G}_1=\{y_e=0, d_1=l\}$, parameterized $x_1 = l\sin s,\ y_1 = l\cos s,\ y_e = 0$, $s\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$; the normal condition $\lambda_1\dot x_1+\lambda_2\dot y_1+\lambda_3\dot y_e = 0$ (eq. (14)) on (13) yields $\lambda_1=\sin s,\ \lambda_2=\cos s$ (eq. (15)), whence $\rho_1 = 1$, $\rho_2 = w = \sqrt{\sin^2 s+\lambda_3^2}$ and $\lambda_3 = \pm\sqrt{w^2-\sin^2 s}$ (eq. (16)). Because $w\ge 1$, $\lambda_3$ is real for every $s$, so **the entire boundary $\mathscr{G}_1$ is usable (BUP)** — the key structural fact.
**Decisive integration** (eq. (12), RPEs): $\mathring x_1 = -\dot x_1 = v_e\lambda_1\big(\tfrac{w}{\rho_1}-\tfrac{1}{\rho_2}\big)$, $\mathring y_1 = -\dot y_1 = \tfrac{v_p\lambda_2}{\rho_1}$, $\mathring y_e = -\dot y_e = -\tfrac{v_e\lambda_3}{\rho_2}$, $\mathring\lambda_i = 0$ (costates constant along retrogressive trajectories). With $\rho_1=1,\rho_2=w,\lambda_1=\sin s,\lambda_2=\cos s$ and integrating from (13): $\dot x_1$-coefficient $v_e(w - 1/w) = v_e(w^2-1)/w = v_p - v_e/w$ reproduces (6) exactly (verified by this archive's independent integration); the sign of $\lambda_3$ is fixed implicitly so that $y_e(\tau)\ge 0$.
**Semipermeability/zones** (p. 5, Fig. 2): region enclosed by $\mathscr{B}_1$ and the plane $0\text{–}x_1y_e$ = capture zone; remainder = escape zone. Fig. 2 plots the 3-D barrier surface for $v_p=1.5, v_e=1, l=2$.
**Physical meaning**: $\mathscr{B}_1$ is exactly the classic Isaacs lifeline barrier transported to the reduced space; projected into the realistic plane with $P_1$ fixed, it intersects the lifeline at $X = \pm\sqrt{l^2-h_1^2}-u$ when the pursuer is within capture radius distance $h_1\le l$ of the line (independent check), and its asymptotes are the lines (19) below.
**Extensibility**: identical form for $P_2$ (eq. (17)) with $x_2 = x_{p_2}-x_e$, $y_2 = y_{p_2}$, parameter $\bar s\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$; this symmetry is what makes the integration step of Theorem 2 possible.

### 5.2 Theorem 2 (p. 6, eqs. (18)–(20)) — barrier of the three-player lifeline game
**Statement.** With $P_1 = (-u,h_1)$, $P_2 = (u,h_2)$ fixed (positions parameterized w.r.t. the lifeline) and $E=(X,Y)$, the barrier $\mathscr{B}$ of the three-player game is **the bond of $\mathscr{B}_1$ and $\mathscr{B}_2$**: the evader trajectory on $\mathscr{B}$ is, from (6)–(17) (eq. (18)):
$$X = \Big[l + \frac{(w^2-1)(h_1-l\cos s)}{w^2\cos s}\Big]\sin s - u,\qquad
Y = \frac{(h_1-l\cos s)\sqrt{w^2-\sin^2 s}}{w^2\cos s},$$
$$X = \Big[l + \frac{(w^2-1)(h_2-l\cos\bar s)}{w^2\cos\bar s}\Big]\sin\bar s + u,\qquad
Y = \frac{(h_2-l\cos\bar s)\sqrt{w^2-\sin^2\bar s}}{w^2\cos\bar s},$$
for $s,\bar s\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$; at $s,\bar s=\pm\pi/2$ the curves degenerate to the asymptotic lines (eq. (19)):
$$X = \pm\big(Y\sqrt{w^2-1} + l\big) - u,\qquad X = \pm\big(Y\sqrt{w^2-1}+l\big) + u$$
(rendering confirms $Y\sqrt{w^2-1}+l$, not $Y/\sqrt{w^2-1}$; verified as the exact asymptote by expanding (18) as $s\to\pi/2$). If the two curves intersect at $(\tilde x,\tilde y)$, then (eq. (20)):
$$(X,Y) = \begin{cases}
\Big(\big[l+\frac{(w^2-1)(h_1-l\cos s)}{w^2\cos s}\big]\sin s - u,\ \frac{(h_1-l\cos s)\sqrt{w^2-\sin^2 s}}{w^2\cos s}\Big), & X\le\tilde x,\\[4pt]
\Big(\big[l+\frac{(w^2-1)(h_2-l\cos\bar s)}{w^2\cos\bar s}\big]\sin\bar s + u,\ \frac{(h_2-l\cos\bar s)\sqrt{w^2-\sin^2\bar s}}{w^2\cos\bar s}\Big), & X > \tilde x .
\end{cases}$$
**Assumptions**: fixed pursuer anchoring $(-u,h_1)$, $(u,h_2)$; $w\ge 1$; individual (non-cooperative but common-interest) pursuers.
**Proof approach** (qualitative, geometric — p. 6, Fig. 3): above the blue curve ($\mathscr{B}_1$) is the capture zone of sub-game 1; above the green curve ($\mathscr{B}_2$) that of sub-game 2; "because the pursuers play individually in the three-player lifeline game, the whole game can be regarded as a superposition of two sub-games": from any point above both curves (union of capture zones) the pursuers win by each playing his own sub-game-optimal strategy; from any point below both curves the evader "can play optimally to reach the lifeline." Hence $\mathscr{B}$ is the pointwise upper envelope (bond) of the two curves, switching at $(\tilde x,\tilde y)$.
**Decisive step**: the transport from reduced-space barrier (6) to realistic-space curve (18) using $y_1(\tau)=h_1 \Rightarrow \tau = (h_1-l\cos s)/(v_p\cos s)$ and $v_e/(w v_p) = 1/w^2$ — verified algebraically by this archive.
**Local/global, analytic/numerical**: analytic and global in $s,\bar s$; the switching abscissa $\tilde x$ is **not given in closed form** — it is read off Fig. 3 (numerical).
**Physical meaning**: the escape zone of the three-player game is the region below both barrier curves; a pursuer farther from the lifeline than $l$ ($h_i > l$) yields a barrier that never touches $\mathscr{L}$.
**Extensibility**: the bond argument needs only one barrier per pursuer, which is why the authors claim scalability to $n$ pursuers (each new pursuer adds one curve); the construction is restricted to "accessible boundaries" (p. 3), with circle/polygon lifelines predicted workable (p. 10).
**Proof gap (escape side)**: the pursuer side of Theorem 2 is airtight (a point above $\mathscr{B}_i$ is lost for $E$ against $P_i$ alone regardless of the other players). The escape side — below both curves $E$ wins — is asserted by superposition, but in the between-pursuers region $E$ must beat **two** pursuers with one strategy; no single feedback strategy certifying this is constructed at barrier level. The paper covers this region only at policy level (Sec. 4: vertical dash under (25), resultant force (28)) with example validation, so Theorem 2's escape-zone claim in the between region is a posteriori rather than proven (p. 6 proof, p. 7–9).

### 5.3 Pursuer optimal strategies (p. 7, eqs. (21)–(23))
**Payoff** (eq. (21)): $J_i = k_1 d_i^2 - k_2 y_e^2 = k_1[x_i^2 + (y_i-y_e)^2] - k_2 y_e^2$, $i=1,2$, $k_1,k_2>0$ (weights of $d_i^2$ and $-y_e^2$; $x_i := x_{p_i}-x_e$, $y_i := y_{p_i}$ in realistic coordinates here).
**Derivative** (eq. (22)), verified by this archive's expansion:
$$\dot J_i = 2k_1 v_p[x_i\sin\phi_i + (y_e-y_i)\cos\phi_i] - 2v_e[k_1 x_i\sin\phi + (k_1 y_e - k_2 y_e - k_1 y_i)\cos\phi].$$
**Result**: whatever $\phi$ is, $P_i$ minimizes $\dot J_i$ with (eq. (23)):
$$\sin\phi_i^* = -\frac{x_i}{d_i},\qquad \cos\phi_i^* = \frac{y_i-y_e}{d_i},\qquad d_i = \sqrt{x_i^2+(y_i-y_e)^2}.$$
Typographical note: the printed middle expression in (23) reads $-x_i/(x_i^2+(y_i-y_e)^2)$ — the denominator is missing the square root; the final form $-x_i/d_i$ (and Fig. 4 geometry) shows pure pursuit is meant. Geometric reading (p. 7): $P_i$ moves straight at the evader to maintain line-of-sight; the paper calls it an "optimal open-loop strategy" although it depends only on the current state (i.e., it is a state-feedback pure-pursuit law).
**Physical meaning**: pursuers' goals reduce to shrinking $d_i$ and growing $y_e$; (23) is strategy-robust for the pursuers (independent of $\phi$), which is what licenses treating pursuer behavior as known in the evader-policy design.

### 5.4 Vertical-dash feasibility (p. 8, eqs. (24)–(25)) — region B policy
**Statement.** If $E$ dashes straight down (ignoring the passive goal), the pursuers' best response is to move straight to the landing point $(X,0)$ (closed-loop), giving interception-time budgets (eq. (24)):
$$\triangle t = \frac{Y}{v_e} \le \min\Big\{\frac{D_1-l}{v_p},\ \frac{D_2-l}{v_p}\Big\},\qquad
D_1 = \sqrt{h_1^2+(X+u)^2},\quad D_2 = \sqrt{h_2^2+(X-u)^2},$$
i.e., the evader wins by the vertical dash iff (eq. (25)):
$$(Yw+l)^2 \le \min\big\{h_1^2+(X+u)^2,\ h_2^2+(X-u)^2\big\}.$$
**Derivation** (verified): $Y/v_e \le (D_i-l)/v_p \Leftrightarrow Yw + l \le D_i$, squared. Assumptions: $E$ in the escape zone between the pursuers; pursuers respond by heading to $(X,0)$ — this is the optimal response to a committed straight-line dash, so (25) certifies region B as a vertical-dash winning region (shortest escape time $y_e(0)/v_e$, p. 9). The red curves in Fig. 6 are exactly the boundary (25).
**Physical meaning**: the "reach the line before anyone can reach my landing spot" condition; $(D_i - l)/v_p$ is the time for $P_i$ to close to within capture radius of the landing point.

### 5.5 Resultant force method (p. 8, eqs. (26)–(29)) — region C policy
**Statement.** Treat players as mass points: $E$ subject to repulsive force $F_1$ from $P_1$, repulsive $F_2$ from $P_2$, and gravitational $F_0$ from the whole lifeline $\mathscr{L}$ (Fig. 4). Resultant components (eq. (26)): $F_x = -F_1\sin\alpha - F_2\sin\beta$, $F_y = F_1\cos\alpha + F_2\cos\beta + F_0$, with directions (eq. (27)):
$$\sin\alpha = \frac{-u-X}{d_1},\ \cos\alpha = \frac{h_1-Y}{d_1};\qquad \sin\beta = \frac{u-X}{d_2},\ \cos\beta = \frac{h_2-Y}{d_2}.$$
Evader heading = direction of the resultant (eq. (28)): $\sin\phi^* = F_x/\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2}$, $\cos\phi^* = F_y/\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2}$.
**Magnitudes** (p. 8): $F_0 = 2/Y$, $F_1 = 1/d_1^2$, $F_2 = 1/d_2^2$ (unit coefficients). The lifeline pull is derived as the continuum resultant of inverse-square attractions of unit masses at $(X+s,0)$, whose vertical component is $Y(s^2+Y^2)^{-3/2}$; horizontal components cancel by symmetry (eq. (29)):
$$F_0 = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} Y(s^2+Y^2)^{-3/2}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{2}{Y}$$
(integral verified: antiderivative $s/(Y\sqrt{s^2+Y^2})$ gives $2/Y$).
**Proof gap / sign inconsistency**: with (27) as printed, the $x$-components $-F_1\sin\alpha$ push $E$ *away* from each pursuer (repulsive, as announced), but the $y$-components $+F_1\cos\alpha$ (with $\cos\alpha=(h_1-Y)/d_1>0$ for a pursuer above $E$) pull $E$ *toward* the pursuer, contradicting the "repulsive force" description; a fully repulsive reading would require $F_y = -F_1\cos\alpha - F_2\cos\beta + F_0$. Since (28) uses only the direction and $F_0 = 2/Y$ dominates numerically (e.g., at the Fig. 5(b) initial state: $F_y^{printed} = 0.1389$ vs. $F_y^{repulsive} = 0.1277$, heading differs by $\approx 0.4^\circ$), the policy is well-defined but its stated mechanics are internally inconsistent (p. 8, eq. (26) vs. (27)). Also, the text says force magnitude is "inversely proportional to the distance" while assigning $1/d_i^2$ (inverse square).
**Physical meaning**: an artificial-potential-style heuristic balancing the active goal (lifeline gravity $\propto 2/Y$, stronger near the line) and passive goal (stronger repulsion from the nearer pursuer), replacing the case analysis of when a pure active-goal dash fails (25).
**Local/global**: heuristic; no guarantee, locality, or optimality claim is proven — the region-C win is claimed ("it can choose the resultant force strategy given by (28) to win the game", p. 9) and demonstrated on one scenario (Fig. 5(b)).

### 5.6 Escape-zone partition and timing (p. 9, Fig. 6)
With $P_1 = (-20,5)$, $P_2 = (20,20)$, $w = 1.2$, $l = 2$ (Fig. 6): region **A** (left of $P_1$, outside its influence) and **D** (right of $P_2$): use sub-game strategy (10) — only one sub-game affects the outcome (p. 7); region **B** (escape-zone band satisfying (25)): vertical dash, escape time $y_e(0)/v_e$; region **C** (escape zone between the pursuers violating (25)): resultant force (28); capture zone: evader loses. Timing bounds (p. 9): maximum capture time $\min\{d_1(0),d_2(0)\}/(v_p-v_e)$ (as printed, under the known open-loop pursuer strategies). Strategy (10) recalled: $\sin\phi^* = -\lambda_1/\rho_2$, $\cos\phi^* = -\lambda_3/\rho_2$ — the two-player barrier-optimal evasion heading of the relevant sub-game.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated (p. 10, Conclusion)**: the study is qualitative only; future work targets both qualitative and quantitative solutions of multi-player pursuit-evasion games with more complex rules/environments — incomplete knowledge about opponents, resource constraints, complicated dynamics, and obstacle environments — plus general methodologies for multi-player games. Earlier (p. 2–3) the authors acknowledge the explicit-policy method is "limited to relatively simple game environments and simple motion models."

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. **Escape side of Theorem 2 is not proven at barrier level** (p. 6 proof): superposition gives the pursuer side rigorously, but a *single* evader feedback strategy winning against both pursuers simultaneously below both curves is never constructed; the between-pursuers escape zone is instead covered by policies (25)/(28) validated by example. The phrase "he can play optimally to reach the lifeline" (p. 6) is the entire argument.
2. **Resultant-force policy lacks any guarantee** (p. 8, eqs. (26)–(29), claim at p. 9): no proof that (28) wins for all of region C, no adversarial analysis (Fig. 5(b) pits it against the fixed pure-pursuit law (23), while Fig. 5(a) pits the vertical dash against a different, stronger response — an asymmetric comparison), and no robustness/sensitivity study of the ad hoc unit gains $F_0 = 2/Y$, $F_i = 1/d_i^2$.
3. **Sign inconsistency in eq. (26)**: the $F_y$ terms $+F_1\cos\alpha + F_2\cos\beta$ attract $E$ toward the pursuers vertically while $F_x$ repels horizontally, contradicting the "repulsive force" definition and Fig. 4 (see 5.5); likely a typo, but as printed the mechanics are inconsistent.
4. **Typo in eq. (23)** (p. 7): printed $-x_i/(x_i^2+(y_i-y_e)^2)$ should be $-x_i/\sqrt{x_i^2+(y_i-y_e)^2}$; the final $-x_i/d_i$ is correct. Also "open-loop" is a misnomer for a state-dependent law.
5. **$\tilde x$ not analytic**: the switching point of (20) is only graphical (Fig. 3); no closed form or algorithm is given (p. 6).
6. **Scalability is empirical** (p. 9–10, Fig. 7): the $n>2$ pursuer claim rests on two simulations; no formal $n$-pursuer barrier/bond construction, and heterogeneous speeds (per-pursuer $v_{p_i}$, which would distort (18)) are not treated — both pursuers share $v_p$ throughout.
7. **Capture radius absent from region-C policy**: (28) ignores $l$; the force law does not reference the capture condition $\min d_i < l$, so the claimed win in region C is not tied to the actual terminal condition.
8. **Interception model in (24) is asserted**: heading to $(X,0)$ is claimed to be the pursuers' best response to a vertical dash; the budget $(D_i-l)/v_p$ prescribes straight-line pursuer paths, reasonable but not proven optimal (p. 8).
9. **Minor**: wording "inversely proportional to the distance" vs. $1/d^2$ assignments (p. 8); Acknowledgements print "Grant No. 1613225" (as printed; evidently malformed); several typographical errors ("blew" for below, "close-loop", "relay on", "between with") do not affect the math. The claim $F_0 = 2/Y$ holds for the infinite line only; for the predicted circle/polygon lifelines (p. 10) the gravity integral must be redone.

## 8. Citation Value
**Paragraph role**: P112 is the reference point for the *lifeline (reach-a-line) variant of three-player pursuit-evasion*: it is, to this archive's knowledge, among the first to give an explicit analytic barrier for two pursuers vs. one evader with a line target, by combining Isaacs-style barrier construction (Theorem 1) with a decomposition/bond integration (Theorem 2) and an explicit-policy layer (regions A–D) that operationalizes the escape zone. In a reach-avoid/target-defense paper it serves three citation roles: (i) as the canonical example that the joint terminal manifold $\min_i d_i = l$ blocks the classic BUP approach, motivating decomposition per pursuer (alongside Zha et al.'s fishing game [13] and the three-car/suicidal-pedestrian precedents [27,29]); (ii) as a source of the explicit two-branch barrier formula (18)–(20) and the vertical-dash feasibility test (25), both directly comparable to Apollonius-circle/HJI-based reach-avoid characterizations for faster pursuers; and (iii) as an early instance of artificial-potential (resultant-force) evasion balancing a reaching objective against avoidance, which is a natural baseline for any learned or optimized evader policy in lifeline/borderline scenarios. Its weakness — heuristic guarantees in the between-pursuers region — makes it equally citable as the motivation for rigorous multi-pursuer lifeline analyses.
**Suggested citation sentences**: "For the three-player lifeline game, Zhang and Zha [P112] decompose the game into two two-player sub-games, integrate their barriers into an explicit two-branch escape-zone boundary, and partition the escape zone into regions with distinct evasion policies, including a resultant-force law balancing lifeline attraction against pursuer repulsion." Or, shorter: "Analytic barriers and region-based evasion strategies for a two-pursuer/one-evader lifeline game were derived via a decomposition method based on explicit policy [P112]."
