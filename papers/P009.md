## 2. Research Background

This letter studies perimeter defense in an inherently one-dimensional environment — a line segment modeling a river, road, or narrow passage — where a single defending vehicle must protect a compact target region from intruders that appear at the ends of the segment and move inward at fixed speed (p. 1, Sec. I). Prior game-theoretic formulations of perimeter defense, from Isaacs' pursuit-evasion framing [4] to reach-avoid models and perimeter-constrained defenders [5]–[7], assume the intruder locations and their number are known a priori (p. 1) [authors' claim]. When intruders arrive over time according to a stochastic process, the standard tool is dynamic vehicle routing, which yields average-case guarantees but "essentially ignore[s] the worst-case where intruders may coordinate their release locations and times to overwhelm the defense" (p. 1) [authors' claim]. The authors' own earlier work [15]–[17] introduced competitive (worst-case) analysis for this problem class but restricted attention to deterministic online algorithms (p. 1) [authors' claim]. This paper is the first in that line to consider randomized online algorithms, using Yao's minimax principle to prove algorithm-independent lower bounds and designing three randomized algorithms whose competitive ratios provably beat the deterministic frontier in part of the parameter space (p. 1–2, Sec. I–IV).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** An adversary releases intruders at the endpoints ±1 of the segment E = [−1, +1]; each intruder moves at fixed speed v > 0 toward the nearest point of the perimeter ∂R(ρ) = {+ρ, −ρ} that bounds the protected region R(ρ) = {y ∈ R : |y| ≤ ρ < 1} (p. 2, Sec. II). A single vehicle, modeled as a first-order (single) integrator with unit speed, must intercept (become coincident with) intruders before they reach the perimeter (p. 1–2). At any instant at most 2N intruders are released (N ∈ Z⁺), and consecutive release events are separated by at least Δ ∈ R⁺ with 0 < Δ < 2 (if Δ ≥ 2 the problem is trivial since the vehicle can cover the environment between arrivals) (p. 2). An input sequence is I = {t, n(t), {−1,+1}^{n(t)}}ᵀ_{t=0} (T possibly infinite) and a problem instance is P(ρ, v, N, Δ) (p. 2). The performance metric is the competitive ratio against an optimal offline algorithm O that knows the entire input in advance (Definition 2, p. 2): c_A(I) = n_O(I)/E[n_{A,i}(I)] ≥ 1, c_A(P) = sup_I c_A(I), c(P) = inf_A c_A(P), where the expectation is over the algorithm's internal randomization. The adversary is the oblivious adversary (Definition 1, p. 2): it commits to the whole input sequence in advance, possibly knowing the algorithm but not the outcomes of its coin flips.

**Gap (with provenance).**
- All prior competitive perimeter-defense results by this group ([15] ACC 2021 line/conical environments, [16] turret defense, [17] multi-vehicle conical environments) cover only the class of deterministic online algorithms; "the key distinction of the current work is its focus on randomized online algorithms" (p. 1) [authors' claim].
- Game-theoretic/reach-avoid approaches ([4]–[7]) "require that the intruder locations and their number be known a priori" (p. 1) [authors' claim].
- Stochastic-process/dynamic-vehicle-routing approaches [10]–[14] give average-case, not worst-case, guarantees (p. 1) [authors' claim].
- Within the deterministic setting, [15, Th. III.2] showed that when v ≥ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) every deterministic online algorithm has competitive ratio at least 2 (p. 6, Sec. V) [authors' claim, characterizing their own reference]. The open question addressed here is whether randomization strictly improves this frontier. The answer given is yes: randomized algorithms with competitive ratios strictly below 2 exist even at v = (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) (p. 6, Sec. V).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Fundamental lower bound, 2-competitive regime (theory)**: For any instance with v > (1−ρ)/ρ, every randomized online algorithm has c(P) ≥ 2 against an oblivious adversary (Theorem 1, p. 3). Hence in this regime randomization cannot help at all — the randomized frontier coincides with the deterministic one.
2. **Fundamental lower bound, 4/3-competitive regime (theory)**: For v ≥ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) and Δ < ε (ε defined in the theorem statement), every randomized online algorithm has c(P) ≥ 4/3 ≈ 1.33 (Theorem 2, p. 3). This is the first randomized lower bound for the problem and shows the deterministic 2 bound of [15, Th. III.2] cannot be the whole story near that speed boundary.
3. **Algorithm Stay and a universal 2-competitive guarantee (algorithm + theory)**: A memoryless open-loop algorithm that commits to a randomly chosen perimeter point ±ρ and stays there is 2-competitive for every instance P(ρ, v, N, Δ), and the bound is tight (Theorem 3, p. 3–4). This is impossible with deterministic algorithms, which suffer ratio > 2 in part of the parameter space (p. 6, Sec. V) [authors' claim, citing (15)].
4. **Algorithm Rand-Stay and a parameter-dependent tight bound (algorithm + theory)**: A randomized wait-then-switch endpoint policy with competitive ratio (1 + 2Np)/(3/4 + Np) when v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ), where p (eq. (1), p. 4) counts the adversary's possible releases inside any time window of length < 2; the bound is tight (Theorem 4, p. 4–5). The ratio approaches the 4/3 lower bound as the product Np → 0.
5. **Algorithm Sleep-Switch and an N-independent tight bound (algorithm + theory)**: A randomized sleep-duration policy using knowledge of Δ, with competitive ratio (2p+2)/(p+2) when v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ), tight and independent of N (Theorem 5, p. 5, Algorithm 1). The ratio → 4/3 as p → 1 and → 2 as p → ∞.
6. **Parameter-regime synthesis (modeling/analysis synthesis)**: A (ρ, v) regime plot (Fig. 2, p. 6) delimiting where each lower bound holds and where each algorithm is finite/near-optimal, plus an explicit demonstration of the power of randomization: at v = 0.25, ρ = 0.6, Δ = 0.2, c_Sleep-Switch ≈ 1.8 < 2, whereas any deterministic algorithm needs c ≥ 2 there (p. 6, Sec. V).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.0 Dynamics, information structure, payoff, termination (p. 1–2)

- **Dynamics.** Intruders: constant-speed, straight-line, first-order motion, ẏ = v · sign-inward, from endpoint ±1 toward the nearest perimeter point ±ρ; speed v > 0 fixed per instance. Vehicle: single integrator with unit speed, control = heading on [−1, 1]: u: I(t) × P(ρ,v,N,Δ) → [−1,1] (deterministic) and u_rand: I(t) × P(ρ,v,N,Δ) × Ω → [−1,1] (randomized), where Ω is the (non-empty) sample space of the randomization (p. 2). Crossing the full segment takes exactly 2 time units (used repeatedly, e.g., p. 4–5).
- **Information structure.** Online algorithms see only the input prefix I(t); the offline optimum O sees the entire sequence I in advance; the adversary is oblivious (Definition 1, p. 2) — it fixes I before the algorithm's random outcomes are drawn.
- **Capture / breach / winning conditions.** Capture = coincidence of vehicle and intruder positions; breach = intruder reaches ∂R(ρ) uncaptured; "capturable" = there exists a vehicle motion capturing it before breach (p. 2). Payoff = number of intruders captured, n_A(I); there is no other terminal cost. The vehicle "wins" on an intruder by capturing it; the adversary "wins" by causing breaches. Termination is at the end of the (possibly infinite) input horizon T; ratios are defined per input sequence, not per unit time (Definition 2, p. 2).
- **Payoff/competitive ratio.** c_A(I) = n_O(I)/E[n_{A,i}(I)] ≥ 1; c_A(P) = sup_I c_A(I); c(P) = inf_A c_A(P); A is c-competitive for P if c_A(P) ≤ c (Definition 2, p. 2). The design goal (Problem Statement, p. 2) is to establish fundamental guarantees on c(P) and design c-competitive randomized algorithms.
- **Load constraints.** n(t) ≤ 2N per release instant; consecutive release instants ≥ Δ apart; 0 < Δ < 2 (p. 2). Notation i^{r_t}_{t,+1}: the r_t intruders released at endpoint +1 at time t (p. 2).

### 5.1 Lemma 1 (Yao's minimax principle) (p. 2–3, unnumbered display)

- **Statement**: Let X̄ be a probability distribution over a set of deterministic input sequences I_x, x ∈ X. If there exists a constant c ≥ 1 such that inf_{A_i ∈ A_det} {E_X̄[n_O(I_x)] − c·E_X̄[n_{A,i}]} ≥ 0, then c is a lower bound on the competitive ratio of any randomized algorithm against an oblivious adversary.
- **Assumptions**: oblivious adversary (so that randomization over inputs dominates randomization over algorithms); n_O(I_x) is the offline optimum on each sampled sequence.
- **Proof approach**: (i) Yao's minimax duality argument [18] — the expected performance of the best deterministic algorithm on a random input lower-bounds the performance of any randomized algorithm on its worst input; (ii) the expectation exchanges sup over inputs with inf over deterministic algorithms; (iii) the printed inequality rearranges to E_X̄[n_O] ≥ c·inf_{A_i} E_X̄[n_{A,i}] ≥ c·E[n_A^rand] on the worst input.
- **Decisive inequality**: the displayed inf-condition itself (p. 3, top).
- **Physical meaning**: to prove a lower bound for all randomized algorithms it suffices to exhibit one probability distribution over deterministic input sequences on which every deterministic algorithm captures at most (1/c) of what the offline optimum captures in expectation.
- **Extensibility**: standard exportable tool for any online perimeter-defense/evasion setting with an oblivious adversary. Note: the authors cite [18] (Krumke's habilitation, 2002) rather than Yao's original papers [my observation]; the letter's Lemma 1 is a specialization of Yao's principle to capture-count payoffs.
- **Proof gap**: none; the lemma is quoted and applied rather than re-proved.

### 5.2 Theorem 1 (Necessary condition for c(P) ≥ 2) (p. 3)

- **Statement**: For any problem instance P(ρ, v, N, Δ) with v > (1−ρ)/ρ, c(P) ≥ 2 against an oblivious adversary.
- **Assumptions**: vehicle assumed to start at the origin ("We assume that the vehicle starts at the origin for both the optimal offline and the deterministic algorithms", p. 3); oblivious adversary; the release time t = ρ is chosen by the adversary.
- **Proof approach**: (i) construct the input family I = {I₁, I₂}: a single intruder i released at endpoint −1 (I₁) or +1 (I₂), both at time t = ρ, with distribution X̄ = {0.5, 0.5}; (ii) offline analysis: O knows the sequence, moves the vehicle ρ units toward the correct endpoint, reaching ±ρ by time ρ and hence intercepting the intruder before/at its perimeter arrival, giving E_X̄[n_O] = 1; (iii) online analysis: if the vehicle sits at the origin at the release instant, since (1−ρ)/v < ρ (equivalent to v > (1−ρ)/ρ), the intruder breaches before the vehicle can reach any point of its path — the intruder's position always remains at distance ≥ ρ from the origin until it breaches at (1−ρ)/v < ρ time units after release [the paper states the inequality (1−ρ)/v < ρ and the conclusion; the distance argument is my reconstruction, p. 3]; (iv) WLOG the vehicle is "located in (0, t] at time t" (printed verbatim — see proof-gap note below), i.e., on one side; it then captures the intruder on its own side (with probability 1/2 over X̄) and provably cannot capture the opposite one, so E_X̄[n_A] = 1/2; (v) apply Lemma 1 with c = 2: 1 − 2·(1/2) = 0 ≥ 0.
- **Decisive inequality**: (1−ρ)/v < ρ ⟺ v > (1−ρ)/ρ (p. 3), which simultaneously makes the origin-started vehicle fail and fixes the boundary curve of the regime.
- **Local/global**: global — the bound holds for all N and Δ (a single intruder suffices); analytic, exact (constant 2 attained by the construction).
- **Physical meaning**: when the intruder is fast relative to the geometry (it covers the endpoint-to-perimeter distance 1−ρ faster than the vehicle can cross half the segment, ρ), the adversary can play a single coin-flip intruder that no online algorithm catches with probability > 1/2, while the offline player always catches it.
- **Extensibility**: the same two-point coin-flip construction exports to any convex 1-D guarding domain; the threshold v = (1−ρ)/ρ is the "intruder can beat the vehicle to the far perimeter point" condition.
- **proof gap (minor)**: the WLOG phrase "the vehicle is located in (0, t] at time t" (p. 3) uses t (a time) where the positive-side interval (0, ρ] is meant — evidently a typo [my observation]; also, the case analysis over the vehicle's position at the release instant (origin vs. either side) is compressed: for a vehicle already positioned on one side at x ∈ (0, ρ], capture of the same-side intruder additionally requires ρ − x ≤ (1−ρ)/v, and in every position the expected capture is ≤ 1/2, so the bound survives, but the printed proof does not spell this out [my observation]. The abstract-style wording "necessary condition for c(P) ≥ 2" (p. 3) is slightly loose: the theorem gives a parameter regime in which c(P) ≥ 2 is forced (i.e., a sufficient condition for the lower bound) [my observation].

### 5.3 Theorem 2 (Necessary condition for c(P) ≥ 1.33) (p. 3)

- **Statement**: Let ε := 2ρv if v = (1−ρ)/(1+ρ), and ε := 1 + ρ − (1−ρ)/v if v > (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) (unnumbered display, p. 3). Then for any instance P with v ≥ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) and Δ < ε, c(P) ≥ 4/3 against an oblivious adversary.
- **Assumptions**: vehicle starts at the origin (O "can have the vehicle move to endpoint +1 arriving at endpoint +1 at exactly time 1", p. 3, which requires a unit-speed crossing of distance 1 from the origin); oblivious adversary; Δ < ε so that the constructed release times 1 and 1+ε are feasible (gap ε ≥ Δ).
- **Proof approach**: (i) input family I₁: single intruder i^{1}_{1,+1} at +1 at time 1 and single intruder i^{1}_{1+ε,−1} at −1 at time 1+ε; I₂ mirrors the endpoints; distribution X̄ = {0.5, 0.5}; (ii) offline: O drives to the correct endpoint, arriving exactly at time 1 (capture #1 upon release), then drives 1+ρ units toward the opposite perimeter point, arriving at time 2+ρ; by the choice of ε, 2+ρ ≤ 1+ε+(1−ρ)/v, i.e., the second intruder (which breaches at 1+ε+(1−ρ)/v) is still interceptable, so E_X̄[n_O] = 2 — this is exactly where ε = 1+ρ−(1−ρ)/v (equality case) and ε = 2ρv > 0 (the marginal-speed case, where (1−ρ)/v = 1+ρ so any ε ≥ 0 works and a positive ε is needed for feasibility) come from [my reconstruction of the design, p. 3]; (iii) online: the best deterministic play is to commit to one endpoint by time 1 (staying near the origin captures strictly less, since the first intruder's breach at 1+(1−ρ)/v can then only be matched marginally); WLOG A is at +1 at time 1; (iv) against I₁, A captures both: the first at the endpoint, the second by heading to −ρ, arriving 2+ρ ≤ 1+ε+(1−ρ)/v; (v) against I₂, A captures exactly one: if v = (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) it captures only the first (a marginal capture at −ρ at the exact tie 2+ρ = 1+(1−ρ)/v, consistent with the weak inequality in Lemma 2), and the second is lost because returning from −ρ to +ρ takes 2ρ > ε = 2ρv (as v < 1) [the paper asserts the capture counts; the 2ρ > 2ρv argument is my reconstruction]; if v > (1−ρ)/(1+ρ), the first breaches before the vehicle can reach −ρ (needs 1+ρ > (1−ρ)/v) and A captures only the second by turning back; (vi) E_X̄[n_A] = (2+1)/2 = 3/2; (vii) Lemma 1 with c = 4/3: 2 − (4/3)(3/2) = 0 ≥ 0.
- **Decisive inequality**: 2 + ρ ≤ 1 + ε + (1−ρ)/v ⟺ ε ≥ 1 + ρ − (1−ρ)/v (p. 3), which is the definition of ε in the case v > (1−ρ)/(1+ρ); plus the tie condition (1−ρ)/v = 1+ρ at v = (1−ρ)/(1+ρ).
- **Local/global**: global in N; requires the release-rate condition Δ < ε, so the 4/3 bound does not cover instances with dense releases (large Δ) even in the speed regime [my observation]; analytic, exact (4/3 attained).
- **Physical meaning**: slightly below the Theorem-1 speed threshold, a coin-flip pair of staggered intruders guarantees every online algorithm only 3/2 expected captures versus 2 for the offline player, because committing to one endpoint sacrifices the mirror sequence's first intruder.
- **Extensibility**: the staggered-pair construction with ε tuned to the capture-time equality is the template for randomized lower bounds in endpoint-release guarding games.
- **proof gap (minor)**: the WLOG that the best deterministic algorithm moves to an endpoint by time 1 is asserted ("Observe that the best way to capture both intruders is to move the vehicle to one of the endpoint", p. 3) without a case analysis over arbitrary deterministic trajectories (e.g., hovering near the origin captures ≤ 1 as well, which supports the WLOG but is not written) [my observation].

### 5.4 Theorem 3 (Algorithm Stay: universal 2-competitiveness and tightness) (p. 3–4)

- **Algorithm** (Sec. IV-A, p. 3): at time 0 select a location from {−ρ, +ρ} uniformly (Ω = {−ρ, +ρ}); stay there until releases end. Open loop and memoryless.
- **Statement**: Algorithm Stay is 2-competitive against an oblivious adversary for any problem instance P(ρ, v, N, Δ); further, it is no better than 2-competitive (the bound is tight).
- **Proof approach**: (i) let R ≥ 0 and L ≥ 0 be the total numbers of intruders preplanned for endpoints +1 and −1 over [0, T]; (ii) the vehicle captures, in expectation, 0.5(R+L) — all intruders on its chosen side (the proof assumes the offline optimum captures all, i.e., n_O ≤ R+L, and counts same-side captures; under the origin start, an intruder on the chosen side is captured exactly when it is capturable from the origin at all, so E[n_A] ≥ 0.5·n_O [the paper writes the expectation as 0.5(R+L) directly "by assuming that there exists an optimal offline algorithm O that can capture all", p. 4; the capturability caveat is implicit — see proof-gap note]); (iii) Definition 2 gives c ≤ 2; (iv) tightness input I: starting at time ρ, R > 0 intruders arrive at +1 and none at −1; O moves to +ρ and captures all R; Stay captures 0.5R in expectation; ratio exactly 2.
- **Decisive equation**: E[n_A] = 0.5(R+L) (p. 4, unnumbered) and the tight-instance ratio n_O/E[n_A] = R/(0.5R) = 2.
- **Local/global**: global — holds for all v > 0, ρ ∈ [0,1), any N and Δ (p. 6, Sec. V). The choice of release time ρ in the tight instance is exactly what lets the offline player (and the committed vehicle) reach ±ρ from the origin before the first breach [my observation].
- **Physical meaning**: coin-flipping which side to guard guarantees half the loot no matter how the adversary times its releases; no deterministic algorithm can guarantee 2-competitiveness on the whole parameter space (p. 6).
- **proof gap (minor)**: the claim E[n_A] = 0.5(R+L) is exact only if every intruder arriving on the chosen side is capturable by a vehicle that goes there; for early releases with v > (1−ρ)/ρ some same-side intruders are uncapturable from the origin by anyone, which lowers n_O as well and preserves the ratio bound, but the printed proof does not perform this case split [my observation].

### 5.5 Lemma 2 (Crossing-capture condition) (p. 4)

- **Statement**: Suppose the vehicle is at endpoint +1 (resp. −1) at time t and l_t (resp. r_t) intruders arrive at endpoint −1 (resp. +1). Then the vehicle can capture them if v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ).
- **Proof approach**: the intruders reach the opposite perimeter point −ρ at time t + (1−ρ)/v; the vehicle, departing +1 at time t, reaches −ρ at t + 1 + ρ; interception is possible iff 1 + ρ ≤ (1−ρ)/v.
- **Decisive inequality**: 1 + ρ ≤ (1−ρ)/v ⟺ v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) (p. 4).
- **Physical meaning**: while sweeping the whole segment, the vehicle sweeps past the far perimeter point early enough to meet any intruder released at the far endpoint at the moment of its departure — this is the speed condition under which all switching-type algorithms work.
- **Extensibility**: the workhorse inequality for both Rand-Stay and Sleep-Switch; it is the algorithmic mirror of the deterministic lower-bound condition in [15, Th. III.2].

### 5.6 Equation (1): release-count parameter p (p. 4)

p := 2/Δ − 1 if 2/Δ ∈ Z⁺; ⌊2/Δ⌋ otherwise (eq. (1), p. 4). "Intuitively, p quantifies the number of times the adversary can release intruders within any time interval of duration strictly less than 2 units" (p. 4). Verified: releases spaced Δ apart inside a window of length < 2 number exactly 2/Δ − 1 when 2/Δ is integral, else ⌊2/Δ⌋ [my check]. Also (p+1)Δ ≥ 2 by construction (used on p. 5). p is the load-intensity parameter: p = 1 at Δ → 2⁻ and p → ∞ as Δ → 0⁺.

### 5.7 Theorem 4 (Rand-Stay: (1+2Np)/(3/4+Np)-competitiveness and tightness) (p. 4–5)

- **Algorithm** (Sec. IV-B, p. 4): at time 0 the vehicle is placed at endpoint +1 or −1 with probability 0.5 each (Ω = {−1, +1}); it waits there until the first intruder arrives (time t); then with probability 0.5 it stays until t+2 and with probability 0.5 it moves to the other endpoint (2 time units); at t+2 the rule repeats. No knowledge of Δ or N needed.
- **Statement**: for any instance P(ρ, v, N, Δ) with v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ), Algorithm Rand-Stay is (1+2Np)/(3/4+Np)-competitive, with p from eq. (1); furthermore it is no better than that (tight).
- **Proof approach** (p. 4–5): (i) define an epoch as the interval from the moment the vehicle starts waiting for an intruder until it either reaches the other endpoint or completes 2 units of waiting; (ii) Case 1 (vehicle committed to +1): with probability 0.25 (at +1 and decides to cross) it captures r_t at its endpoint, then l_t plus all Σ^{p}_{q=1} l_q released at −1 during [t+Δ, t+2) via Lemma 2; with probability 0.25 (at +1 and stays) it captures r_t plus Σ^{p}_{q=1} r_q; summing, Case 1 contributes, with probability 0.25 each, the counts r_t + l_t + Σl_q and r_t + Σr_q; (iii) Case 2 (committed to −1) symmetric, giving 2l_t + r_t + Σ(r_q+l_q) at probability 0.25 [as printed] and l_t + Σl_q; (iv) the expected per-epoch capture is eq. (2): (3/4)(r_t + l_t) + (1/2)Σ^{p}_{q=1}(r_q + l_q); (v) epoch-stationarity: the endpoint at epoch start and the stay/cross choice are independent 50/50 draws, so by resetting τ_k = 0 every epoch k ≥ 1 obeys the same equation (2); (vi) since the vehicle waits until an intruder arrives, 1 ≤ r_t + l_t ≤ 2N and r_q + l_q ≤ 2N; Definition 2 with the offline optimum capturing everything in the epoch yields eq. (3):
  c = [ (r_t + l_t) + Σ^{p}_{q=1}(r_q+l_q) ] / [ (3/4)(r_t+l_t) + (1/2)Σ^{p}_{q=1}(r_q+l_q) ] = (1+z)/(3/4 + 0.5z),  z := Σ^{p}_{q=1}(r_q+l_q)/(r_t+l_t);
  (vii) the objective (1+z)/(3/4+0.5z) is increasing in z (the paper says "taking the derivative of equation (3) with respect to z"; the derivative equals (1/4)/(3/4+0.5z)² > 0 [my computation]), so the worst case maximizes z: r_t + l_t = 1 (minimum load) and r_q + l_q = 2N for all release instants — printed as "∀1 ≤ q ≤ 2N" (p. 4), an apparent typo for ∀1 ≤ q ≤ p, since only p release instants exist in the window [my observation]; (viii) substituting gives c = (1+2Np)/(3/4+Np).
- **Tightness** (p. 5): input I releases a single intruder at +1 at time 0 and 2N intruders at +1 at times Δ, 2Δ, …, pΔ (nothing at −1); O stays at +1 and captures all 1+2Np; Rand-Stay's expected capture is (1+2Np)/2 + 1/4 = (3+4Np)/4 (equivalently eq. (2) with r_t+l_t = 1 and r_q+l_q = 2N, q ≤ p [my check]); the ratio equals (1+2Np)/(3/4+Np), so the bound is tight.
- **Decisive equations**: eq. (2) (per-epoch expectation) and eq. (3) (ratio in z), p. 4.
- **Local/global**: global over all inputs in the regime v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ); the ratio depends on the instance only through the product Np; analytic and exact; the regime boundary is exactly Lemma 2's condition.
- **Physical meaning**: expected captures mix a "guaranteed 3/4 of the triggering release" term with a "half of everything released while mid-epoch" term; the adversary hurts most by triggering an epoch with a lone intruder and then saturating the window with 2N-intruder bursts.
- **Extensibility**: the epoch-decomposition + ratio-monotonicity-in-z template applies to any wait-then-switch randomized policy with per-window release caps.
- **proof gap**: none material; only the quantifier typo in step (vii) above [my observation].

### 5.8 Theorem 5 (Sleep-Switch: (2p+2)/(p+2)-competitiveness, tight, N-independent) (p. 5, Algorithm 1)

- **Algorithm** (Algorithm 1, p. 5; requires knowledge of Δ, unlike Stay/Rand-Stay): (1) select endpoint ±1 with probability 0.5; (2) select s from S := {0, 1, …, p} with probability 1/(p+1); (3) wait until the first intruder arrives; (4) reset time to 0 and sleep for sΔ at the current endpoint; (5) move to the other endpoint (2 time units); (6) repeat from step 3 (with the same s). Sample space Ω = {−1,+1} × S.
- **Statement**: for any instance P(ρ, v, N, Δ) and p per eq. (1), Algorithm Sleep-Switch is (2p+2)/(p+2)-competitive if v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ); further, it is no better than (2p+2)/(p+2)-competitive.
- **Proof approach** (p. 5): (i) epoch k starts when the vehicle begins waiting (step 3) and ends when it reaches the other endpoint (step 5); for ease of exposition an intruder is assumed released at the epoch start τ_k (zero waiting time); (ii) let r_0…r_p (resp. l_0…l_p) be the counts at +1 (resp. −1) at instants τ_k, τ_k+Δ, …, τ_k+pΔ, and r_{p+1}…r_{2p+1} (resp. l) those at τ_k+(p+1)Δ, …, τ_k+(2p+1)Δ, noting (p+1)Δ ≥ 2; (iii) for a fixed sleep s, a vehicle at +1 captures Σ^s_{q=0} r_q while sleeping; crossing takes 2 units, so intruders l_s, …, l_{p+s} are released at −1 by the time it arrives, and by Lemma 2 it captures l_s + Σ^{p+s}_{q=s+1} l_q en route; symmetrically from −1; (iv) the late releases Σ^{2p+1}_{q=p+s+1}(l_q+r_q) are not lost by τ_k + sΔ + 2 and are capturable in epoch k+1; symmetrically, that many late releases of epoch k−1 are captured during epoch k — the authors credit each epoch with its own late releases, an accounting convention that keeps per-epoch counts stationary [as printed, p. 5]; (v) hence, for fixed s and endpoint choice (probability 0.5 each), the capture count is Σ^p_{q=0}(r_q+l_q) + l_s + r_s + Σ^{p+s}_{q=p+1}(l_q+r_q) + Σ^{2p+1}_{q=p+s+1}(l_q+r_q) = r_s + l_s + Σ^{2p+1}_{q=0}(r_q+l_q); (vi) averaging over s (uniform on p+1 values) and the endpoint (0.5) gives the unnumbered display
  E[captures per epoch] = Σ^{2p+1}_{q=0}(r_q+l_q)/(2(p+1)) + (p+1)Σ^{2p+1}_{q=0}(r_q+l_q)/(2(p+1)) = (p+2)Σ^{2p+1}_{q=0}(r_q+l_q)/(2(p+1)),
  where the first term is the expectation of r_s + l_s and the second of the total [my unpacking of the printed two-term display, p. 5]; (vii) assuming an offline optimum that captures all Σ^{2p+1}_{q=0}(r_q+l_q), Definition 2 gives c = 2(p+1)/(p+2) = (2p+2)/(p+2).
- **Tightness** (p. 5): input I releases 2N intruders at +1 at instants 0, Δ, …, (2p+1)Δ; an offline algorithm stationed at +1 captures all; "by following analogous steps as in the proof of Theorem 4 … the bound is tight" [as printed]. Verified consistency: Σ = 2N(2p+2), so E[captures] = (p+2)·2N(2p+2)/(2(p+1)) = 2N(p+2) and the ratio is exactly (2p+2)/(p+2) [my computation].
- **Decisive equation**: the two-term expectation display above (p. 5, unnumbered).
- **Local/global**: global over inputs in the regime v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ); the ratio is independent of N and depends only on p (hence only on Δ); analytic, exact.
- **Physical meaning**: randomizing the sleep duration desynchronizes the vehicle's switches from the adversary's release clock; the uniform distribution over S = {0,…,p} equalizes the adversary's best response over all phase offsets, yielding 2(p+1)/(p+2), which is 4/3 at p = 1 (sparse releases, Δ → 2⁻) and tends to 2 as Δ → 0⁺ (p → ∞, dense releases).
- **Extensibility**: the phase-randomization idea (uniform over one period of the release clock) is directly transferable to other clock-constrained online defense settings; requiring Δ as input is the price of the N-independence.
- **proof gap (minor)**: the tightness step does not print the expected-capture arithmetic (it defers to "analogous steps" from Theorem 4); the waiting-time-zero assumption at epoch starts is stated as an ease-of-exposition simplification and is benign because waiting only delays epochs [my observation].

### 5.9 Synthesis of the bounds (p. 5–6, Sec. V, Fig. 2)

- The regime v > (1−ρ)/ρ forces c(P) ≥ 2 (Theorem 1); the regime v ≥ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) with Δ < ε forces c(P) ≥ 4/3 (Theorem 2); below the curve v = (1−ρ)/(1+ρ), Rand-Stay and Sleep-Switch have finite ratios (Theorems 4–5); Stay is 2-competitive everywhere (Theorem 3) (p. 5–6 and Fig. 2 caption, p. 6).
- Randomization strictly beats the deterministic frontier: [15, Th. III.2] (deterministic, c ≥ 2 when v ≥ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ)) holds even restricted to ≤ 2N intruders Δ apart, yet Sleep-Switch achieves c ≈ 1.8 at v = 0.25, ρ = 0.6, Δ = 0.2 (where (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) = 0.25 exactly), and Stay achieves 2-competitiveness on the whole space, impossible deterministically (p. 6) [authors' claim]. For that instance p = 2/Δ − 1 = 9 since 2/Δ = 10 ∈ Z⁺, so c_Sleep-Switch = 20/11 ≈ 1.818 [my computation; the paper prints only c ≈ 1.8].
- Near-optimality: for low p (Sleep-Switch) and low Np (Rand-Stay) the ratios approach the 4/3 fundamental limit of Theorem 2 (p. 6) [authors' claim].

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated / model-inherent:**
- The adversary is restricted to the *oblivious* model; adaptive adversaries (which observe the algorithm's random outcomes) are explicitly excluded: "In this letter, we consider the oblivious adversary" (p. 2, Sec. II). Lower bounds under adaptive adversaries could be stronger and the algorithms' guarantees would need revisititing [my observation on scope].
- The 4/3 lower bound (Theorem 2) requires the release-separation condition Δ < ε; for coarser release clocks (Δ ≥ ε) in the same speed regime only the weaker conclusions apply, and no lower bound matching Theorems 4–5 is available there [my observation from the theorem statements, p. 3].
- The switching algorithms (Rand-Stay, Sleep-Switch) are proven only for v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) (Lemma 2's crossing condition); between (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) and (1−ρ)/ρ the only universal option is Stay at ratio 2, so the exact value of c(P) in that intermediate band (and even below the blue curve between 4/3 and the algorithm ratios) remains open [my observation from Fig. 2 and Theorem statements].
- Single vehicle, single line segment; "Future work will focus on extending these results to complex environments and with multiple vehicles" (p. 6, Sec. VI) [authors' claim].
- Capture is a point-coincidence event with unlimited capacity: if r_t intruders arrive simultaneously at the vehicle's endpoint, all are captured at once (used in all epoch computations, e.g., p. 4) — no sensor/actuation saturation is modeled [my observation].
- No simulations or hardware validation; all guarantees are worst-case ratios, whose absolute gap to practical performance is not assessed [my observation].

**Critical observations (with page/eq citations):**
- The vehicle's initial position is never specified in the problem formulation (p. 2, Sec. II) but the proofs of Theorems 1–2 assume the origin ("We assume that the vehicle starts at the origin", p. 3), and the tight instances are timed accordingly (first release at time ρ, p. 4). The formulation/proof interface should be read with the origin start as implicit [my observation].
- Theorem 1's proof contains the typographical phrase "the vehicle is located in (0, t] at time t" (p. 3), where the positive-side interval (0, ρ] is evidently intended; the position case analysis is compressed (see Section 5.2 above) [my observation].
- Theorem 4's worst-case characterization prints "r_t + l_t = 1 and r_q + l_q = 2N, ∀1 ≤ q ≤ 2N" (p. 4); the quantifier should read ∀1 ≤ q ≤ p, since the epoch's second window [t+Δ, t+2) contains exactly p release instants by eq. (1); the final ratio (1+2Np)/(3/4+Np) is consistent only with the q ≤ p reading [my observation].
- Lemma 1 cites Yao's minimax principle to [18] (a 2002 habilitation thesis) rather than to Yao's original work — functional but unusual attribution [my observation].
- Intruders are committed to "the nearest location in ∂R(ρ)" (p. 2): from ±1 the target is ±ρ, so no intruder maneuvering is modeled — this is an intruder-strategy-restricted game; the competitive-ratio framework partially compensates by letting the adversary choose release times/locations [my observation].
- The Sleep-Switch accounting credits each epoch with the late releases Σ^{2p+1}_{q=p+s+1}(l_q+r_q) that will only be captured in the next epoch ("we account for … in epoch k and not k+1", p. 5); this is a valid stationarity convention but means the per-epoch "captures" figure is an accounting identity rather than a literal within-epoch capture count [my observation].

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: P009 is the canonical reference for *randomization in worst-case perimeter defense*: it imports Yao's minimax principle and oblivious-adversary competitive analysis into the perimeter-defense/reach-avoid literature, proves the first algorithm-independent lower bounds (2 and 4/3) for randomized online defense on a line, and supplies three tight randomized algorithms (Stay, Rand-Stay, Sleep-Switch) whose ratios beat the deterministic frontier of the same authors' ACC 2021 paper [15]. Cite it (a) as the randomized-online counterpart to deterministic competitive perimeter defense [15]–[17] within the Bajaj–Torng–Bopardikar line of work, (b) whenever a paper needs a citation for lower bounds via Yao's principle in target defense, (c) for release-rate-constrained intruder models (the (ρ, v, N, Δ) parameterization and the p parameter of eq. (1)), and (d) as evidence that randomization strictly improves worst-case defense guarantees — a useful contrast to game-theoretic pure-strategy analyses.

**Ready-to-adapt citation sentences**:
- "Randomization has been shown to strictly improve worst-case perimeter defense: for a single vehicle guarding a segment of a line against intruders released at the endpoints, Bajaj et al. derived Yao-principle lower bounds of 2 and 4/3 on the competitive ratio of any randomized online algorithm and designed matching randomized policies whose ratios beat the deterministic frontier [P009]."
- "In the release-rate-constrained line-defense model P(ρ, v, N, Δ) of [P009], where at most 2N intruders may arrive at each event and consecutive events are Δ apart, the randomized Sleep-Switch policy achieves a tight competitive ratio (2p+2)/(p+2) — independent of the burst size N — whenever the intruder-to-defender speed ratio satisfies v ≤ (1−ρ)/(1+ρ)."
