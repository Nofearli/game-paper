## 2. Research Background

The paper situates itself in the multi-robot systems (MRS) literature on security and defense: a group of defenders must protect a target region from a group of intruders, a two-party conflict that "naturally fits into game-theoretic analyses" (p. 472). The *perimeter defense game* is defined as a variant of Isaacs' target-guarding problem [16] in which intruders try to reach the perimeter of a target region without being intercepted, while defenders — **constrained to move along the perimeter of a convex target region** [25–28] — try to intercept or capture them first (p. 472–473). The authors position the problem within three surrounding literatures: (i) classical 1-vs-1 pursuit-evasion differential games with equilibrium strategies [1,16]; (ii) multi-pursuer/single-evader and single-pursuer/multi-evader games, from either the pursuers' perspective [29,30,35] or the evaders' side [6,12,24]; and (iii) multiplayer reach-avoid / target-guarding variants, including the target-attacker-defender (TAD) game with missile-guidance relevance [9,17,21,22], the reach-avoid game [5,37,38] and its many variants [2,3,14,34–36], coastline/border defense [7,10,13,31], and 3-D environments [8,33] (p. 473). Voronoi-tessellation-based decomposition and assignment [15,18,20,39] is cited as the standard device for reducing large-scale pursuit problems, with the key obstacle being the dimensionality of the joint state space that "prohibits us from naively applying the differential-game techniques" (p. 473). The perimeter constraint on the defenders is the distinguishing feature that yields convenient closed-form solutions with interesting geometric interpretations, and matches applications such as ground vehicles defending a building or aerial vehicles patrolling a no-fly zone (p. 473).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Nature of the paper.** This is a self-contained review — "an overview of various results and developments presented in our previous publications [25–28]" (p. 474) — organized as: Sect. 2 problem statement; Sect. 3 solution method (agent-level + team-level); Sect. 4 extensions/generalizations; Sect. 5 limitations and future directions; Sect. 6 conclusion.

**Recorded taxonomy [authors' own organization, verified from the text]:**

- **Decision-making levels (two layers).** (1) *Agent-level control policy* (Sect. 3.1): low-level velocity-control strategies from small-scale differential games (one-vs-one, two-vs-one), obtained as solutions of the game of kind (barrier surface + winning-region control strategies). (2) *Team-level coordination policy* (Sect. 3.2): high-level assignment/teaming mappings Γ_D, Γ_A from joint states to control vectors ω_D = [ω_{D1},…,ω_{D_{N_D}}], v_A = [v_{A1},…,v_{A_{N_A}}], with the integer score Q as payoff. The unifying architecture: "The low-level velocity control strategies obtained in this small-scale game are combined with the high-level assignment policies to design the team-level strategies" (p. 474).
- **Team-assignment levels (three methods, increasing cooperation).** *Man-to-man defense* via maximum-cardinality matching (MM) on a defender–intruder bipartite graph (1 defender : 1 intruder, no overlap on either side); *Two-on-one defense* via maximum independent set (MIS) on a conflict graph whose nodes are assignments (defender pairs : single intruder, plus single assignments); *Local Game Decomposition* via Local Game Regions (LGRs) — region-based subteaming in which the intruder team selects disjoint LGRs to attack. Cooperation level ordering [authors' claim, p. 483]: "the local-game decomposition generates the most sophisticated team behavior, the maximum matching has the most straightforward ways of being applied to other problems."
- **Single-team vs two-team.** A key recorded asymmetry [authors' claim, p. 481]: "the assignment-based approaches do not work well for the intruder team. More specifically, each intruder cannot simply select a single defender and play a one vs. one game against it. Instead, an intruder has to avoid all defenders in order to reach the target and score a point." Hence MM/MIS produce only defender-team strategies (upper bounds on Q*), while the LGR decomposition is the first device producing a genuine *intruder-team* strategy (lower bound on Q*).

**Trends identified [authors' claims]:** (a) preference for closed-form geometric barrier solutions (valid for all ν ∈ (0,1], with special involute-based geometry at ν = 1) over numerical HJI-PDE computation, which requires pre-computing and storing barriers for every speed ratio ν as a look-up table (p. 481); (b) reduction of continuous swarm-vs-swarm games to combinatorial optimization (matching / independent set / set-selection) using only *pairwise* capturability information; (c) layering of local game results (1v1, 2v1) into team strategies, with the 2v1 barrier enabling analysis of games of arbitrary size n_D vs n_A (p. 477).

**Open problems** (full list in Sect. 7 below): defender dynamics off the perimeter, sequential capture, fast intruders, partial information.

**Assessment [verified from cited-text descriptions within the paper]:** The MM framework originates in Chen, Zhou & Tomlin's multiplayer reach-avoid work [3,4] ("This general approach was originally proposed in [3,4], and it has been applied to other variants of the problems [25,36]", p. 478); the circular-perimeter barrier was first derived geometrically in [25] and "later verified with differential game approach in [32]" (Von Moll, Pachter, Shishika & Fuchs, CDC 2020); the arbitrary-convex-shape barrier and the approach-angle parameterization are from [26]; the LGR decomposition, Theorem 1, and the equilibrium-sandwich result are from [28] (Shishika, Paulos & Kumar, IEEE RA-L 5(2):2738–2745, 2020). The extension items cite [33] (Yan et al., 3-D maximum-matching capture strategies), [23] (Lee, Shishika & Kumar, aerial defender vs ground intruder, CDC 2020), [27] (patroller/defender team composition), and [19] (learned communication for decentralized MM).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Unified two-level solution architecture for multi-agent perimeter defense** (Sect. 3): a recipe — solve small-scale games of kind (1v1, 2v1) for barriers and winning regions, then lift to team-level coordination via combinatorial optimization — presented as the common structure behind the reviewed results.
2. **Comparative taxonomy of three team-level analysis approaches** — MM (maximum matching), MIS (maximum independent set), LGR (local game regions) — with explicit relative-strength/weakness assessment (tightness vs. computational complexity vs. generality of applicability) and the bound chain Q_MIS ≤ Q_MM and the intruder lower bound Q_LG.
3. **Catalog of closed-form agent-level results**: circular barrier ([25], verified [32]), sign-based equilibrium defender strategy, approach-angle solution φ = cos⁻¹ν and involute-based barrier construction for arbitrary convex perimeters ([26]), and the 2v1 pincer-movement barrier whose intruder-winning regions scale to arbitrary n_D-vs-n_A games ([25,26,28,32]).
4. **Extension program (Sect. 4)**: a systematic map of how the framework generalizes — 3-D/hemisphere guarding, heterogeneous speeds, weighted intruders, generalized n-defender-vs-m-intruder subproblems (maximum-weight independent set), and cooperative intruder strategies via local numerical advantage.
5. **Identified limitations and future directions (Sect. 5)**: four specific open directions (defender dynamics, sequential capture, fast intruders, partial information) that seed subsequent research; the abstract states "As a unique contribution of this paper, we discuss how the existing results can be extended into more general problem formulations" (p. 472).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

All items below verified against the rendered PDF pages; equation numbers and page numbers as printed.

- **Item 1 — Problem statement (Sect. 2, p. 474).** Planar dynamics of point particles: N_A intruders (A) and N_D defenders (D), all with *simple dynamics* (first-order integrator; velocity = control). Speed bounds: intruders ν and defenders 1, with constraint **ν ≤ 1** ("the intruders are at most as fast as the defenders"). Assumptions: **(A1)** defenders move along the perimeter of the target region; **(A2)** the target region is convex; **(A3)** each agent has full-state information (positions of all agents); **(A4)** intercept/capture occurs when the intruder–defender distance becomes zero. Outcome categories per intruder: (i) reaches perimeter un-intercepted → scores; (ii) distance to a defender is zero upon reaching the perimeter; (iii) never reaches the perimeter in finite time (defender places itself between target and intruder). Both (ii) and (iii) are defined as *capture*. Team payoff **Q = number of intruders that score**; intruder team maximizes, defender team minimizes; the problem is to find equilibrium strategies giving min_{Γ_D} max_{Γ_A} Q = max_{Γ_A} min_{Γ_D} Q. [Note: the min–max equality is asserted for the team game without the "if an equilibrium exists" caveat at this point; the caveat appears later for Eq. (2).]

- **Item 2 — 1v1 game of degree and barrier definition (Sect. 3.1, Eq. (1), p. 475).** Payoff J = distance between defender and intruder at the time of breaching (intruder maximizes, defender minimizes). Value function:
  V = min_{ω_D} max_{v_A} J = max_{v_A} min_{ω_D} J,  (1)
  where ω_D is the defender's control on a one-dimensional space (along the perimeter) and v_A the intruder's velocity. "Once the equilibrium strategies ω*_D and v*_A are derived, the Value V is a function of the player positions, and the barrier surface can be identified as the level set V = 0." The barrier divides the state space into the intruder-winning region (intruder has a strategy to score) and the defender-winning region (defender guarantees capture). Game-of-kind-first methodology.

- **Item 3 — Circular-perimeter barrier (Fig. 2(a), p. 475; from [25], verified by [32]).** "The barrier for a circular perimeter was first derived in [25] using geometric approach, and later verified with differential game approach in [32]." Equilibrium defender strategy: move in the direction of the intruder; with polar angle θ of the intruder relative to the defender's front,
  ω*_D = sign(θ).
  Intruder's equilibrium strategy: move towards the tangent point on a circle scaled by the speed ratio ν [25,32] (Fig. 2(a) shows the target circle, the defender D on it, the ν-scaled concentric circle, and the tangent construction). The review notes this strategy is "almost trivial" for the circular case since θ = 0 defines the defender's front.

- **Item 4 — Non-circular (arbitrary convex) perimeter: approach angle (Fig. 2(b), p. 476; from [26]).** For a non-circular perimeter (e.g., polygon) the left/right dividing surface is not obvious and the intruder strategy can no longer be parameterized by the polar angle. [26] parameterizes intruder strategies by the **approach angle** φ — the angle between the intruder's direction of motion and the tangent vector of the curve at the breach point. Optimal approach angle:
  φ = cos⁻¹ ν.
  Interpretation [authors']: the intruder should approach the tangent point on the target when ν = 1, and the closest point on the target when ν → 0; matches the circular special case.

- **Item 5 — Barrier for arbitrary convex shape (Fig. 3(a), p. 476; from [26]).** The barrier for a fixed defender position is "a closed curve that completely surrounds the target region" (a slice of the state space); the region enclosed by the barrier is the intruder-winning region, the outside the defender-winning region. For the special case **ν = 1** the barrier is constructed from pieces of geometry called the **involute** — "the locus of a point on a piece of taut string as the string is either unwrapped from or wrapped around the shape" — of the target region; the intruder's equilibrium strategy is to move towards the tangent of the target region for both circular and non-circular perimeters. Results accommodate any ν ∈ (0,1].

- **Item 6 — 2v1 game and the pincer movement (Sect. 3.1 "Two vs. One", p. 477; Fig. 3(b); from [25,26,32]).** With two defenders, the safest breach point becomes "either the optimal breach point against the closer defender or the mid-point between the two defenders." The defender pair's optimal strategy: approach the intruder from both sides — the **pincer movement**. Key structural result [authors' claim, citing [25,28,32]]: "the defender-winning region provided by the pincer movement is larger than the union of the winning regions provided by the individual defenders." Beyond assignment, the 2v1 intruder-winning regions "give us a way to directly analyze an arbitrary size of the game played between n_D defenders and n_A intruders" (p. 477) — the scaling device for the whole framework.

- **Item 7 — Team-level game (Sect. 3.2, Eq. (2), p. 477).** With N_D defenders and N_A intruders, team strategies Γ_D, Γ_A map joint positions to ω_D = [ω_{D1},…,ω_{D_{N_D}}], v_A = [v_{A1},…,v_{A_{N_A}}]; payoff Q = number of intruders that reach the perimeter without capture. "If an equilibrium exists, the Value function is
  Q* = min_{Γ_D} max_{Γ_A} Q = max_{Γ_A} min_{Γ_D} Q,  (2)
  The goal is to find the equilibrium team strategies (Γ*_D, Γ*_A) and the Value Q*." Since directly solving this large-scale game is "very challenging", three approximation methods follow.

- **Item 8 — Man-to-man defense via maximum matching (Eq. (3), p. 478).** Bipartite graph: defenders on one side, intruders on the other; an edge defender→intruder is drawn whenever the intruder lies in that defender's (1v1) defender-winning region. Constraints: no defender assigned to multiple intruders (capture guaranteed against only one) and no intruder assigned multiple defenders (redundant assignments reduce total captures, raising the score). Maximum-cardinality matching (MM) gives the optimal man-to-man defense and an **upper bound** on the score:
  Q* ≤ Q_MM = N_A − N_MM,  (3)
  where N_MM is the number of matches. Origin [verified from cited text]: "This general approach was originally proposed in [3,4]" (Chen, Zhou & Tomlin, ACC 2014 and IEEE TAC 62(3):1451–1457, 2017 — the pairwise-outcomes/matching framework), "and it has been applied to other variants of the problems [25,36]" ([36] = Yan, Shi & Zhong, IEEE T-RO 36(1):107–124, 2019, task assignment for multiplayer reach-avoid in convex domains via analytical barriers). Downside: "this naive coordination strategy does not account for a tighter cooperation that can happen between the defenders or the intruders" (p. 478).

- **Item 9 — Two-on-one defense via maximum independent set (Eq. (4), p. 478).** To incorporate 2v1 cooperation, one is tempted to augment the bipartite graph with defender-pair nodes, "however, we cannot perform maximum-cardinality matching on this bipartite graph since a node representing a single defender and one that represents a pair may share the same defender, which leads to an overlapping assignment." Remedy: construct a **conflict graph** whose nodes are assignments of the original graph and whose edges connect assignments sharing any defender or intruder; solving Maximum Independent Set (MIS) on it yields a non-overlapping assignment set with a **tighter upper bound**:
  Q* ≤ Q_MIS = N_A − N_MIS ≤ Q_MM,  (4)
  where N_MIS is the cardinality of the maximum independent set. Downside: "Since MIS problem is NP-hard, it does not scale well with large number of agents" (p. 478).

- **Item 10 — Local Game Regions (LGR) decomposition (Fig. 3(b)/Fig. 4, Eqs. (5)–(6), p. 478–479; from [28]).** For a defender pair, the associated intruder-winning region is the **Local Game Region (LGR)**, "as it leads to a region-based decomposition method [28]." Two consequences of the 2v1 result: the n_A^k intruders inside LGR k can win against the defender pair by approaching near the mid-point; and any defender *outside* the LGR cannot capture any of those intruders, so only the n_D^k defenders inside can contribute to capture. With **local numerical advantage q_k = n_A^k − n_D^k > 0**, the intruders achieve score q_k (k indexes defender pairs / LGRs). The intruder team maximizes its guaranteed score by selecting the set G of *disjoint* LGRs in which to play the local game:
  Q_LG = max_G Σ_{k∈G} q'_k,  where q'_k = max{0, q_k}.  (5)
  Fig. 4 (verbatim caption): "Local game regions (LGRs) and the associated subteams. (a) An LGR that contains three intruders and two defenders. (b) The intruder team's optimal selection of the LGRs, G*, leading to the overall score of Q = 3." Panel (a) annotates n_A^k = 3, n_D^k = 2, q_k = 1; panel (b) annotates q_i = 2, q_j = 1 and Q_LG = 3 [verified from rendered page].

- **Item 11 — Theorem 1 (p. 479; from [28]).** Verbatim: "**Theorem 1 (From [28]).** Let Γ*_A denote the intruder strategy corresponding to the teaming into G* and then playing the two vs. one game against the defender pair associated to each LGR. The strategy Γ*_A guarantees
  Q* ≥ Q_LG  (6)
  for all permissible defender strategies." The review stresses: "this is the first result in the team-level coordination policy that gives an intruder team strategy and a corresponding lower bound on the score" (p. 479) — i.e., the only two-sided (upper + lower bound) machinery among MM/MIS/LGR.

- **Item 12 — Defender team strategy Γ*_D and equilibrium sandwich (Eq. (7), p. 479–480; from [28]).** The LGR-based defender strategy: (1) "removes any uncapturable intruders based on the result of Theorem 6" [as printed — the reference is to a theorem of [28]; the review itself contains only Theorem 1]; (2) assigns two-on-one defense to intruders near the boundary of LGRs with score q_k = 0, "to ensure that no additional intruder enters the LGRs that already has an equal number of intruders and defenders, which we call the occupied LGRs [28]"; (3) assigns one-on-one defense to the remaining agents. Unlike MM/MIS, this strategy "accounts for the sequence of captures that occur in time" (p. 479–480). For a class of initial configurations (characterized in [28]):
  Q(Γ*_D, Γ_A) ≤ Q(Γ*_D, Γ*_A) ≤ Q(Γ_D, Γ*_A),  (7)
  "which implies that the strategies are at an equilibrium." This is the only place in the reviewed framework where a (saddle-point) equilibrium relation between explicit team strategies is claimed.

- **Item 13 — Extensions catalogue (Sect. 4, p. 480–481).** (a) **3-D engagements**: free-moving agents with a planar target [33] (Yan et al., maximum-matching capture strategies for 3-D heterogeneous multiplayer reach-avoid); direct extension = **hemisphere guarding problem** (defenders on a hemisphere vs aerial intruders), with the 1v1 aerial-defender-vs-ground-intruder game of [23] (Lee, Shishika & Kumar, CDC 2020) as the intermediate step — once the barrier surface is known, pairwise capturability converts continuous velocity design into a matching problem. (b) **Heterogeneous speed**: defenders with different speed limits → different barrier per defender, but "this variation does not affect the assignment method, since all we need in the high-level matching algorithm is the pair-wise information of whether a given defender can capture a given intruder or not"; same for heterogeneous intruder speeds. The closed-form 1v1 solution matters here: with the HJI-PDE numerical approach (as in [3,4]) "the barrier surfaces for all possible values of ν have to be computed a priori and be stored to be used as a look-up table" (p. 481). (c) **Intruder weights**: per-intruder damage weights turn maximum-cardinality matching into a **Linear Sum Assignment Problem** solvable by the Hungarian Algorithm. (d) **Generalized MIS**: for any solved n-defender-vs-m-intruder subproblem, nodes = team assignments carrying weights (number of intruders or weighted threat sum), edges = conflicts (shared defender/intruder); **Maximum-weight Independent Set** optimizes cooperative assignments. (e) **Cooperative intruder strategies**: the local-numerical-advantage analysis (intruder subteams simultaneously attacking a single perimeter point) "will likely appear in the multi-player reach-avoid game if it is assumed that the defender is consumed by the intruder whenever a capture occurs"; the generalization challenge is defining regions with two properties [as printed, p. 481]: "(a) an associated intruder subteam that, and (b) a set of defenders that every intruder in the subteam can win against" — the complement of the defender subteam, yielding the sufficient scoring condition **n_A^k > n_D^k**.

- **Item 14 — Scenario figure (Fig. 1, p. 473).** Verbatim caption: "Illustration of a perimeter defense game. Multiple intruders are approaching the target, while the defenders are tasked to intercept them." (No formal taxonomy diagram exists in the paper — the MM/MIS/LGR taxonomy is textual; Fig. 1 is the only scenario illustration.)

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated limitations and open problems (Sect. 5, pp. 481–482 — listed in full; these seed the skill's research-gap knowledge):**

1. **Defender dynamics (A1: defenders confined to the perimeter).** "The most significant consequence of this constraint is the fact that the intruders can ensure no defender captures more than one intruder, by approaching the perimeter simultaneously. This property makes the perimeter defense game to be interesting only when N_A < N_D, since the intruder team can always guarantee a score otherwise." (p. 482; quoted verbatim — the printed inequality is N_A < N_D.) In other words the one-capture-per-defender ceiling is structural, and the game is non-degenerate only when defenders outnumber intruders.
2. **Sequential capture.** If defenders may leave the perimeter and pursue, a single defender can achieve multiple captures, which opens: (a) defender team strategy now embeds a **vehicle routing problem** — "a complex combinatorial optimization problem even in the case where the locations to be visited by the agents are stationary"; (b) intruder behavior gains a meaningful distinction between **evasive and offensive maneuvers** — "some of the intruders may try to lure the defenders away from the perimeter so that other intruders can successfully reach the target," with small-scale cooperation schemes already studied in [29]; (c) the defender design faces "a potential conflict between (i) pursuing an intruder, and (ii) staying close to the target for subsequent captures." Coordination for large teams is then interesting from both sides.
3. **Fast intruders (ν > 1).** Existing results hold only for ν ∈ (0,1]; the assumption is necessary for the 1v1 game because "if the intruder has a speed advantage, then it can come arbitrarily close to the perimeter and outrun the defender to always guarantee intrusion," and this persists even with multiple defenders. "The degeneracy arises due to the combination of assumptions (A1) and (A4)" (point capture + perimeter confinement). Remedy: relax either or both (e.g., **nonzero capture radius**) and design coordinated defender motion.
4. **Partial information.** "One of the strongest assumptions in the existing variants of the target guarding problems is the assumption that each agent knows the location of all other agents." Steps toward a holistic solution: [27] designs a **patroller team** ensuring detection of any agent approaching a certain distance from the perimeter; [19] learns communication strategies from a centralized expert policy and yields a decentralized version of the MM assignment. "Importantly, neither of the above works directly addressed the issue of unseen intruders." Partial information "will make any pursuit-evasion game much more difficult to solve. When an equilibrium is infeasible to obtain, a design of reasonable team strategies will be of great importance to the field."
5. **Conclusion-level summary (p. 483):** "There are several limitations in the current problem formulation that lead us to future developments in the aspects related to the agent dynamics, payoff function, and the information structure." (Payoff-function generalizations correspond to the weighted-intruder extension of Sect. 4.1.)

**Critical observations [this archive's verification, with page/section citations]:**

- **Scope: a self-review.** The core results (Items 2–6, 10–12) are exclusively from the authors' own chain [25]–[28] plus their collaborator Von Moll et al. [32]; external frameworks ([3,4,36] etc.) are cited as context/origins (p. 474, 478). The paper's abstract frames "three different approaches ... (MM, MIS, and LGR)" as the field's landscape, but the taxonomy is effectively the authors' own research program — a citation-context caveat when using it as a field-wide survey.
- **Duplicate reference.** Refs. [12] and [13] are the same paper (Garcia, Von Moll, Casbeer, Pachter, "Strategies for defending a coastline against multiple attackers," CDC 2019, pp. 7319–7324) printed twice [verified from the reference list, p. 484]; in-text citations [12] and [13] (p. 473, coastline/border defense) therefore both resolve to one work.
- **Dangling theorem pointer.** p. 479 cites "the result of Theorem 6" for removing uncapturable intruders — the review contains only Theorem 1, so this must refer to a theorem of [28] [as printed; not resolvable within this PDF].
- **Existence caveat.** Eq. (2)'s min–max equality is conditioned on "If an equilibrium exists" (p. 477), and Eq. (7)'s sandwich holds only "for the class of initial configurations" discussed in [28] (p. 480); the integer payoff Q and the layered (approximate) construction mean a global Value for the N_A-vs-N_D game is generally not established by this review — MM/MIS/LGR certify one-sided bounds only, except in the [28] equilibrium class.
- **Typographical noise** [as printed]: "planer dynamics" and "bounds on there speed" (p. 474), "anoteher" (p. 477), incomplete sentence "(a) an associated intruder subteam that, and (b) …" (p. 481), arXiv id printed as "arXiv arXiv:190903989" for [26] and "Von Moll1" for [32] (p. 484–485).
- **No taxonomy diagram; no experiments** (see Sect. 6): the paper's comparative claims (cooperation level ordering, generality of MM) are qualitative judgments, not measured.
- **The N_A < N_D "interesting regime" claim (p. 482)** is printed as such; the mechanism given (simultaneous approach caps captures at one per defender) actually guarantees the intruders a score of N_A − N_D when N_A > N_D, so the claim is coherent as "only defender-superiority regimes are non-trivial" — but readers citing it should quote the printed inequality carefully.
- **Intruder-side asymmetry is identified but only partially solved**: the LGR construction presumes the 2v1 barrier geometry (defender pairs), and the review itself flags (p. 481) that defining the right regions for reach-avoid or other target-guarding variants "is the main challenge" — this is the sharpest seed for follow-up research in this knowledge base.

## 8. Citation Value

- **Likely role in a future IEEE Trans introduction**: the canonical related-work anchor for the *multi-agent perimeter defense / target guarding with perimeter-confined defenders* line. Use it (i) to introduce the perimeter-defense game and its two-level (local differential game + combinatorial teaming) solution paradigm, (ii) to justify the MM/bipartite-assignment and barrier-based pairwise-capturability machinery by citation, (iii) to motivate open problems — faster intruders, capture radius, defenders leaving the perimeter (sequential capture / VRP), and partial information — as gaps. It also anchors the sub-line: Shishika & Kumar CDC 2018 [25], arXiv 2019 [26], CDC 2019 [27], RA-L 2020 [28], Von Moll et al. CDC 2020 [32], Lee et al. CDC 2020 [23].
- **Ready-to-adapt English citation sentence 1** (related work / problem positioning): "Perimeter defense games, in which perimeter-confined defenders protect a convex target region from a team of intruders, admit closed-form barrier surfaces for the one-vs-one and two-vs-one engagements, which can be lifted to team-level strategies through maximum matching, maximum independent set, or local game region decomposition [Shishika & Kumar, GameSec 2020]."
