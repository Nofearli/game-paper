## 2. Research Background
The review concerns multiplayer reach-avoid (M-RA) differential games: two adversarial teams, N_p pursuers (defenders) and N_e evaders (attackers), play in a game region Ω ⊂ R^n (n ≥ 2) split by a hypersurface T into a play region Ω_play and a goal region Ω_goal (p. 2, Sec. 2.1, Fig. 1). Evaders start in the play region and strive to enter the goal region without being captured; pursuers cooperatively capture them to protect the goal region. Compared with classical pursuit-evasion where capture is the only termination, reach-avoid is harder and more practical because the evader must simultaneously reach a target set and avoid capture [authors' claim, p. 2]. All players are point masses with Isaacs' simple motion — first-order integrators with bounded speed and instantaneous heading change — a suitable abstraction for highly maneuverable, speed-limited vehicles (humanoids, quadrotors, small underground vehicles) [authors' claim, pp. 1–2]. The paper is positioned as a selective overview of algorithms and theoretical results for this class, motivated by building/infrastructure defense, UAV-vs-poacher wildlife protection, boat patrolling of ferries/prohibited waters, and escape/path-planning through exits (p. 3, Sec. 2.2). It is closely related to lifeline games (Garcia et al. 2019b; Yan et al. 2021a,b; Chen & Yu 2022), two-target games (Blaquière et al. 1969; Olsder & Breakwell 1974; Pachter & Getz 1980; Getz & Pachter 1981), and target-guarding games (Mohanan et al. 2018) (p. 2).

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
As a review, P104 does not solve one game; it organizes the field. **Taxonomy (authors' organization, verified against the section structure):**
- **Problem classes**: (i) game of kind — decide which team wins from an initial state (win/lose); (ii) game of degree — given a payoff, synthesize saddle-point equilibrium strategies that ensure winning and optimize a metric (p. 3, Sec. 2.3). Team-level payoff: number J of evaders captured in the play region, with saddle-point value max_{Σ_P} min_{Σ_E} J = min_{Σ_E} max_{Σ_P} J (eq. (2), p. 3). Per-player modes: a pursuer has no task / fulfills it alone / forms a coalition; an evader is captured in the play region / captured exactly on the splitting hypersurface / reaches the goal region (p. 3).
- **Problem formulations**: simple-motion dynamics (1) under Assumptions 1–4 (faster-or-equal pursuers, full state information, radius/point capture, constant pursuer count); terminal set M = {x | g(x) ≤ 0} and terminal payoff J = Φ(x(t_f)) (eq. (3), p. 4); barriers and winning regions W_P, W_E, B (Sec. 2.3.1); four strategy structures — open loop, state feedback, non-anticipative, anticipative — following Mitchell et al. (2005) (Sec. 2.3.2).
- **Solution approaches** (the review's two-method taxonomy, Sec. 3): (1) the **geometric method**, valid when optimal trajectories are straight lines under simple motion, built on dominance regions — Voronoi cell (Def. 1), Apollonius circle (Def. 2), function-based dominance region (Def. 3); (2) the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs (HJI) method**, "more general and is able to handle with more complicated player dynamics. However, it also suffers from high computational complexity" [authors' claim, p. 3, citing Mitchell et al. 2005; Margellos & Lygeros 2011; Chen et al. 2018; Fisac et al. 2015]. The workflow it advocates: qualitative analysis of small-scale (many-vs-one) games via barriers → optimal task allocation across pairs → cooperative feedback strategies (Secs. 4–6). [This decomposition claim is the authors'; its ingredients are verified in the cited sources, e.g., Yan et al. 2020 (IEEE T-Robotics) and Yan et al. 2022 (Automatica).]
- **Challenges identified** (p. 2, Sec. 1): (a) non-unique terminal conditions (capture OR entry), which complicate backward integration from differential terminal surfaces because of complicated singular surfaces — "This results from a lack of systematic analysis methods" [authors' claim]; (b) complex intra-team cooperation and inter-team competition, e.g., two pursuers needed jointly to catch one evader; evaders that lure pursuers away or sacrifice themselves, "like prey animals" (p. 2).
- **Trends identified**: barrier construction for many-vs-one games in 2D/3D, with heterogeneous speeds/capture radii, lifetime limits, evasion-team cooperation, and orbit-restricted pursuers (Sec. 4); exact game-theoretic assignment via constrained matching plus polynomial approximation (Sec. 5); dominance-region-based cooperative feedback strategies keyed to speed ratio and capture radius (Sec. 6); HJI used both as a solver (active target defense, turret/perimeter defense) and as a verification tool for candidate value functions (Sec. 3.2).
- **Open problems**: five axes — player dynamics, sequential capture, spatial-temporal coupling, fast evaders, partial information (Sec. 7, inspired by Shishika & Kumar 2020) — all listed in §7 below.

No pre-existing classification tables exist in prior literature and the review provides none either (no tables at all; taxonomy is narrative + 6 schematic figures) — a gap the review itself leaves open [verified: the PDF contains Figures 1–6 only].

## 4. Core Contributions
1. **Taxonomy/survey**: a structured, selective review of M-RA differential games with simple motions, organized as game elements (Sec. 2) → two solution methods (Sec. 3) → barrier construction for five representative many-vs-one games (Sec. 4) → task allocation for many-vs-many (Sec. 5) → three cooperative strategy families (Sec. 6) → open problems (Sec. 7).
2. **Unified formulation**: a common model (dynamics (1), Assumptions 1–4, payoff (2), terminal data (3)) under which Voronoi, Apollonius, δ-Apollonius and function-based dominance regions, HJI equations, barriers, ILP assignment and convex-optimization strategies are presented in one notation (n-dimensional regions, speed ratios α_ij = v_Pi/v_Ej, capture radii r_i).
3. **Assessment of methods**: geometric method = exact and cheap when optimal trajectories are straight lines (validated a posteriori by the ELODE analysis: regular equilibrium trajectories are straight lines, controls constant, value constant — Sec. 3.2); HJI = general but computationally heavy; HJI also packaged as a three-condition verification certificate for candidate value functions (Sec. 3.2, items 1–3).
4. **Algorithmic synthesis (of the authors' own line, faithfully recapped)**: 0–1 ILP for maximum-capture matching between pursuit coalitions and evaders (eq. (18)) with NP-hardness (special constrained matching problem, Tanimoto et al. 1978; Yan et al. 2022) and a 1/N_c-approximation "Sequential Matching" polynomial algorithm (Sec. 5.2); convex-optimization pursuit strategy valid for positive capture radii (eq. (19), Sec. 6.3).
5. **Open-problem list**: five concrete future-research axes (Sec. 7) that seed research-gap analysis for the field.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive catalog)
The review states no numbered theorems of its own; its mathematical content is 19 numbered equations, 3 definitions, 4 assumptions, and 6 figures cataloguing results from cited papers (identities given below). Everything was verified against 200–400 dpi renders of pp. 2–9.

**5.0 Game model (Sec. 2.1, eq. (1), p. 2).** ẋ_Pi = v_Pi u_Pi, x_Pi(0) = x⁰_Pi (Pi ∈ P); ẋ_Ej = v_Ej u_Ej, x_Ej(0) = x⁰_Ej (Ej ∈ E); controls are instantaneous headings u ∈ U = {u ∈ R^n | ‖u‖₂ ≤ 1}; v_Pi, v_Ej ∈ R>0. Assumptions: **A1** v_Pi ≥ v_Ej (pursuers at least as fast); **A2** full-state information (all positions and speeds known to every player); **A3** capture when dist(x_Pi, x_Ej) ≤ r_i ≥ 0 — radius capture if r_i > 0, point capture if r_i = 0; **A4** pursuer count constant; pursuers chase until no evader remains in the play region. Payoff (2): J = number of evaders captured in the play region; saddle point max_{Σ_P} min_{Σ_E} J = min_{Σ_E} max_{Σ_P} J.

**5.1 Winning regions and barrier (Sec. 2.3.1, p. 3).** For the pursuit team P against one evader E_j (state x = (x_P1,…,x_Np, x_Ej)): W_P = {x | ∃u ∈ Σ_P, ∀u_Ej ∈ U, s.t. P wins against E_j from x}; W_E = {x | ∃u_Ej ∈ U, ∀u ∈ Σ_P, s.t. E_j wins against P from x}; barrier B = {x | ∃u ∈ Σ_P, ∃u_Ej ∈ U, s.t. P and E_j cannot win from x}. B partitions the state space into PWR and EWR; winning regions follow from barriers "directly" [authors' claim], so the review deliberately omits them (p. 5, Sec. 4 preamble).

**5.2 Four strategy types (Sec. 2.3.2, p. 3, after Mitchell et al. 2005).** Open loop (entire u(τ), τ ∈ [t, ∞), no knowledge of others' decisions); state feedback (u(τ) from current state); non-anticipative (state feedback + others' current input, declared before others choose); anticipative (knowledge of all future inputs of others). The review notes non-anticipative structure leaves opponents "at a slight disadvantage… at a minimum they have access to using state feedback" [authors' paraphrase of Mitchell et al. 2005].

**5.3 Dominance regions — geometric method (Sec. 3.1, Defs. 1–3, Fig. 2, p. 4).**
- **Def. 1 (Voronoi cell)**: partition V(Ω) = {R¹_p,…,R^Np_p, R¹_e,…,R^Ne_e} with R^p_i = {x ∈ Ω | ‖x − x_Pi‖₂ ≤ min{‖x − x_Pi'‖₂, ‖x − x_Ej'‖₂}, ∀i' ≠ i, 1 ≤ j' ≤ N_e} (and symmetrically R^e_j). If v_Pi = v_Ej and r_i = 0 ∀i, j, the Voronoi cell of P_i (E_j) is its dominance region. Covers equal-speed, point-capture games; simplest dominance tool.
- **Def. 2 (Apollonius circle)**: if v_Pi > v_Ej and r_i = 0, R^a_ij has center (α²_ij x_Ej − x_Pi)/(α²_ij − 1) and radius α_ij‖x_Pi − x_Ej‖₂/(α²_ij − 1), where α_ij = v_Pi/v_Ej. The evader's dominance region against P is ∩_{i=1}^{N_p} R^a_ij. Covers strict speed superiority, point capture; the intersection form underlies point-based pursuit (Sec. 6.2).
- **Def. 3 (Function-based dominance region; attributed to Yan et al. 2022, Automatica)**: f_ij: R² → R, f_ij(x) = ‖x − x_Pi‖₂ − α_ij‖x − x_Ej‖₂ − r_i; if v_Pi ≥ v_Ej and r_i ≥ 0, then {x ∈ Ω | f_ij(x) ≥ 0, ∀1 ≤ i ≤ N_p} is E_j's dominance region. Handles BOTH equal-or-better pursuers and positive capture radii — the other two cannot [review's implicit assessment]; this convex level-set representation enables the convex-optimization strategy (19) and the 3D barrier (Sec. 4.2).
- Fig. 2 (p. 4) depicts all three for multiple pursuers vs one evader (crosses = Apollonius centers).

**5.4 HJI method (Sec. 3.2, eqs. (3)–(7), pp. 4–5).** State x = (x_P1,…,x_PNp, x_E1,…,x_ENe) ∈ R^{n(N_p+N_e)}; terminal set M = {x | g(x) ≤ 0}; terminal payoff J = Φ(x(t_f)), x(t_f) ∈ M (3). Zero-sum value V(x) is the unique viscosity solution of min_{u_e} max_{u_p} H = max_{u_p} min_{u_e} H = Σ_{i=1}^{N_p} v_Pi‖λ_Pi‖₂ − Σ_{j=1}^{N_e} v_Ej‖λ_Ej‖₂ = 0 (4), with H(x,λ,u_p,u_e) = λᵀf, λ = ∇V, and minimax controls u*_Pi = λ_Pi/‖λ_Pi‖₂, u*_Ej = −λ_Ej/‖λ_Ej‖₂; boundary condition V = Φ on M. Minimax Hamiltonian H*(x,λ) = Σ_i v_Pi‖λ_Pi‖₂ − Σ_j v_Ej‖λ_Ej‖₂ (5). If V ∈ C², characteristics give the EL ODEs ẋ = ∂H*/∂λ = f*, λ̇ = −∂H*/∂x = 0 (6): **regular equilibrium trajectories are straight lines and regular equilibrium controls are constant — the review's key argument validating geometric methods for simple-motion M-RA games** [authors' claim, p. 5]; along them V̇ = ∇ᵀV ẋ = λᵀf* = 0 (value constant). On ∂M the costate is λ = ∇Φ(x) + μ∇g(x) (7), μ ∈ R found by substituting (7) into (4); then integrate (6) in inverse time. **HJI-as-verifier**: if X ⊇ M and V is (1) C¹ on X\M, (2) satisfies (4) on X\M, (3) V = Φ on ∂M, then V is the value over X [authors' restatement of a standard certificate]; examples: Garcia et al. (2020, RA-L; 2021, IEEE TAC), Yan et al. (2022, Automatica). Solver applications cited: active target defense (Pachter, Garcia & Casbeer 2019, Dyn. Games Appl.; Garcia et al. 2018, IEEE TAES; Liang et al. 2019, Automatica; Garcia et al. 2019a, IEEE TAC; Liang et al. 2021, IEEE TAC), turret defense (Akilan & Fuchs 2017, IEEE CCTA), turret-runner-penetrator/perimeter defense with boundary-restricted defenders (Von Moll et al. 2021 ACC; 2022a IEEE TAC; 2022b IEEE TAES). Assessment: general, handles richer dynamics, but high computational complexity (p. 3).

**5.5 Barrier catalog, game 1 — 2D bounded convex region (Sec. 4.1, eqs. (8)–(9), Fig. 3, p. 5; source: Yan et al. 2020, IEEE Trans. Robotics 36(1):107–124).** Ω closed convex 2D; T a straight segment of length ℓ; point capture (r_i = 0); homogeneous teams; speed ratio α > 1. A pursuer is **active** iff it dominates at least one point of T against the other pursuers; only active pursuers enter the barrier. Unique active pursuer P_i: B(x_Pi) = B̃(x_Pi) ∩ Ω_play, B̃ = ∪_{k=1}^{3} B̃_k, with B̃₁: α‖x − x₁‖₂ − ‖x_Pi − x₁‖₂ = 0 for x ≤ σ₁, y > 0 (straight-line branch to goal endpoint x₁ = [0,0]ᵀ); B̃₂: (α² − 1)y² − (x − x_Pi)² − (1 − 1/α²)y²_Pi = 0 for x ∈ (σ₁, σ₂), y > 0 (hyperbolic middle branch); B̃₃: α‖x − x₂‖₂ − ‖x_Pi − x₂‖₂ = 0 for x ≥ σ₂, y > 0, with x₂ = [ℓ,0]ᵀ, σ₁ = x_Pi/α², σ₂ = (1 − 1/α²)ℓ + x_Pi/α². Two active pursuers P_c = {P₁, P₂}, x_P₁ < x_P₂: five curves B̃₁–B̃₅ (9), alternating straight/hyperbolic branches for P₁ then P₂, with x₂ = (‖x_P₂‖²₂ − ‖x_P₁‖²₂)/(2(x_P₂ − x_P₁)) — the α-weighted equipoint of the two pursuers on T (400-dpi render confirms B̃₃ uses ‖x_{P₂} − x₂‖₂; the plain-text layer mis-gives x_P₁) — and σ₁ = x_P₁/α², σ₂ = (1 − 1/α²)x₂ + x_P₁/α², σ₃ = (1 − 1/α²)x₂ + x_P₂/α², σ₄ = (1 − 1/α²)ℓ + x_P₂/α². **Key structural result [quoted from Yan et al. 2020]**: with more than two active pursuers, any point of the barrier is determined by at most two active pursuers, so the barrier is the concatenation of two-pursuer barriers over adjacent active pursuers along T. Assessment: exact, closed-form, geometry-only; restricted to homogeneous speeds, point capture, straight T, convex Ω.

**5.6 Barrier catalog, game 2 — 3D game region (Sec. 4.2, p. 5–6; source: Yan et al. 2022, Automatica 140:110207).** Ω = R³, T a plane (two half-spaces); point and radius capture (r_i ≥ 0); heterogeneous speeds and capture radii; v_Pi > v_Ej. Barrier = tangency condition on the evader's dominance region: B(x_Pc) = {x_Ej ∈ Ω | {x ∈ Ω | ‖x − x_Pi‖₂ − α_ij‖x − x_Ej‖₂ − r_i ≥ 0, ∀1 ≤ i ≤ N_p} tangent to Ω_goal}, using that the dominance region is strictly convex before capture [result of Yan et al. 2022]. **Complexity lever [quoted]**: the unique point of E_j's dominance region closest to the goal is determined by at most three pursuers, so checking tangency over all pursuer subsets of size ≤ 3 suffices — "improving the computational efficiency drastically" [authors' claim]. Extension to a convex play region with an exit also discussed in Yan et al. (2022). Assessment: fully heterogeneous (speeds + radii), n-dimensional mindset, but the barrier is implicit (a tangency condition), not a closed curve list.

**5.7 Barrier catalog, game 3 — limited evasion lifetime (Sec. 4.3, eqs. (10)–(12), Fig. 4, p. 6; source: Yan et al. 2021b, Int. J. Control 94(8):2238–2251).** Ω = R², T a line; radius capture; pursuer faster; evader must reach Ω_goal within lifetime t_a > 0 or loses. Construction: start from the no-lifetime barrier B^∞ (as in Sec. 4.1); discard barrier points whose capture/reach time exceeds t_a (dashed in Fig. 4); complete with two cases — (i) lifetime-only active: evader runs straight to Ω_goal arriving exactly at t_a (green); (ii) lifetime and capture simultaneously active: capture, entry and lifetime expiry coincide (magenta). Result (one pursuer P_i vs one evader, α_ij = v_Pi/v_Ej): if |y_Pi| > v_Pi t_a + r_i, B(x_Pi) = {x ∈ R² | y = v_Pi t_a/α_ij} (10) (a horizontal line — the evader cannot beat the lifetime anywhere above it); otherwise B = ∪_{k=1}^{5} B_k (11): two horizontal lines y = v_Pi t_a/α_ij (x ≤ x₁ and x ≥ x₅), two circular arcs (x − x₁)² + y² = v²_Pi t_a²/α²_ij (x₁ < x < x₂) and (x − x₃)² + y² = v²_Pi t_a²/α²_ij (x₄ < x < x₅), and a middle branch x = x* − d₁d₂/α²_ij, y = d₁√(α²_ij − d₂²)/α²_ij, where x* ∈ R if |y_Pi| ≥ r_i and |x* − x_Pi| ≥ √(r_i² − y²_Pi) otherwise, and (12) x₁ = x_Pi − √((v_Pi t_a + r_i)² − y²_Pi), x₅ = x_Pi + √((v_Pi t_a + r_i)² − y²_Pi), x₂ = x₁ + v_Pi t_a√((v_Pi t_a + r_i)² − y²_Pi)/(α²_ij(v_Pi t_a + r_i)), x₄ = x₅ − (same increment). (d₁, d₂ are not defined in the review; they belong to Yan et al. 2021b — flagged as a gap of the recap, not of the source.) Assessment: adds a state constraint (lifetime) on top of capture; barrier piecewise geometry changes qualitatively with |y_Pi| vs v_Pi t_a + r_i.

**5.8 Barrier catalog, game 4 — view of the evasion team, 1 pursuer vs 2 evaders (Sec. 4.4, eqs. (13)–(15), Fig. 5, p. 6–7; source: Yan et al. 2021a, Int. J. Syst. Sci. 52(1):1–19).** Ω = R², T a line; point capture; α_j = v_P/v_Ej > 1, j = 1, 2; evaders know which of them is being chased. The barrier splits the state space into three parts: no capture / one capture / two captures under optimal play. **δ-Apollonius circle (13)**: 𝔼^δ_j = {x ∈ R² | α_j‖x − x_Ej‖₂ = ‖x − x_P‖₂ + v_P δ} — the dominance region when the pursuer starts chasing E_j after E_j has moved freely for δ. Barrier with time difference (14): B^δ_j(x_P) = {x ∈ R² | x = x* − ab, y = a√(1 − b²), x* ∈ P}, with x* = [x*,0]ᵀ, a = α_j‖x* − x_P‖₂ + v_Ej δ, b = α_j(x* − x_P)/‖x* − x_P‖₂; feasible set: if δ ≤ (1 − α_j²)|y_P|/(α_j v_Ej) then P = R (Fig. 5A) [as printed — see §7 erratum: with α_j > 1 the right-hand side is ≤ 0, so the intended threshold is presumably (α_j² − 1)|y_P|/(α_j v_Ej)]; if δ exceeds that bound, P = {x ∈ R | |x − x_P| ≥ √((α_j v_Ej δ/(1 − α_j²))² − y_P²)} (15) (Fig. 5B). Two-evader barrier = combination of the no-time-difference and time-difference one-vs-one barriers. **Cooperative evasion result**: an **aiding strategy** — the first-pursued evader E₁ moves away from the goal to improve E₂'s relative escape position at the moment E₁ is captured (sacrificial behavior, "frequently observed between prey animals") [authors' claim]. Fig. 5C/D: if P pursues E₁ first, the plane splits into Q_P (P captures E₂ after E₁) and Q_E (E₂ escapes); comparing the two pursuit orders shows P should pursue E₁ first [authors' conclusion from Yan et al. 2021a]. Assessment: rare review item — barrier analysis from the evasion team's viewpoint; introduces time-offset dominance, the key device for sequential-capture reasoning.

**5.9 Barrier catalog, game 5 — Lady in the Lake with multiple pursuers (Sec. 4.5, eqs. (16)–(17), Fig. 6, p. 7–8; source: Yan, Shi & Zhong 2017, IEEE ICCA, pp. 958–963).** Ω = R², T a circle; Ω_play = disk inside T, Ω_goal = exterior; one evader ("lady"); point capture; pursuers faster, **restricted to the circle T and uniformly distributed by cooperation**. The evader must penetrate T between two adjacent pursuers. **Critical speed ratio (16)**: let α₀ ∈ (1, +∞) be the unique solution of π/N_p + arccos(1/α₀) − √(α₀² − 1) = 0. If α < α₀, B(x_Pc) = ∅ — no barrier, the evader always escapes; escape strategies two types [quoted]: escape directly along a straight line if the distance to the closest pursuer is long enough, otherwise return to the center, adjust relative position, then escape straight (Fig. 6B: curve separates Υ₂, Υ₃; Υ₁ is the circle E should enter from Υ₂). If α ≥ α₀, the barrier emerges (Fig. 6C): B(x_Pc) = ∪_{i=1}^{N_p} B_i(x_Pc), B_i = {(ρ, θ_i) | |θ_i| = arccos(R/(αρ)) − √(α²ρ² − R²)/R − arccos(1/α) + √(α² − 1), ρ ∈ [ρ₀, R], |θ_i| ≤ π/N_p} (17), ρ₀ solving (17) at θ_i = π/N_p; R = circle radius. Assessment: the only catalog entry with goal-region-relative defender motion constraints; winner determination reduces to a scalar speed-ratio threshold depending only on N_p.

**5.10 Task allocation as constrained matching (Sec. 5, eq. (18), p. 8; sources: Tanimoto, Itai & Rodeh 1978, J. ACM; Yan et al. 2022, Automatica).** Coalitions of size ≤ N_c ≤ N_p; N_all = C¹_Np + C²_Np + ⋯ + C^{N_c}_Np candidate coalitions. Bipartite graph G = (V_P ∪ V_E, E): V_P = all coalitions, V_E = evaders; edge e_cj ∈ E iff P_c can capture E_j before entry to Ω_goal **while no strict subcoalition of P_c can** (minimality). Conflict graph C = (E, Ē) on the edges of G enforcing that a pursuer appears in at most one coalition of an executable matching. 0–1 ILP (18):
maximize Σ_{e_cj ∈ E} x_cj subject to Σ_{P_c ∈ V_P} x_cj ≤ 1 ∀E_j ∈ V_E; Σ_{E_j ∈ V_E} x_cj ≤ 1 ∀P_c ∈ V_P; x_cj + x_pq ≤ 1 ∀(e_cj, e_pq) ∈ Ē; x_cj ∈ {0,1} ∀e_cj ∈ E; x_cj = 0 ∀e_cj ∉ E.
Problem (18) is a special constrained matching problem, **proved NP-hard** [Yan et al. 2022] — intractable for large teams [authors' claim]. **1/N_c-approximation — Sequential Matching algorithm (Sec. 5.2)**: compute a maximum matching (e.g., max-flow) on the subgraph using only one-pursuer coalition vertices; remove matched players; repeat for two-pursuer coalitions, …, up to size N_c; merge the matchings (disjoint by construction) → a 1/N_c-factor approximation. Assessment: exact game-theoretic outcomes feed the edge set, so allocation quality is limited by the many-vs-one barrier analysis, and the approximation ratio degrades with maximum coalition size.

**5.11 Cooperative strategies (Sec. 6, p. 9).**
- **Voronoi-based (Sec. 6.1)**, for equal speeds: area-based (minimize the evader's Voronoi cell area — Pan et al. 2012, ACC); point-based (each pursuer targets a specific point of the evader's dominance region, e.g., the farthest point from the evader or the point closest to the goal — Yan et al. 2019b, IEEE TCyb. 49(4):1035–1046; Garcia et al. 2020, RA-L; Yan et al. 2022); relay pursuit (pursuers take over based on whose dominance region contains the evader).
- **Apollonius-circle-based (Sec. 6.2)**, for unequal speeds, point capture: since the evader's dominance region (∩ of Apollonius circles) is strictly convex, the point closest to a disjoint convex goal region is unique, and moving toward it under state feedback guarantees pursuit winning [result of Yan et al. 2019b]; **singularity caveat**: non-convex goal regions break uniqueness (Von Moll et al. 2020, J. Aerosp. Inf. Syst. 17(7):407–416) [review's stated limit].
- **Convex-optimization-based (Sec. 6.3, eq. (19))**, for v_Pi ≥ v_Ej with r_i ≥ 0 (where Voronoi/Apollonius closed forms fail because positive capture radii destroy closed-form dominance regions [review's assessment]): find the point x_I (may be non-unique) of the evader's dominance region closest to the goal by minimize_{(x,y) ∈ R^n×R^n} ‖x − y‖₂ s.t. f_ij(x) ≥ 0, g(y) ≤ 0, ∀i ∈ c (19), with f_ij from Def. 3 and Ω_goal = {x | g(x) ≤ 0} non-empty closed convex (index set "c" as printed — the active-pursuer set of Yan et al. 2022; not defined in the review — flagged in §7). Convexity follows from convexity of both the dominance region and the goal region. Each pursuer's control = direction toward x_I. **Guarantee [quoted from Yan et al. 2022]**: if pursuers are faster, x_I is unique and this feedback strategy ensures the dominance region never approaches the goal region → guaranteed pursuit winning, for both point and radius capture.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated open problems (Sec. 7, p. 9–10 — listed in full; they seed the skill's research-gap knowledge):**
1. **Player dynamics (7.1)**: simple motion permits instantaneous heading changes; ignored constraints (minimum turning radius, maximum acceleration, external forces) can make the synthesized strategies fail the actual task; incorporating them "will inevitably complicate the strategy synthesis" [authors' claim].
2. **Sequential capture (7.2)**: if a pursuer may capture multiple evaders sequentially, the problem becomes a dynamic vehicle routing problem in an adversarial setting (Bopardikar et al. 2010, IEEE TAC 55:2554–2569); existing barrier constructions are **myopic** — they reason only about the currently pursued evader. Consequences: luring behaviors (evaders draw pursuers away so others reach the goal), pursuers chasing farther evaders first; constructive results exist only for probabilistic spatio-temporal evader arrivals (Smith et al. 2009; Bajaj & Bopardikar 2019; Bajaj et al. 2021).
3. **Spatial-temporal coupling (7.3)**: the assignment of Sec. 5 assumes each coalition-vs-evader game is independent, but all players share one environment; trajectories across pairs are spatially/temporally coupled, which may cause collisions or be exploited "to design wiser strategies" — coupling-aware matching is open.
4. **Fast evaders (7.4)**: nearly all results assume v_Pi ≥ v_Ej; the crucial consequence is convexity of the evader's dominance region (Voronoi cell, Apollonius circle, or function level set), which guarantees a capture-free straight path to any goal point inside it. With faster evaders, capture requires more complex cooperation or exploitation of region characteristics (boundaries, convexity) — fundamentally different and open.
5. **Partial information (7.5)**: full-state knowledge (A2) is unrealistic: limited detection range, measurement errors depending on sensing devices, and difficulty counting/locating evaders in dense swarms. (Cf. Shishika et al. 2021, ICRA, partial-information target defense.)
