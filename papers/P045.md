## 2. Research Background
Robotic swarms — large numbers of small, cheap, individually limited robots — are motivated by missions that a few large robots cannot handle (surveillance, ad hoc network relay, disaster management, cooperative radar jamming) [1]–[9]. Because human operators cannot supervise each member of a large swarm, the swarm itself must autonomously solve task allocation: partitioning the agent set into subgroups and assigning each subgroup to a task, in the ST-MR category of Gerkey–Matarić's taxonomy [10] (each agent performs at most one task; each task may need multiple robots). The authors argue (citing [4], [5], [12]–[14]) that a swarm decision-making framework should be decentralized, scalable, predictable (convergence and outcome quality), adaptable to dynamic environments, robust to asynchrony, and able to accommodate agents with different interests. Existing self-organized approaches are either top-down (macroscopic Markov-chain/ODE population models, predictable only numerically, weak on individual preferences) or bottom-up (threshold-based microscopic rules, needing a macroscopic abstraction for performance evaluation); neither branch provides analytic guarantees on both convergence speed and solution quality under purely local information. Hedonic games [16]–[18] and, specifically, Darmann's recent result on anonymous hedonic games with social inhibition [44] supply the economic-theory seed that this paper transplants into multi-robot task allocation.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
**Problem.** Given $n_a$ homogeneous ST agents and $n_t$ multi-robot (MR) tasks plus a void task $t_\phi$, partition the agents among tasks to maximize the global utility (sum of individual utilities), where each individual utility $u_i(t_j, p)$ depends on the task and the number of co-working "participants" $p$ (Problem 1, eqs. (1)–(3), p. 1536).

**Gap, as stated by the authors.**
- [Authors' claim] Decentralized, scalable frameworks with analytic guarantees on convergence speed *and* outcome quality are missing; orchestrated (negotiation/auction) approaches give quality guarantees but scale poorly [20]–[23], while self-organized approaches scale but lack predictability (top-down: "such prediction... can be made mainly numerically", p. 1535; bottom-up: emergent performance requires a macroscopic model, [35]–[41]).
- [Authors' claim] The closest hedonic-game result, Darmann [44], shows agents with social inhibition converge to a Nash stable partition of an anonymous hedonic game, but only via a *centralized recursive* algorithm needing $O(n_a^2 \cdot n_t)$ iterations, under full information/synchronization.
- [Verified from cited text] Darmann [44] (Group Activity Selection from Ordinal Preferences, ADT 2015) indeed studies anonymous games where agents only care about coalition size ("social inhibition" = preferring smaller coalitions) and gives a recursive centralized procedure; the $O(n_a^2 \cdot n_t)$ bound is quoted consistently on pp. 1535 and 1540. The claim that Nash stability permits permission-free unilateral deviation (Remark 1, p. 1537, citing [18], [46]–[48]) is standard in the hedonic-games literature. The physical plausibility of decreasing individual utility (congestion/interference) is supported by the cited simulation evidence [49, Figs. 3–4] and the nonsuperadditive-coalition argument of [50] (verified as plausible citations, not re-derived).

**What the paper adds to close the gap**: a decentralized, asynchronous executable algorithm with $O(n_a^2)$-iteration convergence (removing the $n_t$ factor and the centralization of [44]), plus the first (for this setting) computable suboptimality lower bound and a 50% fixed bound under a nondecreasing-social-utility condition.

## 4. Core Contributions
1. **Theory (convergence)**: Identification of *single-peaked-at-one* (SPAO) preferences (Definition 4, p. 1537) — i.e., social inhibition — as the condition under which a Nash stable partition always exists (Theorem 1) and is reachable in at most $|A|(|A|+1)/2$ iterations (Theorem 2), improving the iteration count of [44] from $O(n_a^2 n_t)$ to $O(n_a^2)$ (under a fully connected network; $O(d_G n_a^2)$ time steps under a strongly connected network of diameter $d_G$).
2. **Algorithm (decentralization/asynchrony)**: Algorithm 1 (per-agent greedy decision loop) + Algorithm 2 (`D-Mutex`), a distributed mutex subroutine that selects a unique "valid partition" via lexicographic tie-breaking on (iteration count $r_i$, random time stamp $s_i$), making Theorems 1–2 hold under any strongly connected communication network, even asynchronously (p. 1539), with an explicit running-time/communication-overhead trade-off (Remark 5: capping transactions $n_c=1$ gives $O(n_a^3)$ time steps but $O(n_a)$ per-iteration communication).
3. **Theory (quality)**: A computable suboptimality lower bound $\alpha \ge J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}/(J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\lambda)$ (Theorem 3, eqs. (8)–(9)) and a fixed 50% bound $\alpha \ge 1/2$ when the social utility of every coalition is nondecreasing in membership (Theorem 4); extended to minimum-requirement tasks via auxiliary utilities (Propositions 1–2, eqs. (29)–(30)).
4. **Modeling (generality)**: The framework accommodates heterogeneous *interests* (different $u_i$, e.g., different distances to tasks) as long as all preferences are SPAO (Remark 3), and handles tasks with minimum required agent counts $R_j$ via dummy utilities satisfying Condition 1 (Section V).
5. **Experiments**: Monte Carlo studies (up to $n_a=320$, $n_t=20$) validating scalability (quadratic iteration growth with constant $C_1 \ll 1/2$), near-optimality with the Theorem-3 bound always enclosing the true suboptimality, adaptability to ±50% agent/task changes, and robustness to up to 80% non-operating agents.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

### 5.1 Model, information structure, and game mapping (pp. 1536–1538)
**Problem 1 (p. 1536, eqs. (1)–(3))**:
$\max_{\{x_{ij}\}} \sum_{\forall a_i\in A}\sum_{\forall t_j\in T} u_i(t_j,p)\,x_{ij}$ subject to $\sum_{\forall t_j\in T} x_{ij}\le 1,\ \forall a_i$ (2) and $x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ (3); $u_i: T\times|A|\to\mathbb{R}$, $u_i(t_\phi,p)=0$. The *global utility* is the objective (1); the *social utility* of a group is the sum of its members' individual utilities. This is an integral assignment problem; no complexity claim (NP-hardness) is made in the paper.

**Assumptions** (pp. 1536–1538): (1) homogeneous agents (mass production, [4]), utilities depend only on coalition *cardinality* and task, though task preferences may differ per agent (e.g., distance); each agent is an ST robot; (2) communication graph at least strongly connected, $\mathcal{N}_i$ = neighbor set; (3) every task is MR and executable (slowly) by a single agent (relaxed in Sec. V); (4) each agent knows only its own $u_i$; through communication it knows (a possibly stale, local copy of) the current partition; (5) a mutual-exclusion mechanism picks the single *deciding agent* per iteration (relaxed by Algorithm 2).

**GRAPE (Definitions 1–5, pp. 1537–1538)**. Instance $(A, T, \mathcal{P})$ with preferences $\mathcal{P}_i$ over task-coalition pairs $\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^*\cup\{t_\phi\}$, $\mathcal{X}^*=T^*\times\{1,\dots,n_a\}$. A *partition* $\Pi=\{S_1,\dots,S_{n_t},S_\phi\}$ disjointly covers $A$ ($S_\phi\equiv S_0$ = void-task coalition); $\Pi(i)$ = agent $a_i$'s task index. **Nash stable (Definition 3)**: $(t_{\Pi(i)},|S_{\Pi(i)}|)\succeq_i (t_j,|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|),\ \forall S_j\in\Pi$ — no agent gains by unilateral deviation; Remark 1 stresses that Nash stability (vs. core/other concepts [16], [46]–[48]) needs no negotiation permission, only broadcast of the altered decision. **SPAO (Definition 4)**: for every $(t_j,p)\in\mathcal{X}^*$, $(t_j,p_1)\succeq_i(t_j,p_2)$ whenever $p_1<p_2$ — individual utility monotonically nonincreasing in coalition size (*social inhibition*). Remark 2 grounds SPAO physically: interference reduces per-robot efficiency [49], shared-resource contention [51], [52, Example 3], and nonsuperadditive environments [50]; social utility itself is *unrestricted* (may be monotone or not). **Iteration (Definition 5)**: one arbitrary deciding agent re-optimizes over all task-coalition pairs given the current partition and exclusively updates it; iterations repeat until no agent deviates.

### 5.2 Lemma 1 (p. 1538, eq. (4)) — incremental re-convergence
**Statement.** Given a SPAO instance $(A,T,\mathcal{P})$ with an established Nash stable partition, if a new agent $a_r\notin A$ with SPAO preferences joins, the new instance $(\tilde{A},T,\tilde{\mathcal{P}})$, $\tilde{A}=A\cup\{a_r\}$: (1) still satisfies SPAO; (2) contains a Nash stable partition; (3) re-converges in at most $|\tilde{A}|$ iterations.

**Proof approach.** (i) Define per-agent slack, eq. (4):
$\Delta_\Pi(i):=\min_{S_j\in\Pi\setminus\{S_{\Pi(i)}\}}\ \max_{\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}}\{\Delta \mid (t_{\Pi(i)},|S_{\Pi(i)}|+\Delta)\succeq_i (t_j,|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|)\}$ — the fewest extra co-workers $a_i$'s current coalition can tolerate while $a_i$ still (weakly) prefers staying over defecting to each other coalition. (ii) Three SPAO characteristics: (a) $\Pi$ Nash stable $\Rightarrow\Delta_\Pi(i)\ge 0\ \forall i$; (b) $\Delta_\Pi(i)<0\Rightarrow a_i$ deviates next iteration; (c) an agent that just deviated has $\Delta\ge 0$ in the updated partition (it moved to its most preferred option). (iii) After $a_r$ joins, only members of the coalition $a_r$ selected can have $\Delta<0$ (their coalition grew by one); if none, $\Pi_1$ is already Nash stable. (iv) If some $a_q$ has $\Delta_{\Pi_1}(q)<0$, $a_q$ deviates; then $a_r$'s slack rises to $\Delta_{\Pi_2}(r)\ge 1$, i.e., a deviated agent tolerates one future joiner. The paper concludes "at most $|\tilde{A}|$ of iterations are required to hold $\Delta_{\tilde{\Pi}}(i)\ge 0$ for every agent".
- **Decisive quantity**: eq. (4) (the slack $\Delta_\Pi(i)$), and the "slack ≥ 1 after deviation" mechanism.
- **Local/global**: local reasoning per coalition; the bound is a worst-case *analytic* count.
- **Physical meaning**: one arrival disturbs at most the coalition it joins; social inhibition makes the disturbance "daisy-chain" die out because each re-arranged agent gains one unit of tolerance.
- **Extensibility**: this lemma is the engine of Theorems 1–2 *and* of the adaptability claim (Sec. IV-C): any environmental change reduces to a sequence of "new agent joins".
- **proof gap (minor, p. 1538)**: the counting step "at most $|\tilde{A}|$ iterations" is asserted from the immunity remark ("an agent who deviates to a coalition and expels one of the existing agents in that coalition will not deviate again even if another agent joins the coalition in a next iteration"). The immunity covers exactly *one* subsequent joiner per deviated agent; an agent receiving two later joiners (slack 0 twice consumed) could in principle deviate twice, and no potential-function or no-repeat argument is provided. The argument is therefore informal; experiments (Fig. 2, C1 ≪ 1/2) support practical fast convergence, and the existence claim (Theorem 1) survives via the constructive induction, but the tightness of the stated iteration bound is not formally airtight as written.

### 5.3 Theorem 1 (Existence, p. 1538)
**Statement.** If $(A,T,\mathcal{P})$ is SPAO, a Nash stable partition always exists.
**Proof approach.** Induction on $n=|A|$: base case $n=1$ trivial (agent takes its most preferred pair); induction step adds one SPAO agent to a Nash-stable $k$-agent instance and invokes Lemma 1(2). $M(k)\Rightarrow M(k+1)$; hence $M(n)$ for all $n\ge 1$. Decisive ingredient: Lemma 1. Global, analytic, constructive. Meaning: with selfish, socially inhibited agents, a self-consistent, negotiation-free assignment always exists regardless of task/agent configuration.

### 5.4 Theorem 2 (Convergence, p. 1538, eq. (5))
**Statement.** If $(A,T,\mathcal{P})$ is SPAO, at most $|A|(|A|+1)/2$ iterations are needed to determine a Nash stable partition.
**Proof approach.** Unfold the induction of Theorem 1: adding the $k$-th agent to a Nash-stable $(k-1)$-agent instance costs ≤ $k$ iterations (Lemma 1(3)), so $\sum_{k=1}^{|A|} k = |A|\cdot(|A|+1)/2$ (eq. (5)). A second, paragraph-length argument extends validity to *random initial partitions*: liberate agents one at a time from a "frozen" set $A'$; each liberation is seen by the others as a new-agent arrival per Lemma 1, so $A'\cup\{a_r\}$ re-converges within $|A'\cup\{a_r\}|$ iterations.
- Decisive equation: (5). Analytic worst case; Remark 4 notes the bound is conservative because Algorithm 1 lets all agents act concurrently rather than sequentially per subset.
- Meaning: polynomial (quadratic) convergence *in the number of unilateral decision events* — the predictability ingredient promised in Sec. I. Caveat: inherits the Lemma 1 counting informality (proof gap above); also "iterations" count decision events, not arithmetic operations (each iteration costs $O(n_t)$ comparisons, see §5.5).

### 5.5 Decentralized algorithm and complexity (pp. 1539–1540)
**Algorithm 1** (each agent, infinite loop): if not satisfied, pick $(t_{j^*},|S_{j^*}|)=\arg\max_{\forall S_j\in\Pi_i}(t_j,|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|)$ (Line 5 — $n_t+1$ evaluations, hence $O(n_t)$ per iteration); if strictly preferred to the current pair, join it, $r_i\leftarrow r_i+1$, draw $s_i\sim\mathrm{unif}[0,1]$; broadcast $M^i=\{r_i,s_i,\Pi_i\}$ to $\mathcal{N}_i$. **Algorithm 2 (`D-Mutex`)**: adopt the neighbor message with lexicographically larger $(r_k,s_k)$ (iteration count, then random time stamp) and mark self unsatisfied if a more valid partition exists — yielding a unique "valid partition" that eventually dominates all others under strong connectivity. Ties among multiple Nash stable partitions are resolved arbitrarily by the same mutex, "one of them can be distributedly selected" (p. 1539).
**Complexity results (Sec. IV-A, analytic, no theorem environment)**: a *time step* = one pass of the main loop; propagation-only passes are *dummy iterations*; ≤ $d_G$ dummy iterations occur between consecutive normal ones, so total time steps $=O(d_G n_a^2)$ ($O(n_a^2)$ if fully connected, $d_G=1$) — vs. $O(n_a^2\cdot n_t)$ for centralized [44]; per-agent running time $O(d_G n_t n_a^2)$; per-iteration communication overhead $O(|\mathcal{N}_i|\cdot n_a)$ (message = whole locally-known partition; quadratic only if $|\mathcal{N}_i|\propto n_a$, deemed unrealistic due to range limits). **Remark 5**: capping transactions at $n_c$ per iteration preserves Theorems 1–2 provided the union of communication graphs over time is connected; at $n_c=1$ (unicast) time steps degrade to $O(n_a^3)$ while communication drops to $O(n_a)$ — an explicit runtime/communication trade-off.

### 5.6 Theorem 3 (Suboptimality lower bound, general case, p. 1540, eqs. (8)–(9); proof pp. 1540–1541, eqs. (10)–(14))
**Statement.** For a Nash stable partition $\Pi$ obtained by GRAPE, with $\alpha:=J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}/J_{\mathrm{OPT}}$ (eq. (7), $J$ per eq. (6): $J=\sum_{\forall a_i\in A}u_i(t_{\Pi(i)},|S_{\Pi(i)}|)$),
$$\alpha \ \ge\ J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}/(J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\lambda),\qquad
\lambda\equiv\sum_{\forall S_j\in\Pi}\ \max_{a_i\in A,\,p\le|A|}\big\{p\cdot[u_i(t_j,p)-u_i(t_j,|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|)]\big\} \quad (8),(9)$$
**Assumptions**: $\Pi$ is Nash stable (Definition 3); individual utilities known a posteriori (the bound is computable once $\Pi$ and the $u_i$ are given); no further structure.
**Proof approach.** (i) Nash stability vs. the optimal partition $\Pi^*$: for every agent, $u_i(t_{\Pi(i)},|S_{\Pi(i)}|)\ \ge\ u_i(t^*_{j\leftarrow i},|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|)$ (eq. (10)), where $t^*_{j\leftarrow i}$ is the task $a_i$ would have under $\Pi^*$ and $S_j$ is the *Nash* coalition of that task. (ii) Add-and-subtract the optimal utility (eq. (11)) and sum over agents (eq. (12)): $J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}\ \ge\ J_{\mathrm{OPT}} - \sum_{\forall a_i}\{u_i(t^*_{j\leftarrow i},|S^*_j|)-u_i(t^*_{j\leftarrow i},|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|)\}$ — the subtracted term is each agent's "utility loss caused by the belated decision to its optimal task". (iii) Group per task and maximize: the loss is bounded by $\sum_{j=1}^{n_t}|S^*_j|\cdot\max_{a_i\in S^*_j}\{u_i(t^*_{j\leftarrow i},|S^*_j|)-u_i(t^*_{j\leftarrow i},|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|)\}$ (eq. (13)). (iv) Enlarge to $\lambda$: taking $p:=|S^*_j|$, each term is dominated by $\max_{a_i\in A,\,p\le|A|} L_{ij}[p]$ with $L_{ij}[p]=p\cdot(u_i(t_j,p)-u_i(t_j,|S_j\cup\{a_i\}|))$, summed over $S_j\in\Pi$ (eq. (14)). (v) Hence $J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}\ge J_{\mathrm{OPT}}-\lambda$, and rearranging gives (8).
- **Decisive inequality**: eq. (10) (Nash stability applied against optimal assignments) combined with eq. (14).
- **Local/global**: global, analytic; $\lambda$ is instance-dependent (no universal constant) but *computable* from the obtained partition.
- **Physical meaning**: the optimality gap is charged to the "displacement losses" of agents that the optimal partition would have placed differently; SPAO keeps those losses bounded because utilities cannot collapse arbitrarily with $p$.
- **Extensibility**: the bound is the workhorse for the large-scale experiments (Fig. 4) and is lifted to Problem 2 via Proposition 2.
- **Observation (compressed step, pp. 1540–1541)**: the passage "(13) is at most (14)" is stated without derivation; it holds because the maximization over $a_i\in A\supseteq S^*_j$ and over $p\le|A|$ at $p=|S^*_j|$ reproduces (13)'s summands, but the paper leaves the correspondence (including the task-index shift between $S^*_j\in\Pi^*$ and $S_j\in\Pi$) implicit.

### 5.7 Theorem 4 (Suboptimality lower bound, 50% special case, p. 1541, eqs. (15)–(22))
**Statement.** Given a SPAO instance, if (i) the social utility of any coalition is nondecreasing in participants — for any $S_j\subseteq A$, $a_l\in A\setminus S_j$: $\sum_{\forall a_i\in S_j}u_i(t_j,|S_j|)\ \le\ \sum_{\forall a_i\in S_j\cup\{a_l\}}u_i(t_j,|S_j\cup\{a_l\}|)$ — and (ii) all individual utilities induce SPAO preferences, then any Nash stable partition $\hat{\Pi}$ from GRAPE achieves at least 50% of the optimal global utility: $V(\Pi^*)\le 2V(\hat{\Pi})$, i.e., $\alpha\ge 1/2$ (a 2-approximation).
**Proof approach.** (i) Notation $V(\Pi)$ (eq. (15)) and the merge operator $\Pi^A\oplus\Pi^B:=\{S^A_0\cup S^B_0,\dots,S^A_{n_t}\cup S^B_{n_t}\}$, where an agent appearing in two merged coalitions is counted twice (so $|\oplus|$-partitions may exceed $|A|$). (ii) Condition (i) implies $V(\Pi^A)\le V(\Pi^A\oplus\Pi^B)$ (eq. (16)) — adding members never lowers social utility. (iii) Decompose $V(\hat{\Pi}\oplus\Pi^*)$ (eq. (17)) into (a) the $\hat{\Pi}$-indexed sum over all of $A$ and (b) the $\Pi^*$-indexed sum over $A^-$ (agents following only $\Pi^*$). (iv) By SPAO (condition (ii)), enlarging coalitions cannot increase the individual sums: term (a) $\le V(\hat{\Pi})$ (eq. (18)); term (b) $\le\sum_{\forall a_i\in A^-}u_i(t_{\Pi^*(i)},|\hat{S}_{\Pi^*(i)}\cup\{a_i\}|)$ (eq. (19)) $\le$ (Nash stability, eq. (20)) $\sum_{\forall a_i\in A^-}u_i(t_{\hat{\Pi}(i)},|\hat{S}_{\hat{\Pi}(i)}|)\le V(\hat{\Pi})$ (eq. (21), since $A^-\subseteq A$). (v) Hence $V(\hat{\Pi}\oplus\Pi^*)\le 2V(\hat{\Pi})$ (eq. (22)), and (16) with $\Pi^A=\Pi^*$ gives $V(\Pi^*)\le V(\hat{\Pi}\oplus\Pi^*)\le 2V(\hat{\Pi})$. $\blacksquare$
- **Decisive inequality chain**: (17)→(22) plus (16). Global, analytic, clean.
- **Physical meaning**: when teamwork has nondecreasing returns to the *group* (e.g., submodular rewards under equal sharing with mild costs) while individuals suffer congestion, the selfish equilibrium is provably within a factor 2 of the social optimum — the hedonic-game analogue of price-of-anarchy bounds.
- **Extensibility/caveat**: condition (i) is *orthogonal* to SPAO and fails for peaked-reward tasks (social utility decays beyond $n^d_j$; Fig. 1(a)) and whenever individual costs dominate; there the computable Theorem-3 bound still applies. The proof's double-counting convention in $\oplus$ (an agent counted twice) is essential and correctly handled, but note $V(\hat{\Pi}\oplus\Pi^*)$ is a quantity of an infeasible "doubled" system — the argument is a counting/device argument, not a feasible-solution construction.

### 5.8 Section V: minimum-requirement tasks (p. 1542, eqs. (23)–(30))
**Problem 2** (eqs. (23)–(26)): maximize (1) subject additionally to $\sum_{\forall a_i\in A}x_{ij}\ge R_j,\ \forall t_j$ (24), with $u_i(t_j,p)=0$ if $p<R_j$ (27) (task infeasible below requirement). Because (27) breaks SPAO, an **auxiliary individual utility** is introduced, eq. (28): $\tilde{u}_i(t_j,p)=u^0_i(t_j,p)$ if $p\le R_j$, else $u_i(t_j,p)$, with dummy utility $u^0_i$.
**Condition 1**: for every agent and any two tasks, $(t_j,R_j)\succ_i(t_k,R_k+1)$ — an under-staffed task beats every properly-staffed task, so agents flock to fill requirements first.
**Proposition 1.** If every $u_i(t_j,p)$ is monotonically decreasing for $p\ge R_j$ and the dummy utilities $u^0_i$ are chosen to satisfy Condition 1 and SPAO for $p\le R_j$, then the $\tilde{u}_i$ transform into a preference $n_a$-tuple holding Condition 1 *and* SPAO for all $p\in\{1,\dots,n_a\}$; hence Theorems 1–2 apply and, if $|A|\ge\sum_{\forall t_j}R_j$, the Nash stable partition satisfies (24). Example construction (p. 1542): $u^0_i(t_3,p)=\max_{\forall t_j}\{u_i(t_j,R_j+1)\}+\beta$, $\beta>0$, for $p\le R_3$. (Direct construction; no proof beyond the definition check.)
**Proposition 2 (eqs. (29)–(30)).** For a Nash stable partition obtained via Proposition 1,
$$\alpha\ \ge\ \frac{J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}}{J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\tilde{\lambda}}\cdot\frac{J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}}{J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\delta},\qquad \delta\equiv\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}-J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}\ \ (\ge 0,\ \tilde{u}_i\ge u_i), \quad (29)$$
and if every $\tilde{u}_i$ satisfies the Theorem 4 conditions, $\alpha\ge\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}}{J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\delta}$ (30). **Proof** (p. 1543, eq. (31)): apply (8) in the auxiliary world, $\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}/\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\ge\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}/(\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\tilde{\lambda})$; use $\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}=J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\delta$, $\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\ge J_{\mathrm{OPT}}$, and monotonicity of the right side in $\tilde{J}_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}$; multiply through by $J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}/(J_{\mathrm{GRAPE}}+\delta)$. If $\delta=0$ the bounds reduce to Theorems 3/4. Analytic; the two-factor penalty separates the game's price of anarchy ($\tilde{\lambda}$ factor) from the dummy-utility distortion ($\delta$ factor).

**Summary of formal results**: Lemma 1, Theorems 1–4, Propositions 1–2 (7 labeled results), plus the analytic complexity statements of Sec. IV-A and Remarks 1–5. No differential-game content — this is a discrete/coalitional game; the value for the differential-game archive is methodological (decentralized game-theoretic allocation with stability and approximation guarantees).

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated** (p. 1547): (i) a fair head-to-head comparison with existing self-organized methods is "nontrivial" and remains ongoing work; (ii) agent anonymity is restrictive — heterogeneous agents with social inhibition were *observed experimentally* to converge but are unproven; (iii) analysis of the outcome's quality in a min–max (fairness) sense is future work.

**Critical observations (with page/eq. anchors)**:
1. **Informal counting in Lemma 1/Theorem 2** (p. 1538): the "at most $|\tilde{A}|$ iterations" argument covers only one subsequent joiner per deviated agent (see §5.2 proof gap); a formal potential-function or no-repeat argument for termination/bound is absent. Theorem 1's existence claim is more robust, but as printed the polynomial bound rests on an informal chain.
2. **Compressed bound step** (13)→(14) (pp. 1540–1541): the $p$-multiplier and the $\Pi^*$-to-$\Pi$ task-index correspondence are implicit; a careful reader must reconstruct why $\lambda$ dominates the grouped loss.
3. **The 50% guarantee is narrower than the abstract suggests**: Theorem 4 requires nondecreasing *social* utility, which fails for peaked-reward tasks beyond $n^d_j$ (Fig. 1(a)) and whenever distance costs are non-negligible (eq. (32): social utility $=r-\sum_i c_i$). Only the submodular-reward family with mild costs qualifies — yet the headline experiments (Fig. 2) run both task types.
4. **No termination detection**: Algorithm 1 is a `while true` loop (p. 1539); agents are never told the partition is Nash stable, so a stopping rule / convergence-detection protocol is unaddressed (relevant for real deployments).
5. **Communication footprint**: each message carries the entire locally-known partition, giving $O(|\mathcal{N}_i|\cdot n_a)$ per-iteration overhead (p. 1540); scalability beyond $n_a=320$ is untested, and stale local partitions $\Pi_i$ (Assumption 4, p. 1536) mean agents decide on outdated information — reconciled only ex post by the mutex, informally.
6. **Model-vs-implementation bridge**: Theorems 1–2 assume one deciding agent per iteration; Algorithm 1 lets all agents act simultaneously, and the equivalence is argued in prose ("From each partition's viewpoint, it can be regarded as being evolved by a random sequence of the agents", p. 1539) rather than proven.
7. **Printed inconsistency**: Sec. VI-E text ($n_t=5$, $n_a=40$) vs. Fig. 6 caption ($n_t=10$, $n_a=160$), p. 1546.
8. **Optimality verified only at toy scale**: brute-force $J_{\mathrm{OPT}}$ requires $n_a=12$, $n_t=3$ (p. 1544); large-scale near-optimality is only assessed through the (instance-dependent, ~60–70%) lower bound, which itself depends on the partition found.
9. **No physical experiments**; costs and rewards are synthetic distance/log functions; equal fair allocation [53], [54] is assumed rather than derived from robot dynamics.
10. **Coverage/fairness**: Problem 1 (eqs. (1)–(3)) has no task-completion constraint — tasks can be abandoned when $u_i\le 0$; Problem 2 needs $|A|\ge\sum_j R_j$ and Condition 1 (p. 1542), and the dummy-utility design is instance-specific (the $\beta$-shift example).

## 8. Citation Value
**Paragraph role**: P045 is the foundational reference for *hedonic-game-based decentralized task allocation* in multi-robot systems: it imports Darmann's anonymous hedonic games with social inhibition into swarm robotics, converts a global ST-MR assignment problem into a Nash-stability-seeking coalition game, and delivers the full package (existence, polynomial convergence, computable and fixed approximation bounds, asynchronous decentralized implementation) that top-down and bottom-up swarm methods lack. In a paper's citation economy it serves as (a) the canonical "cooperation of selfish agents is compatible with social welfare under inhibition" evidence, (b) the contrast point against market/auction (CBBA-style [22]) and macroscopic-population allocation, and (c) the methodological ancestor of later anonymous/hedonic MRTA and congestion-aware coalition work. For a differential-game/reach-avoid collection, it is the standard citation when a higher-level *task/coalition layer* above continuous pursuit-evasion gameplay is needed (e.g., defender teams self-partitioning among intruders before a geometric game is played per coalition).
**Suggested citation sentences**: "Self-interested agents with single-peaked-at-one (socially inhibitive) preferences are guaranteed to reach a Nash stable task allocation within $O(n_a^2)$ iterations, with a computable suboptimality lower bound and a 2-approximation when social utilities are nondecreasing [P045]." Or: "At the task-allocation layer we adopt the anonymous hedonic game framework of [P045], which yields a decentralized, asynchronous Nash stable partition of defenders into interception teams under strongly connected communications."
