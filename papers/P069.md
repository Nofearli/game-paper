## 2. Research Background
This paper studies planar pursuit–evasion (PE) games in which mobile agents move with fixed (not necessarily equal) speeds and simple motion (instantaneous turns, unbounded maneuverability) in a plane containing known obstacles that inhibit motion. The central object is the **dominance region**: a point is dominated by a player if that player can reach it before the opponents *regardless of their actions*; a dominance region is the set of all points dominated by one player. Obstacle-bearing PE is motivated by predator/prey dynamics shaped by human infrastructure, robot teams in urban search-and-rescue, and military operations in urban terrain; obstacles also act **asymmetrically** (a ground vehicle is blocked by curbs/buildings that do not affect a UAV, while no-fly zones affect only the UAV). The intellectual lineage is Isaacs' Apollonius-circle solution of the obstacle-free game, extended here via time-optimal-path (isochrone) machinery from computational geometry (continuous Dijkstra; Mitchell 1993; Hershberger & Suri 1999). The work generalizes the authors' CDC 2014 conference paper, which handled only a line-segment obstacle with symmetric effects and no strategy analysis.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
Two problems are posed (Section 1.2):
- **P1 (PE dominance regions)**: given pursuer P (speed vP) and evader E (speed vE) with simple motion, full mutual knowledge of positions, and a known obstacle, find the locus L separating the region dominated by P from that dominated by E.
- **P2 (Prey–Protector–Predator, "P3", game)**: two teams — Red = {predator P, goal: capture E}, Blue = {protector R and prey/evader E, goal: rendezvous to rescue E before capture} — find the region where Blue can rendezvous before capture regardless of P's actions. (Related to the Lady–Bandits–Bodyguards game of Rusnak (2005), but there the bodyguards intercept the bandit and often start at the lady's location, whereas here the protector starts away from the prey and must rendezvous with it.)

**Gap — [authors' claim]**: "the literature … does not contain a description of the dominance regions for these scenarios" (p. 2), i.e., a complete description of dominance regions for PE games with known obstacles is missing. **[Verified from cited text as characterized by the authors]**: Isaacs' (1965) Obstacle Tag game only illustrates dispersal surfaces and instantaneous mixed strategies; Giovannangeli et al. (2010) use visibility-constrained portions of the standard Apollonius circle but never the overall dominance structure; Schaudt's (1991) multiplicatively weighted crystal-growth Voronoi diagrams forbid paths through other competitors' regions (unlike PE, where players traverse each other's regions); Bakolas & Tsiotras (2012) relay pursuit via dynamic Voronoi diagrams requires equal-speed pursuers and no obstacles; Hwang et al. (2005) reach-set collision avoidance ignores obstacles. The CDC 2014 precursor [authors' claim, p. 3] handled only line-segment obstacles, symmetric effects, and no strategies — consistent with its title. Five motivating questions Q1–Q5 (p. 3) — dominance regions with obstacle, who benefits, asymmetric effects, can the protector rescue in time, and optimal pursuit/evasion strategies — instantiate P1/P2.

## 4. Core Contributions
As listed verbatim in Section 1.5 (C1–C8), reorganized by type:
1. **Theory — dominance partition with obstacles (C1)**: Theorem 6: the plane is exhaustively divided into three disjoint regions (P strictly dominates / E strictly dominates / neither dominates), and the third region is obtained by intersecting bundles of isochrones.
2. **Theory — closed-form boundary (C2)**: Theorem 7 + Remark 1: every piece of the dominance boundary is a generalized Apollonius curve (limaçon, Apollonius circle, or vertex-centered circle) parameterized by the final-segment geometry; singular surfaces (type-(p,u,p) lines and hyperbolic dispersal surfaces) organize the assembly of the solution "in the large".
3. **Modeling / analysis (C3)**: comparative study of dominance regions with vs. without obstacles — an obstacle can benefit *or* hinder either the faster or the slower player.
4. **Modeling / analysis (C4)**: obstacles with asymmetric effects on the players (UAV vs. UGV); only the affected player generates singular surfaces.
5. **Modeling (C5)**: the novel P3 game for rescue / combat search-and-recovery / biological scenarios.
6. **Theory (C6)**: Theorem 8: obstacle-free P3 game of kind decided by one inequality in four dimensionless parameters (γR, γP, α, θP), generalizing to n predators and m protectors (≤ mn evaluations).
7. **Theory (C7)**: Theorem 9: obstacle-laden P3 game decided by pairwise obstacle-modified dominance (Blue wins iff some point is dominated by both prey and protector vs. the predator).
8. **Strategy (C8)**: optimal pursuit and evasion strategies for the two-player game of degree with time-to-capture payoff in the presence of obstacles (Section 7).

**Dynamics / information / termination (used throughout)**: simple motion, constant speeds, instantaneous turns; capture requires zero pursuer–evader distance (p. 1); rescue = E–R rendezvous; each player knows opponents' locations at all times (P1 statement, p. 2); the complete solution assumes full information (p. 3). No HJI/viscosity machinery is used — the approach is purely geometric (isochrones = level curves of the time-optimal-control value function).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results (exhaustive)

**Setting**: agents move with simple motion at constant speeds; time-optimal paths = shortest Euclidean paths avoiding obstacles S (p. 4); an **isochrone** is a level curve of the time-optimal transfer-time value function; a **generating point** is an obstacle vertex or the agent's initial location.

### 5.1 Background theorems (Section 2, p. 4)

- **Theorem 1 (p. 4)**: Without obstacles, time-optimal paths are straight lines and isochrones are concentric circles centered at the agent's initial location. *Proof approach*: wavefront propagation (Mitchell 1993). *Meaning*: recovers the classical setting.
- **Theorem 2 (p. 4)**: With polygonal obstacles, time-optimal paths are broken lines breaking at obstacle vertices, and isochrones are arcs of concentric circles centered at generating points. *Proof approach*: simulated wavefront propagation (continuous Dijkstra). *Meaning*: the crucial structural fact — even with obstacles, every isochrone piece is a circular arc about a *known* center, so pairwise isochrone intersections are analytically tractable. *Extensibility*: holds for arbitrary polygonal obstacle sets; used verbatim for polygonal (Section 5.1) and asymmetric (Section 5.2) obstacles.
- **Theorem 3 (p. 4)**: The curves separating the plane into regions with a unique generating point are line segments or arcs of hyperbola. *Meaning*: with Fig. 2, regions 2 and 3 (reached around opposite ends of the obstacle) are separated by a hyperbola whose points are equitime via either direction. *Role*: becomes the singular-surface taxonomy of Remark 1.
- **Theorem 4 (p. 4, eq. (1))** [cited from Isaacs 1965; Kabamba & Girard 2014]: Without obstacles the PE dominance regions are divided by an Apollonius circle:
  $(\gamma^2 - 1)\,r^2 + 2d\cos(\theta)\, r - d^2 = 0$, (1)
  where (r, θ) are polar coordinates with origin at E's initial location, zero azimuth along the line of sight to P; γ = vP/vE; d = initial separation. *Assumptions*: simple motion, no obstacles. *Consequences drawn (pp. 4–5)*: games of kind are solved by the circle; if vP > vE then E's region is bounded and P has a **dominant strategy** (go to E's initial location, then follow E's path); dominance regions also suffice for games of degree.
- **Theorem 5 (p. 5)** [cited from Isaacs 1965]: In the obstacle-free two-player game of degree with time-to-capture payoff (P minimizes, E maximizes), capture occurs at the point C on the Apollonius circle **farthest from E's initial position**, and both players travel to C in minimum time. Corollary geometry (p. 5): P, E, C are co-linear → classical pursuit (ψP = β) and classical evasion (ψE = β), equivalently from the range-rate equation
  $\dot\rho = v_E\cos(\beta - \psi_E) - v_P\cos(\beta - \psi_P)$, (2)
  where ψP, ψE are headings and β the line-of-sight angle; P minimizes ρ̇ and E maximizes ρ̇ by aligning with β. Also stated: if both play optimally, the Apollonius circle at any intermediate time is **tangent** to the initial circle at C; a lateral deviation by either player shifts the capture point and curves the other's path.

### 5.2 Dominance partition by isochrone intersection (Section 3, p. 5)

- **Theorem 6 (p. 5)**: In the PE game **with obstacles**, the plane is exhaustively divided into three disjoint regions: (1) where one player strictly dominates, (2) where the other strictly dominates, (3) where **neither** dominates; moreover region (3) is obtained by intersecting bundles of isochrones (the two bundles parameterized by the common time t). *Proof (complete, 10 lines)*: take an arbitrary point and solve the two time-optimal control problems from the players' initial locations to it; either one transfer time is strictly smaller (interior of a dominance region) or they are equal (interface, characterized by equality of transfer time, hence an intersection of same-duration isochrones). *Key logical step*: because trajectories do not interact (players do not block each other), "reach before the opponent regardless of actions" reduces to pointwise comparison of the two decoupled minimum arrival times — this is exactly where the paper departs from crystal-growth Voronoi diagrams (Schaudt 1991), as flagged on p. 2. *Verification against eq. (1)* (pp. 5–6, eqs. (3)–(6)): obstacle-free isochrones
  $x_E^2 + y_E^2 = v_E^2 t^2$, (3); $(x_P - x_{P,0})^2 + (y_P - y_{P,0})^2 = v_P^2 t^2$, (4);
  eliminating t with xE = xP = x, yE = yP = y:
  $\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{v_E^2} = \dfrac{(x-x_{P,0})^2 + (y-y_{P,0})^2}{v_P^2}$, (5);
  substituting xP,0 = d, yP,0 = 0, γ = vP/vE:
  $(\gamma^2-1)(x^2+y^2) + 2dx - d^2 = 0$, (6),
  which agrees with (1). *Meaning*: the dominance boundary is the equal-time locus ("simultaneous arrival set") even with obstacles; the obstacle-free case is exactly recovered. *Physical meaning of L*: it is role-free — constructed identically for pursuer and evader, yet suffices for both games of kind and degree.
- **Section 3.2 (p. 5–6, Fig. 3)**: for a line-segment obstacle the boundary is **piecewise smooth with two cusps**; plotting intersections of same-duration isochrones (marked ∗) traces the boundary, which bulges relative to the obstacle-free Apollonius circle (shown dashed for comparison).

### 5.3 Closed-form boundary — solution in the small (Section 4.1, p. 6)

- **Theorem 7 (p. 6, eq. (7))**: Each portion of the dominance boundary satisfies, for specific constants tB and d:
  $(\gamma^2 - 1)\,r^2 + 2\bigl(d\cos\theta - \gamma^2 v_A t_B\bigr)\, r + \bigl(\gamma^2 v_A^2 t_B^2 - d^2\bigr) = 0$. (7)
  *Setup*: A = the player who starts the **final segment** of its time-optimal path first (departs at t = 0); B = the other, departing its final segment at t = tB; d = distance between the final segments' starting points; polar origin at the start of A's final segment, zero azimuth along the line of sight from that origin to B's position at t = tB; γ = vB/vA. *Proof (complete)*: law of cosines on the final-segment triangle (Fig. 4):
  $v_B^2 (t - t_B)^2 = v_A^2 t^2 + d^2 - 2 v_A t\, d\cos\theta$, (8);
  rearranging with γ = vB/vA:
  $(\gamma^2-1)t^2 + 2\bigl(\tfrac{d}{v_A}\cos\theta - \gamma^2 t_B\bigr) t + \bigl(\gamma^2 t_B^2 - \tfrac{d^2}{v_A^2}\bigr) = 0$, (9);
  substituting r = vAt and multiplying by vA² yields (10) = (7). *Decisive step*: eq. (8); the "head start" tB enters as the extra constant term −2γ²vAtB·r in (7), which destroys circularity.
  *Three forms depending on (tB, d)* (p. 6):
  1. a **limaçon** when tB ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0 — the generic obstacle case (the obstacle forces at least one player's final segment to start after the other's);
  2. an **Apollonius circle** when tB = 0, d ≠ 0 — recovers (1);
  3. a **circle centered at an obstacle vertex** when tB ≠ 0, d = 0 — both players funnel past the same vertex at different times. Setting d = 0 in (7):
     $(\gamma^2-1)r^2 - 2\gamma^2 v_A t_B\, r + \gamma^2 v_A^2 t_B^2 = 0$, (11),
     with two roots
     $r_1 = \dfrac{\gamma v_A t_B}{\gamma - 1}, \qquad r_2 = \dfrac{\gamma v_A t_B}{\gamma + 1}$. (12)
     Since intersections of this type require vB > vA (if A is faster, A dominates the entire such region), γ > 1 and r1, r2 > 0; **r2 corresponds to a suboptimal path for A** (A turns back toward the vertex exactly when B reaches it), so the time-optimal isochrones intersect along the circle of radius **r1**. The fourth case tB = d = 0 (both at the vertex) is ignored because capture occurs there (p. 6).
  *Physical meaning*: the obstacle converts circular Apollonius boundaries into limaçon arcs; each boundary piece is indexed by (d, tB), i.e., by which obstacle vertices the two players' geodesics bend around and the resulting time offset. *Extensibility*: immediate for polygonal obstacles (more generating points, same equation) and asymmetric obstacles (fewer singular surfaces).
- **Remark 1 (p. 7)**: The singular surfaces of the two-player PE game with obstacles are precisely the curves of Theorem 3, namely (1) surfaces of type **(p, u, p)** (Isaacs' taxonomy): straight-line portions emanating from a generating obstacle vertex and extending away from another generating point parallel to the line of sight — across them, time-optimal paths are parallel to the surface but bend at the vertex on one side only; and (2) **dispersal surfaces**: arcs of hyperbola, representing a choice of which way to round the obstacle. *Consequences (p. 7)*: **cusps of the dominance boundary occur where the boundary crosses a dispersal surface**; assembling solutions in the small over the regions delimited by singular surfaces reproduces the isochrone-intersection boundary of Fig. 3 — the two construction methods agree.

### 5.4 Obstacle effects, polygonal and asymmetric obstacles (Sections 4.3, 5, pp. 7–8)

- **Comparative result (Section 4.3, Fig. 5, contribution C3)**: with vP = 2vE in both scenarios, the obstacle-modified dominance region may be **contained in** the obstacle-free Apollonius disk or **contain** it: in Fig. 5(a) the slower evader benefits, in Fig. 5(b) the faster pursuer benefits. Hence "the obstacle can be either a benefit or a hindrance to both the faster and the slower player, depending on the initial player locations" (p. 7). Region labels: "1" = both players reach by straight paths, "2" = one player must round the obstacle, "3" = both must.
- **Polygonal obstacles (Section 5.1, Fig. 6)**: method unchanged; the number of singular surfaces grows with the number of generating points (constructible via Hershberger & Suri 1999 / Mitchell 1993); isochrones remain circular arcs so Theorem 7 still holds. For the triangular obstacle, the evader's dispersal surface splits into two hyperbola arcs: curve "b" = equitime via vertex 1 vs. vertex 3; curve "a" = equitime via vertex 1 vs. via vertices 3 then 2; the hyperbolas meet at the singular surface rising from vertex 2.
- **Asymmetric obstacles (Section 5.2, Fig. 7, contribution C4)**: UAV vs. UGV — obstacles affect only one player, so **only that player generates singular surfaces** and their number decreases. With six singular surfaces in the symmetric case, four dominance boundaries arise (neither / both / only-△ / only-∗ affected): all four coincide with a portion of the Apollonius circle inside the straight-line-reachable region "1"; boundary "a" (only △ affected) is the worst case for △ (smallest region); boundary "c" (only ∗ affected) is △'s best case (largest region); boundary "b" (symmetric) is intermediate.

### 5.5 P3 game of kind (Section 6, pp. 8–10)

Coordinate frame (p. 8–9): origin at the prey; zero azimuth along the prey→protector line of sight. **Four dimensionless parameters**: γR (protector/prey speed ratio), γP (predator/prey speed ratio), α = dR/dP (ratio of prey–protector to prey–predator initial distances), θP (angle between the prey's lines of sight to protector and predator).

- **Theorem 8 (p. 9, eq. (13))**: Given γR > 1, γP > 1, α, θP: if there exists θ such that
  $\alpha\,\dfrac{\gamma_P^2 - 1}{\gamma_R^2 - 1}\cdot \dfrac{-\cos\theta + \sqrt{\gamma_R^2 - \sin^2\theta}}{-\cos(\theta - \theta_P) + \sqrt{\gamma_P^2 - \sin^2(\theta - \theta_P)}} < 1$, (13)
  then the **Blue team dominates the game of kind**; if no such θ exists, the **Red team dominates**. *Proof (complete)*: the polar form of the protector/prey and predator/prey Apollonius circles (from (1)):
  $r_R = \dfrac{-\cos\theta \pm \sqrt{\gamma_R^2 - \sin^2\theta}}{\gamma_R^2 - 1}\, d_R$, (14);
  $r_P = \dfrac{-\cos(\theta-\theta_P) \pm \sqrt{\gamma_P^2 - \sin^2(\theta-\theta_P)}}{\gamma_P^2 - 1}\, d_P$, (15).
  Because the prey is the slowest player (γR, γP > 1), the dominance boundaries surround the prey, so (14)–(15) give one positive and one negative root per θ; taking the positive (+) branches, Blue dominates at direction θ iff rR < rP (16), equivalently rR/rP < 1 (17); substituting (14)–(15) and α = dR/dP gives (13). *Decisive inequality*: (16). *Interpretation*: a rendezvous point on the protector/prey Apollonius boundary at bearing θ must lie inside the prey's dominance region versus the predator (i.e., the whole three-player simultaneous-arrival condition collapses, by transitivity of equality among the three Apollonius circles, to a single pairwise comparison per bearing). *Local/global*: global (one sweep over θ ∈ [0, 2π)); the check is a 1-D search over a continuous periodic function (not stated explicitly).
- **Worked example (p. 9)**: motivating example without obstacle: γR = 2, γP = 3, α = 1, θP ≈ 49°. The left-hand side of (13) exceeds 1 for all θ (its minimum, at θ = 0, is ≈ 1.19 by direct evaluation), so **Red dominates** — no Blue rendezvous point exists against optimal play; Fig. 8 shows the protector/prey Apollonius circle lying entirely inside the predator's dominance region.
- **Generalization to n predators, m protectors (p. 9)**: evaluate (13) at most **mn** times; Blue dominates iff some protector dominates all n predators; otherwise Red dominates.
- **Multi-predator example (Fig. 9, p. 9, eqs. (18)–(20))**: n = 3, m = 1, γR = 3, γP1 = γP2 = γP3 = 2, dR = dP1 = dP3 = 5, dP2 = 4, θR = 0, θP1 = 90°, θP2 = 180°, θP3 = 270°. At θ = 0: Predator 1: √3/4 < 1 (18); Predator 2: 5/16 < 1 (19); Predator 3: √3/4 < 1 (20) — the protector dominates each pairwise game, hence **Blue dominates** (rendezvous possible near θ = 0, to the left in the figure). (Independently re-derived and confirmed: e.g., (18) equals α(γP²−1)/(γR²−1)·(−1+3)/(0+√3) = (3/8)(2/√3) = √3/4.)
- **Theorem 9 (p. 10, contribution C7)**: Given a P3 game **with obstacles**: if there exists a point where the predator is dominated by **both** the prey and the protector in their respective pairwise games (constructed with Theorem 7 + Remark 1 instead of Apollonius circles), then Blue dominates the P3 game and can rendezvous at that point regardless of Red's actions; if no such point exists, Red dominates and Blue can rendezvous only if Red acts suboptimally. *Proof*: **none is given in the paper** — the statement is followed directly by the Fig. 8/Fig. 10 discussion (proof gap below). *Meaning*: the obstacle flips the motivating example's outcome: in Fig. 10 a portion of the protector/prey dominance boundary (solid) lies inside the prey's dominance region versus the predator (dashed), so the protector rescues the prey at any point of that arc and capture-first is impossible — whereas without the obstacle (Fig. 8) Red dominated. Obstacles can therefore change the winner of a multiplayer game of kind.

### 5.6 Game of degree with obstacles (Section 7, p. 10)

- **Result (C8, extension of Theorem 5)**: with time-to-capture payoff, the optimal capture point C is **the point on the (obstacle-modified) dominance boundary with the longest minimum-time path from E's initial location**; optimal play sends both P and E to C in minimum time. Unlike the obstacle-free case, P, E, C are not necessarily co-linear and paths are not single segments. Claim: if both play optimally, the dominance boundary at any intermediate time is **tangent to the initial boundary at C** (illustrated in Fig. 11(a) for the Fig. 3/5(a) geometry — C stays fixed and the moving boundaries remain tangent at C).
- **Off-optimal play (Fig. 11(b))**: if the pursuer rounds the obstacle in the wrong direction, the minmax capture point moves, E's optimal path curves, and the eventual capture point C′ lies **outside** the initial dominance boundary DB with capture later than the minmax time at C — the evader profits from the pursuer's error.

### Proof gaps and unclear steps
- **Theorem 9 is stated without proof** (p. 10). The intuitive argument — if both prey and protector dominate x versus the predator, both arrive at x strictly before the predator, so they can meet (wait) at x — also requires the unargued transitivity step that the prey can safely *transit* to x (survive en route), i.e., that no point of the prey's time-optimal path to x is predator-capturable earlier; this is the classical dominance-region guarantee but is not restated or proven here, and the pairwise-then-lifted logic for obstacles (where geodesics bend) is nowhere written out.
- **Section 7's game-of-degree claims are asserted, not proven**: the characterization of C and especially the tangency property of the moving dominance boundary with obstacles are supported only by Fig. 11's constructions, with no analytical derivation (pp. 10–11).
- Minor: Theorem 6's region (3) is called "neither player dominates", consistent with the strict ("before") definition of dominance on p. 1; boundary points are simultaneously reachable, and the payoff-relevant interpretation (equal-time interface) is clear but the paper never formally links region (3) to a game-of-kind outcome set.
- Minor: in Theorem 8 the existence check over θ is a continuous 1-D search; no algorithm, discretization guarantee, or complexity bound for locating θ (or for the full boundary construction) is provided.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated (Section 8.2, p. 10)**:
- Simple motion with instantaneous turns only; future work will use more complex dynamics (e.g., bounded turn radii) — although the isochrone-intersection method itself is claimed not specific to simple motion.
- Full information assumed; P3 extensions to partial information (e.g., prey location unknown to predator at start) and restricted prey–protector communication are open.

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. Theorem 9 is unproven (p. 10) — the pairwise-dominance-to-team-dominance lifting, including prey survivability en route under bent geodesics, is the paper's only major claim without even a proof sketch.
2. The game-of-degree solution with obstacles (Section 7) is an illustrated conjecture-style extension of Theorem 5; no proof of the tangency property or of C's optimality with obstacles (pp. 10–11, Fig. 11).
3. No computational complexity analysis: with many obstacle vertices the number of generating points/singular surfaces grows, and building the full boundary (or checking condition (13) over θ) is described only qualitatively via Hershberger & Suri (1999)/Mitchell (1993); no algorithm, pseudocode, discretization error bounds, or runtimes are given (pp. 3, 7).
4. Obstacles are static, known, and polygonal (or line segments); curved obstacles, moving obstacles, and uncertainty in the obstacle map are out of scope (p. 4, problem statement).
5. Dominance comparison presumes non-interacting trajectories (players may pass through each other's regions and, implicitly, through each other except at capture); this modeling choice is contrasted with crystal-growth Voronoi (p. 2) but its implications (e.g., no body-blocking by the protector) are not discussed for the P3 game.
6. The P3 solution decides the game of kind only (non-empty rendezvous set); the P3 game of degree (e.g., earliest guaranteed rendezvous time) is not addressed (Section 6).
7. The limaçon form (7) case taxonomy covers the final-segment geometry, but degenerate configurations (multiple equal-time routings, boundaries through vertices) are handled only through the tB = d = 0 exclusion remark (p. 6).
8. Only toy-scale examples (≤ 4 players, ≤ 3 obstacle vertices in figures) are analyzed; scalability in |vertices| × |players| is untested.

## 8. Citation Value
This is the canonical reference for **dominance regions in PE games with obstacles**: it establishes (i) the equal-time (isochrone-intersection) characterization of the dominance boundary, (ii) the obstacle-modified closed-form boundary pieces — Apollonius circle generalized to **limaçons** and vertex-centered circles indexed by final-segment start-time offsets (Theorem 7), and (iii) the singular-surface architecture (type-(p,u,p) lines and hyperbolic dispersal surfaces, Remark 1) that organizes the piecewise assembly. It is also the origin of the **P3 (Prey–Protector–Predator) rescue game** with a four-dimensionless-parameter solvability condition (Theorem 8) and an obstacle-driven outcome-flip result (Theorem 9). Cite it whenever an obstacle-coupled dominance-region construction, a shortest-path/Apollonius generalization (limaçon boundaries), asymmetric obstacle effects, or a defender-escorts-evader team game is invoked. Typical citation sentences: "Dominance regions for planar pursuit–evasion with polygonal obstacles were characterized as intersections of isochrone bundles, yielding limaçon-shaped boundary pieces that reduce to Apollonius circles when the obstacle is removed [Oyler et al., 2016]." Or: "In the Prey–Protector–Predator game, the blue team wins exactly when some point is dominated by both prey and protector against the predator; the presence of an obstacle can reverse the game's outcome [Oyler et al., 2016]." Caveats when building on it: Theorem 9 and the obstacle-bearing game-of-degree tangency claims are stated without proof, and no complexity analysis of the boundary construction is provided — useful hooks for follow-up theoretical work.
