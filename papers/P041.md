## 2. Research Background
Pursuit–evasion (PE) differential games formalize control, navigation, biological behavior and military confrontation problems; besides the two-side maximum principle and the Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs (HJI) equation, the geometric method — built around the evader's dominance region (Apollonius circle and its generalizations) — is a standard solution tool since Isaacs (1965) and Dutkevich & Petrosyan (1972). Information patterns are decisive in differential games: the non-anticipative pattern (Varaiya 1967; Roxin 1969; Elliott & Kalton 1972), under which a player's current control may depend only on the other player's past states and controls, is strictly more practical than the open-loop pattern, and state-feedback and time-delay strategies are special cases of it (Bardi–Dolcetta 1997; Cardaliaguet–Rainer 2018). In obstacle environments, HJI-based reachability (Margellos & Lygeros 2011; Fisac et al. 2015) handles PE games but suffers the curse of dimensionality for multi-player settings, motivating geometric approaches; however, dominance regions in the presence of obstacles had been studied almost exclusively by Oyler, Kabamba & Girard (2016), whose framework the authors read as implicitly anticipative (open-loop). The paper therefore asks whether the classical geometric dominance-region machinery survives when the pursuer is restricted to physically realizable, non-anticipative strategies.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap
**Problem.** Two point-mass players with Isaacs' simple motion in a connected closed planar domain $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ (the complement $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus X$ acts as obstacles); evader speed 1, pursuer speed $\alpha > 1$; capture radius $l \geq 0$ ($l = 0$ whenever obstacles exist). The evader's dominance region is defined via the **shortest path distance** $d_L$ on $X$ (Definition 2, p. 4). The core question (p. 2): *under what condition does the pursuer have a non-anticipative strategy that prevents the evader from leaving its initial dominance region before being captured, regardless of the evader's control?* Such a strategy is named a **PELIDR non-anticipative strategy** ("Preventing the Evader Leaving its Initial Dominance Region").

**Gap.** [Authors' claim, p. 2 and p. 3]: Oyler et al. (2016) studied dominance regions with obstacles but "did not emphasize the information pattern"; based on the whole article the authors believe Oyler's results hold under the (Stackelberg) open-loop pattern, where the pursuer may know the evader's future control — an anticipative strategy. [Verified from the present paper's own account only — Oyler et al. was not directly inspected for this archive.] The authors also state (p. 2) that Oyler showed the initial dominance region equals the evader's reachable region in the Stackelberg open-loop sense "though there was not a formal and rigorous proof". The implications drawn (p. 2, invoking Başar & Olsder 1995) are: (i) since any pursuer strategy becomes an open-loop control once the evader's control function is fixed, the evader can reach any point of its initial dominance region regardless of the information pattern; (ii) with anticipative strategies the pursuer can confine the evader to the initial dominance region. What was missing is the non-anticipative counterpart — i.e., whether confinement is achievable by a pursuer that cannot anticipate — and this paper fills that gap with a rigorous theory (Theorems 4, 5, 7, 14, 15/20), a separating counterexample (Example 13), and a target-defense application (Section 6).

## 4. Core Contributions
1. **Theory (definition + rigorous reachability proof)**: the evader's dominance region is defined by the shortest path distance $d_L$, and it is rigorously proved for the first time (per the authors) that the initial dominance region equals the evader's reachable region in the open-loop sense — Theorem 4 (no obstacles) and Theorem 5 (obstacles, $l=0$), with supporting Lemma 6.
2. **Theory (non-anticipative confinement, obstacle-free)**: existence of a PELIDR non-anticipative strategy without obstacles (Theorem 7), with an explicit uniform capture-time bound and a monotone containment of the moving dominance region inside the initial one; built on an angular comparison Lemma 8 on first-type Cartesian ovals. Remark 9 extends part (b) to convex domains.
3. **Theory (obstacles, negative + necessary result)**: a counterexample (Example 13) showing PELIDR non-anticipative strategies may fail to exist with obstacles, and a necessary condition for existence in general obstacle environments (Theorem 14), derived from a boundary-evolution equation (21)–(23).
4. **Theory (sufficient condition, single corner obstacle)**: for the domain with one corner obstacle, the condition $\|x_{p0}\| < \alpha\|x_{e0}\|$ (obstacle vertex outside the closure of the initial dominance region) guarantees a PELIDR non-anticipative strategy (Theorem 15, detailed as Theorem 20), proved via an auxiliary regularization $\delta^\epsilon$ and an ODE continuous-dependence argument; supported by Lemma 17, Proposition 18, Lemma 19, and an auxiliary curve $F(x_p,x_e)$ constructed in supplementary Appendix C.
5. **Application**: static target defense under the non-anticipative pattern — an iff winning condition without obstacles (eq. (48)) and a discussion of why sufficiency breaks down with obstacles (p. 13).
6. **Experiment**: none — the paper is purely theoretical/geometric (see Section 6 of this archive).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.0 Model, information structure, termination (Section 2, pp. 3–4)
- **Dynamics (eq. (1), p. 3)**: $\dot{x}_p(t) = \alpha u_p(t)$, $\dot{x}_e(t) = u_e(t)$, $x_p(t_0)=x_{p0}$, $x_e(t_0)=x_{e0}$; controls take values in $\partial B_2(0,1)$; admissible control sets $\mathcal{U}^p_{t_0}(x_{p0}), \mathcal{U}^e_{t_0}(x_{e_0}) \subseteq \mathcal{U}_{t_0} \triangleq \{u : [t_0,+\infty) \to \partial B_2(0,1) : u \text{ piecewise continuous}\}$, restricted so that players never enter $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus X$. Trajectories are continuous, piecewise smooth; sudden direction changes allowed.
- **Termination (p. 3)**: terminal set $\mathcal{T} = \{(x_p,x_e) : \|x_p - x_e\| \le l\}$; terminal time $t_f(t_0,x_0,u_p,u_e) = \inf\{t : x(t;t_0,x_0,u_p,u_e) \in \mathcal{T}\}$ (eq. (2)); $\inf\emptyset = +\infty$ ($t_f=+\infty$ means no capture).
- **Information structure (Definition 1, p. 4, eq. (3))**: a pursuer strategy is a map $\delta : \mathcal{U}^e_{t_0}(x_{e_0}) \to \mathcal{U}^p_{t_0}(x_{p0})$; it is **non-anticipative** if
  $$u_e(t) = u'_e(t) \ \text{a.e. } [t_0,t'] \;\Rightarrow\; \delta[u_e](t) = \delta[u'_e](t) \ \text{a.e. } [t_0,t'],$$
  for all $t' > t_0$; the set of all such maps is $\Delta_{t_0}(x_0)$.
- **Strategy generation (eqs. (4)–(5), p. 4)**: if $\gamma : \mathbb{R}^2\times\mathbb{R}^2\times\partial B_2(0,1) \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is continuous and locally Lipschitz in $x_p$, then $u_p(t) = \gamma(x_p(t), x_e(t), u_e(t))$ (4) generates a well-defined non-anticipative map $\delta_p[u_e](t) = \gamma(\xi_p(t;\gamma,u_e), x_e(t;u_e), u_e(t))$ (5), where $\xi_p$ solves the pursuer ODE. Note this generator requires the pursuer to know the evader's **current** control $u_e(t)$; the justification is deferred to the supplementary material (p. 4, end of Subsection 2.4).
- **Shortest path distance (p. 4)**: $d_L(x_1,x_2) \triangleq \inf\{L(r) : r \text{ continuous piecewise smooth in } X, r(a)=x_1, r(b)=x_2\}$; a metric on $X$ (Burago–Burago–Ivanov 2001, Example 2.2.2); a length-minimizing shortest path exists ($X$ closed, Theorem 2.5.23). Visibility, visible region $\mathcal{V}(x)$ defined p. 4.
- **Dominance regions (Definition 2, p. 4)**: without obstacles, $D_l(x_1,x_2) \triangleq \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \|x - x_1\| - \alpha\|x - x_2\| > l\}$ (boundary = first-type Cartesian oval with foci $x_1,x_2$ when $\|x_1-x_2\|>l$; degenerates to the Apollonius circle when $l=0$); with obstacles ($l=0$), $D(x_1,x_2) \triangleq \{x \in X : d_L(x,x_1) - \alpha d_L(x,x_2) > 0\}$.
- **Reachable region (Definition 3, p. 4)**: $\mathcal{R}_e(t_0,x_0) \triangleq \{x \in X : \exists u_e \in \mathcal{U}^e_{t_0}, \forall u_p \in \mathcal{U}^p_{t_0}, \exists t \in [t_0, t_f(u_p,u_e)) : x_e(t;t_0,x_{e_0},u_e) = x\}$ — i.e., points the evader can reach before capture against *every* pursuer control function; time-invariant, written $\mathcal{R}_e(x_0)$. Its complement (eq. (6), p. 5) is $\mathcal{R}_e(x_0)^c = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \forall u_e, \exists u_p, \forall t \in [t_0,t_f(u_p,u_e)) : x_e(t;u_e,x_{e_0}) \neq x\}$, where the $u_p$ may depend on the whole $u_e$ — an anticipative strategy; the game in (6) "can be seen as an open-loop Stackelberg game" (p. 5). This is precisely the gap Theorem 7 closes.
- **Cartesian ovals (p. 3)**: locus $|PS| + m|QS| = a$; first type ($m<-1$, $a \in [0,|PQ|)$): bounded, strictly convex, simple closed curve, interior a bounded strictly convex set, $P$ outer and $Q$ inner foci; $a=0$ gives the Apollonius circle. Second type ($m<-1$, $a \in (m|PQ|, 0)$): bounded, simple closed, **not** convex.

### 5.1 Dominance region = open-loop reachable region
- **Theorem 4 (p. 4)**: Without obstacles, $\mathcal{R}_e(x_{p0},x_{e0}) = D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$. *Proof*: not reproduced; "easy to prove by the convexity of Cartesian ovals of the first type"; a brief proof is cited to Yan, Shi & Zhong (2021). Analytic, global. Physical meaning: the classical lifeline/Apollonius region is exactly what the evader can reach in the open-loop sense, so no information pattern can shrink it from the evader's side.
- **Lemma 6 (p. 4)**: For $x_{p0} \neq x_{e0}$ and $x \in D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$, let $r^*_x(\cdot)$ be a shortest path from $x_{e0}$ to $x$; then $\{r^*_x(s) : s \in [a,b)\} \subseteq D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$. *Proof*: contradiction + metric triangle inequality; decisive chain (p. 4): $d_L(x,x_{p0}) \le d_L(r^*_x(s),x_{p0}) + d_L(r^*_x(s),x) < d_L(r^*_x(s),x_{p0}) + \alpha d_L(r^*_x(s),x) \le \alpha d_L(x,x_{e0})$, contradicting $x \in D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ (uses $\alpha > 1$ and $d_L(r^*_x(s),x_{e0}) + d_L(r^*_x(s),x) = d_L(x,x_{e0})$ on the shortest path).
- **Theorem 5 (p. 4–5)**: With obstacles and zero capture radius, $\mathcal{R}_e(x_{p0},x_{e0}) = D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$. *Proof* (two inclusions): define minimum arrival times $t_p(x) = t_0 + \tfrac{1}{\alpha} d_L(x_{p0},x)$, $t_e(x) = t_0 + d_L(x_{e0},x)$ (exact, since players move at constant speeds along shortest paths). (i) $D \subseteq \mathcal{R}_e$: the evader runs along $r^*_x$; by Lemma 6 the whole path stays in $D$, so $t_p(r^*_x(s)) > t_e(r^*_x(s))$ for all $s$ — the pursuer cannot stand on any point of the path at the moment the evader passes; hence the evader reaches $x$ before capture. (ii) $D^c \subseteq \mathcal{R}_e^c$: for an evader control $\bar u_e$ passing through $x \in D^c$, let $\tau^*$ = first exit time from $D$ and $x^* = x_e(\tau^*) \in \partial D$; by Definition 2, $t_p(x^*) = t_e(x^*)$ on the boundary; the pursuer goes to $x^*$ along the shortest path; if $t_e(x^*) = \tau^*$ then $t_f = \tau^*$; otherwise the pursuer "retraces the trajectory of the evader from the moment $t_e(x^*)$ and the position $x^*$" and captures at $t_f < \tau^*$; in both cases the evader never leaves $D$ before $t_f$, so it cannot reach $x$. Global, analytic/constructive. Physical meaning: with obstacles, dominance is measured in shortest-path metric, and the boundary of $D$ is exactly the capture-time-equal surface under obstacle-constrained routing.

### 5.2 Non-anticipative confinement without obstacles (Section 3, pp. 5–7)
- **Strategy (p. 5, Fig. 4)**: given $(x_p(t), x_e(t), u_e(t))$, draw the ray from $x_e(t)$ along $u_e(t)$; since $x_e$ is the inner focal point it lies in the interior of the strictly convex curve $\partial D_l(x_p(t),x_e(t))$, so the ray hits it at a unique point $x_c(x_p,x_e,u_e)$; set
  $$u_p(t) = \gamma(x_p,x_e,u_e) = \frac{x_c(x_p,x_e,u_e) - x_p}{\|x_c(x_p,x_e,u_e) - x_p\|},$$
  and let $\delta^*_{t_0,x_0}$ (abbrev. $\delta^*$) be the generated non-anticipative strategy via (5). This is a pursuit-to-the-intersection-point law: the pursuer always runs toward the point where the evader would exit its current dominance region.
- **Lemma 8 (p. 5–6, eq. (7)–(10))**: For $x_p, x_e \in \mathbb{R}^2$, $\|x_p - x_e\| > l$, and any $x_1, x_2 \in \partial D_l(x_p,x_e)$:
  $$\frac{(x_1 - x_p)\cdot(x_2 - x_p)}{\|x_1-x_p\|\|x_2-x_p\|} \;\ge\; \frac{(x_1 - x_e)\cdot(x_2 - x_e)}{\|x_1-x_e\|\|x_2-x_e\|},$$
  i.e., the visual angle subtended by any two boundary points at the pursuer is at most that at the evader. *Proof steps*: (1) define $\chi(x)$ = angle between $x - x_e$ and $x_p - x_e$, $\psi(x)$ = angle between $x - x_p$ and $x_p - x_e$; assume $\chi(x_2)-\chi(x_1) \in [0,\pi]$; (2) it suffices to show $|d\psi/d\chi| \le 1$ (7) on the whole oval, then integrate: $|\psi(x_2)-\psi(x_1)| \le \chi(x_2)-\chi(x_1)$, and cos-monotonicity gives the claim; (3) parametrize the oval: $r_1 = \|x - x_p\| = \alpha r_2 + l$, $r_2 = \|x - x_e\|$, $\sigma = \|x_p - x_e\|$; by the parametric representation of the Cartesian oval (Garcia 2021, Lemma 1) $\chi \leftrightarrow r_2$ is one-to-one on $[0,\pi]$ with $r_2 \in [\tfrac{\sigma-l}{\alpha+1}, \tfrac{\sigma-l}{\alpha-1}]$; (4) law of cosines for $\cos\chi, \cos\psi$; differentiate w.r.t. $r_2$; law of sines to get $d\psi/d\chi = \tfrac{\sin\chi}{\sin\psi}\tfrac{d\cos\psi/d r_2}{d\cos\chi/d r_2} = \tfrac{r_1}{r_2}\tfrac{f_1}{f_2}$ with $f_1 = \alpha r_1^2 r_2 - 2 r_1 r_2^2 + \alpha r_2^3 - \alpha\sigma^2 r_2$ (eq. (8)), $f_2 = r_1^3 - 2\alpha r_1^2 r_2 + r_1 r_2^2 - \sigma^2 r_1$ (eq. (9)); (5) substituting $r_2 = \tfrac{1}{\alpha}r_1 - \tfrac{l}{\alpha}$ into (9): $f_2 = -\tfrac{r_1}{\alpha^2}\left[(\alpha^2-1)(r_1-l)^2 + \alpha^2(\sigma^2 - l^2)\right] < 0$ for all $r_1 > 0$ (uses $\alpha>1$, $\sigma>l$) — decisive identity; (6) $|f_1/f_2| \le 1 \iff f_1 - f_2 \ge 0 \wedge f_1 + f_2 \le 0$ (eq. (10)); with $g_1 = f_1 + f_2 = l(\alpha^2-1)r_2^2 - 2\alpha(\sigma^2-l^2)r_2 - l(\sigma^2-l^2)$ (convex quadratic, evaluated negative at both endpoints, p. 6) and $g_2 = f_1 - f_2 = 2(\alpha^3-\alpha)r_2^3 + 3l(\alpha^2-1)r_2^2 + l(\sigma^2-l^2)$ (cubic with positive leading coefficient and non-negative remaining coefficients), so $g_1 < 0 < g_2$ on the whole admissible range, proving (10). [Presentation gap: the printed endpoint evaluations of $g_1$ (p. 6) appear as fractions with denominators $(\alpha\pm1)/(\sigma-l)$, whose algebra is garbled/typographical; the sign conclusion is nonetheless correct.]
- **Theorem 7 (p. 5, proof pp. 6–7)**: Without obstacles ($X = \mathbb{R}^2$), for initial data $(t_0, x_0)$ and the strategy $\delta^*$: **(a)** there exists $c > 0$ with $t_f(\delta^*[u_e], u_e) < c$ for all $u_e \in \mathcal{U}_{t_0}$; in fact the proof yields the explicit uniform bound
  $$t_f(\delta^*[u_e],u_e) \;\le\; t_0 + \frac{\|x_{p0} - x_{e0}\| - l}{\alpha - 1};$$
  **(b)** for all $u_e$ and all $t \in [t_0, t_f(\delta^*[u_e],u_e))$: $x_e(t;u_e) \in D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ — the evader never leaves its initial dominance region before capture. *Proof of (a)* (pp. 6): let $x_c(t)$ be the aim point; by the law of sines in triangle $(x_p, x_e, x_c)$ and the boundary relation $\|x_c - x_p\| = \alpha\|x_c - x_e\| + l$: $\tfrac{\sin\chi}{\sin\psi} = \tfrac{\|x_c - x_p\|}{\|x_c - x_e\|} \ge \alpha$, hence $\cos\psi \ge \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{1}{\alpha^2}\sin^2\chi}$; then
  $$\frac{d}{dt}\|x_p(t) - x_e(t)\| = -\alpha\cos\psi - \cos\chi \;\le\; f(\chi) := -\alpha\sqrt{1 - \tfrac{1}{\alpha^2}\sin^2\chi} - \cos\chi,$$
  with $f'(\chi) = \left(\tfrac{\cos\chi}{\sqrt{\alpha^2 - \sin^2\chi}} + 1\right)\sin\chi \ge 0$; since $f(\pi) = 1 - \alpha$, integration gives the bound. *Proof of (b)* (pp. 6–7): let $\phi(x,t) = \|x - x_p(t)\| - \alpha\|x - x_e(t)\| - l$ (eq. (11)); for an arbitrary $x_d \in \partial D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ draw the tangent $l(x_d)$; by convexity $D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ lies in the inner half-space $l(x_d)_{\text{in}} = \{x : \nabla_x\phi(x_d,0)\cdot(x - x_d) \ge 0\}$ (eq. (12)–(13)); solve the strictly convex QP $\min_{x}\ \nabla_x\phi(x_d,0)\cdot(x - x_d)$ over the moving set $D_l(x_p(t),x_e(t))$ (eq. (14)); unique minimizer $x_H(t)$ lies on the moving boundary, $\phi(x_H(t),t) = 0$ (15); KKT conditions (16)–(18) give $\nabla_x\phi(x_d,0) = \lambda\nabla_x\phi(x_H(t),t)$, $\lambda \ge 0$; differentiating (15) in $t$ (eq. (19)) and computing $\partial_t\phi(x_H(t),t)$ explicitly, Lemma 8 yields $\partial_t\phi(x_H(t),t) \le 0$ (20), hence $\nabla_x\phi(x_d,0)\cdot\dot{x}_H(t) \ge 0$ and $\nabla_x\phi(x_d,0)\cdot(x_H(t) - x_d) \ge 0$, i.e., the whole moving set is inside $l(x_d)_{\text{in}}$; intersecting over all $x_d \in \partial D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ and using Rockafellar (1970, Theorem 18.8), $D_l(x_p(t),x_e(t)) \subseteq D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$. Since $x_e(t)$ is the inner focus of the moving region, $x_e(t) \in D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$. Local-to-global: the tangent/support argument is pointwise in $x_d$ but concluded globally by intersection. Tools: KKT conditions, convex set separation (supporting hyperplanes), ODE comparison, trigonometry. Physical meaning: aiming at the evader's current "exit point" both shrinks the dominance region monotonically and guarantees capture in finite time bounded by the classical Apollonius time. The authors note (p. 5) this generalizes Dutkevich & Petrosyan (1972), who proved the capture property only when the evader moves along a straight line.
- **Remark 9 (p. 7)**: If $X$ is a convex subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$, Theorem 7(b) holds verbatim with $D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0}) \cap X$ in place of $D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$.

### 5.3 Obstacles: counterexample and necessary condition (Section 4, pp. 7–9)
- **Theorem 10 (p. 7, quoted)**: With a set of polygonal obstacles, shortest paths in $X$ are broken lines breaking at obstacle vertices (proof in Chein & Steinberg 1983; framework credited to Oyler et al. 2016). The authors note the statement extends to obstacles bounded by finite line segments, rays and lines.
- **Theorem 11 (p. 7, quoted)**: With polygonal obstacles, $\partial D(x_p,x_e)$ consists of three curve types: (a) Cartesian ovals and (b) Apollonius circles with foci among the pursuer position, the evader position, and the obstacle vertices; (c) circles centered at obstacle vertices (from Oyler et al. 2016, Theorem 7, plus the discussion after its proof).
- **Proposition 12 (p. 7)**: If $d_L$ is continuously differentiable near $(x_1,x_2)$, then $\|\partial_1 d_L(x_1,x_2)\| = \|\partial_2 d_L(x_1,x_2)\| = 1$ (unit tangent vectors of the shortest path at its endpoints). Proof in supplementary Appendix B (not in this PDF).
- **Example 13 (p. 8, counterexample; Fig. 8)**: Corner obstacle with vertex at the origin $O$, each edge making $5°$ with the $x$-axis; $x_{p0} = (4,5)$ (point $P$), $x_{e0} = (2,-1)$ (point $E$), $\alpha = 1.5$, $l = 0$. By Theorems 10–11, $\partial D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ splits into curve $AB$ (part of a Cartesian oval), $BC$ (part of an Apollonius circle) and $CD$ (another Cartesian oval), where $A,D$ are the intersections of $\partial D$ with the two obstacle edges, $B$ the intersection with the extension line of $EO$, $C$ that with the extension line of $PO$. On curve $AB$ the shortest path $E \to C_i$ ($i=1,2$, two points on $AB$) is the broken line $E$–$O$–$C_i$, while $P \to C_i$ is the straight segment $PC_i$. Suppose a PELIDR non-anticipative strategy exists. If the evader moves along $EOC_1$ (control $u_e$), the pursuer "must move to $C_1$ directly" to prevent the evader entering $D^c$, so its velocity direction is $\overrightarrow{PC_1}$ on $[t_0, t_0+\Delta t)$; moving along $EOC_2$ ($u'_e$) forces direction $\overrightarrow{PC_2}$ on the same interval. But $u_e(t) = u'_e(t)$ on $[t_0, t_0+\Delta t)$ (both along $\overrightarrow{EO}$), while $\delta[u_e] \ne \delta[u'_e]$ there — violating non-anticipativity (3). Hence **no PELIDR non-anticipative strategy exists** for this instance. *Proof gap (critical observation)*: the step "the pursuer must move to $C_1$ directly" is asserted on geometric grounds (any other heading lets the evader slip past the vertex) but is not formalized as a lemma; the argument is a heuristic necessity claim embedded in the contradiction.
- **Boundary evolution equation (p. 8, eqs. (21)–(23))**: with $\phi(x,t) \triangleq d_L(x, x_p(t)) - \alpha d_L(x, x_e(t))$ and $d_L$ differentiable,
  $$\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(x,t) = \partial_2 d_L(x, x_p(t))\cdot\dot{x}_p(t) - \alpha\,\partial_2 d_L(x, x_e(t))\cdot\dot{x}_e(t) \quad (21);$$
  if $\partial D$ has a smooth parametrization $C(s,t)$, then $\phi(C(s,t),t) = 0$ implies (22) $\nabla_x\phi\cdot\partial_t C + \partial_t\phi = 0$, hence
  $$\nabla_x\phi(C(s,t),t)\cdot\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}(s,t) = \alpha\,\partial_2 d_L(C,x_e)\cdot\dot{x}_e - \partial_2 d_L(C,x_p)\cdot\dot{x}_p \quad (23).$$
  Geometric reading (p. 8): $\nabla_x\phi$ is the inner normal of $\partial D$; if the left side of (23) is negative for all $s$, the boundary **expands outward**; the right side is the difference of the projections of the players' velocities onto the shortest paths toward the boundary point. (23) links dominance-region motion to player motion and is the engine behind Theorem 14.
- **Theorem 14 (p. 8, eq. (24))**: Let $x_{c1}, x_{c2} \in \partial D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ and assume $d_L$ is continuously differentiable near $(x_{c1},x_{p0})$ and $(x_{c2},x_{p0})$. If there exists a (pursuer) strategy $\delta_p$ with $x_e(t;t_0,x_{e0},u_e) \in D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ for all $u_e$ and all $t \in [t_0, t_f(\delta_p[u_e],u_e))$ — i.e., a PELIDR strategy — then
  $$\partial_2 d_L(x_{c1},x_{p0})\cdot\partial_2 d_L(x_{c2},x_{p0}) \;-\; \partial_2 d_L(x_{c1},x_{e0})\cdot\partial_2 d_L(x_{c2},x_{e0}) \;\ge\; 0 \quad (24).$$
  *Proof*: contradiction. Choose $u_e$ with $u_e(t_0) = -\partial_2 d_L(x_{c1},x_{e0})$. If $\delta_p[u_e](t_0) \ne -\partial_2 d_L(x_{c1},x_{p0})$, then (26): $\tfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(x_{c1},t_0) = \alpha\left(\partial_2 d_L(x_{c1},x_{p0})\cdot\delta_p[u_e](t_0) + 1\right) > 0$; otherwise (i.e., the pursuer aligns with $-\partial_2 d_L(x_{c1},x_{p0})$), assumption (25) (negation of (24)) makes (27) hold at $x_{c2}$: $\tfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(x_{c2},t_0) > 0$. In either case Taylor expansion of $\phi$ at $(x_{c_i},t_0)$ in $t$ gives $\phi(x_{c_i}, t_0+\Delta t) > 0$ for small $\Delta t$, i.e., $x_{c_i} \in D(x_p(t_0+\Delta t), x_e(t_0+\Delta t))$ for some boundary point while that point is outside the initial region; then there exists $x \in D(x_{p0},x_{e0})^c \cap D(x_p(t_0+\Delta t), x_e(t_0+\Delta t))$, and by Theorem 5 the evader reaches $x$ before capture from the shifted state — contradicting the PELIDR property. Tools: first-order boundary sensitivity (23), Taylor expansion, Theorem 5. Local, analytic. Physical meaning: a PELIDR strategy can exist only if the initial dominance-region boundary is "pursuer-convex" in the sense that the pursuer's unit tangents toward any two boundary points are sufficiently aligned (inner product non-dominated by the evader's pair); the counterexample instance violates (24) along curve $AB$, where tangents fan out around the vertex. [Minor notation: the strategy class in the statement is printed "$\exists\delta_p \in \Delta_p$", with $\Delta_p$ not formally defined; presumably $\Delta_{t_0}(x_0)$.]

### 5.4 Single corner obstacle: sufficient condition (Section 5, pp. 9–12)
- **Domain (p. 9)**: $X = \{(\rho\cos\theta, \rho\sin\theta) : \rho \ge 0,\ \theta \in [\theta_0, 2\pi - \theta_0]\}$, $\theta_0 \in (0,\pi/2)$ — the plane minus a wedge (two rays from the origin); "similar with the 'configuration space' in Zou and Bhattacharya (2018)". The authors argue (p. 9) the single-corner case is the simplest obstacle case and that general cases "can be locally approximated" by it — asserted, with the connection left to future work.
- **Explicit metric (eq. (28), p. 9)**: for $x_i = (\rho_i\cos\theta_i, \rho_i\sin\theta_i)$,
  $$d_L(x_1,x_2) = \begin{cases} \|x_1 - x_2\|, & |\theta_1 - \theta_2| \le \pi \\ \|x_1\| + \|x_2\|, & |\theta_1 - \theta_2| > \pi \end{cases},$$
  and the visible region is $\mathcal{V}(x_1) = \{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta) \in X : |\theta - \theta_1| \le \pi\}$ (in the upper half plane, $\theta \in (\theta_0, \theta_1 + \pi)$).
- **Boundary decomposition (pp. 9, eqs. (29)–(31))**: under $\|x_p\| < \alpha\|x_e\|$, Lemma 6 implies the shortest path from $x_e$ to any $x \in \partial D(x_p,x_e)$ avoids the vertex, hence is a segment; each boundary point satisfies either (29) $\|x - x_p\| - \alpha\|x - x_e\| = 0$ (Apollonius circle, foci $x_p,x_e$) or (30) $\|x\| - \alpha\|x - x_e\| = -\|x_p\|$ (second-type Cartesian oval, foci origin and $x_e$); both iff $x, x_p, O$ are collinear. With $\mathcal{A}(x_1,x_2) \triangleq \{x : \|x-x_1\| - \alpha\|x-x_2\| > 0\}$ and $\mathcal{C}(x_1,x_2) \triangleq \{x : \|x\| - \alpha\|x-x_2\| > -\|x_1\|\}$,
  $$\partial D(x_p,x_e) \subseteq \partial\mathcal{A}(x_p,x_e) \cup \partial\mathcal{C}(x_p,x_e) \quad (31).$$
  An auxiliary set $F(x_p,x_e) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is defined (supplementary Appendix C) whose boundary $\partial F$ satisfies: (a) $\partial F \subseteq \partial\mathcal{A} \cup \partial\mathcal{C}$; (b) $\partial D \subseteq \partial F$; (c) every ray from $x_e$ meets $\partial F$ exactly once; (d) $\partial F$ is bounded, continuously differentiable, simple and closed.
- **Strategy (p. 10)**: $x_c = x_c(x_p,x_e,u_e)$ = unique intersection of the ray from $x_e$ along $u_e$ with $\partial F(x_p,x_e)$; the pursuit law is piecewise:
  $$\gamma^*(x_p,x_e,u_e) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x_c - x_p}{\|x_c - x_p\|}, & x_c \in \partial\mathcal{A}(x_p,x_e) \\[2mm] -\dfrac{x_p}{\|x_p\|}, & x_c \in \partial\mathcal{C}(x_p,x_e) \end{cases}$$
  (toward the exit point, or directly away from the vertex when the exit point lies on the vertex-hugging oval part); $\delta^*_{t_0,x_0}$ generated as in (5). **Auxiliary strategy**: for $\epsilon > 0$, $\gamma^\epsilon = \gamma^* - \epsilon\,\partial_1 d_L(x_p,x_e)$, generating $\delta^\epsilon_{t_0,x_0}$ mapping into $\{u : [t_0,+\infty) \to B_2(0, 1+\epsilon)\}$ — a relaxed (slightly faster-than-physical) non-anticipative strategy used purely as a proof device.
- **Lemma 17 (p. 10, eq. (32))**: When $\|x_p\| < \alpha\|x_e\|$, for all $x \in \partial D(x_p,x_e)$ and $u_e \in \partial B_2(0,1)$:
  $$\partial_2 d_L(x,x_e)\cdot u_e - \partial_2 d_L(x,x_p)\cdot\gamma^*(x_p,x_e,u_e) \;\ge\; 0 \quad (32),$$
  i.e., switching $\gamma^*$ never lets $\phi$ increase along the boundary. *Proof*: reduces to (33): for all $x_1,x_2 \in \partial F$, $\gamma^*(x_p,x_e,\tfrac{x_1-x_e}{\|x_1-x_e\|})\cdot\gamma^*(x_p,x_e,\tfrac{x_2-x_e}{\|x_2-x_e\|}) - \tfrac{(x_1-x_e)\cdot(x_2-x_e)}{\|x_1-x_e\|\|x_2-x_e\|} \ge 0$; proved exactly as Lemma 8 by establishing $|d\psi/d\chi| \le 1$ (34) on $\partial F$: on $\partial\mathcal{A}$ this holds by Lemma 8; on $\partial\mathcal{C}$, $\psi$ is invariant ($\gamma^* = -x_p/\|x_p\|$ constant) while $\chi$ varies, so $d\psi/d\chi = 0$.
- **Proposition 18 (p. 10, eq. (35))**: When $\|x_p\| < \alpha\|x_e\|$, for all $x \in \partial D(x_p,x_e)$: $\partial_1 d_L(x_p,x_e)\cdot\partial_2 d_L(x,x_p) > 0$. Proof in supplementary Appendix D (not in this PDF).
- **Lemma 19 (pp. 10–11)**: If $\|x_{p0}\| < \alpha\|x_{e0}\|$ and the pursuer uses $\delta^\epsilon_{t_0,x_0}$ ($\epsilon > 0$), then for all $u_e$ and $t \in [t_0, t_f(\delta^\epsilon[u_e],u_e))$: $D(x_p(t),x_e(t)) \subseteq D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$. *Proof*: contradiction. Let $\tau^*$ be the first time some point leaves the initial region; by continuity first $D(x_p(\tau^*),x_e(\tau^*)) \subseteq D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$; then pick sequences $\{x_n\} \subseteq X$, $\{t_n\} \subseteq (\tau^*,+\infty)$ with $x_n \in D(x_p(t_n),x_e(t_n))$, $x_n \notin D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$, $t_n \to \tau^*$ (eqs. (36)–(38)); boundedness gives a convergent subsequence $x^* = \lim x_n$ (39); passing to limits yields (40)–(41): $d_L(x^*,x_p(\tau^*)) - \alpha d_L(x^*,x_e(\tau^*)) \ge 0$ and $d_L(x^*,x_{p0}) - \alpha d_L(x^*,x_{e0}) \le 0$; combined with the set inclusions (42)–(43) both quantities equal $0$, so
  $$x^* \in \partial D(x_{p0},x_{e0}) \cap \partial D(x_p(\tau^*),x_e(\tau^*)).$$
  Now $\tfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(x^*,\tau^*) = \alpha\,\partial_2 d_L(x^*,x_p(\tau^*))\cdot\gamma(\cdot) - \epsilon\alpha\,\partial_2 d_L(x^*,x_p(\tau^*))\cdot\partial_1 d_L(x_p(\tau^*),x_e(\tau^*)) - \alpha\,\partial_2 d_L(x^*,x_e(\tau^*))\cdot u_e(\tau^*) < 0$ — **strictly** negative because the $-\epsilon\,\partial_1 d_L$ term combined with Lemma 17 (non-positivity of the $\gamma^*$ part) and Proposition 18 (strict sign of $\partial_1 d_L\cdot\partial_2 d_L$) dominates; continuity gives $\partial_t\phi < -M$ on a neighborhood, and the Cauchy mean value theorem gives $\phi(x,t) < 0$ for $x \in B_2(x^*,\delta_3) \cap D(x_p(\tau^*),x_e(\tau^*))^c$, $t \in (\tau^*, \tau^* + \delta_3)$ — contradicting (36)–(39). Tools: compactness/subsequences, uniform strict decrease, Cauchy MVT.
- **Theorem 20 = detailed statement of Theorem 15 (pp. 11–12)**: In the single-corner-obstacle setting, if $\|x_{p0}\| < \alpha\|x_{e0}\|$ and the pursuer uses $\delta^*_{t_0,x_0}$: **(a)** for all $u_e \in \mathcal{U}^e_{t_0}(x_{e0})$ and $t \in [t_0, t_f(\delta^*[u_e],u_e))$: $x_e(t) \in D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$; **(b)** there is a constant $c$ with $t_f(\delta^*[u_e],u_e) - t_0 < c$ for all $u_e$. *Proof of (a)*: contradiction with the same $\tau^*$-sequence machinery as Lemma 19; the pursuer runs $\delta^*$ on $[t_0,\tau^*)$ and $\delta^\epsilon_{\tau^*,x(\tau^*)}$ after $\tau^*$; by the continuous dependence theory of ODEs (Hale 2009; Hsieh & Sibuya 1999), $\lim_{\epsilon\to 0} x_p^\epsilon(t) = x_p(t)$ on $(\tau^*, \tau^*+\Delta t)$; for $x \in D(x_{p0},x_{e0})^c$, Lemma 19 gives $x \notin D(x_p^\epsilon(t), x_e(t))$ for every $\epsilon$, and the limit $\epsilon \to 0$ yields $x \notin D(x_p(t),x_e(t))$ — contradicting (44)–(47). *Proof of (b)*: $\tfrac{d}{dt} d_L(x_p(t),x_e(t)) = \alpha\,\partial_1 d_L\cdot\gamma^* + \partial_2 d_L\cdot u_e$, bounded case-by-case by $1 - \alpha$ under $\|x_p(t)\| < \alpha\|x_e(t)\|$ (maintained by (a)) through **four cases**: Case 1 ($x_c \in \partial\mathcal{A}$, $x_p,x_e$ mutually visible): reuse Theorem 7(a). Case 2 ($x_c \in \partial\mathcal{A}$, not visible; Fig. 10): with corner-shifted angles $\psi_0, \chi_0$, $\tfrac{d}{dt} d_L = -\alpha\cos(\psi - \psi_0) - \cos(\chi - \chi_0) \le -\alpha\cos\psi - \cos\chi \le 1 - \alpha$. Case 3 ($x_c \in \partial\mathcal{C}$, visible; Fig. 11): $\partial_1 d_L = \tfrac{x_p - x_e}{\|x_p - x_e\|}$, $\gamma^* = -\tfrac{x_p}{\|x_p\|}$, $\partial_2 d_L = \tfrac{x_e - x_p}{\|x_e - x_p\|}$; the line through $x_p$ and the origin meets $\partial F$ at two points of $\partial\mathcal{A}$; with $x'$ the farther one, $\tfrac{x' - x_p}{\|x' - x_p\|} = -\tfrac{x_p}{\|x_p\|}$ and $\tfrac{x_e - x_p}{\|x_e-x_p\|}\cdot\tfrac{x' - x_e}{\|x' - x_e\|} \ge \tfrac{x_e - x_p}{\|x_e-x_p\|}\cdot\tfrac{x_c - x_e}{\|x_c - x_e\|}$; chaining gives $\tfrac{d}{dt} d_L \le 1 - \alpha$. Case 4 ($x_c \in \partial\mathcal{C}$, not visible): $\partial_1 d_L = -\partial_2 d_L = -\gamma^* = \tfrac{x_p}{\|x_p\|}$, so $\tfrac{d}{dt} d_L = -\alpha + \partial_2 d_L\cdot u_e \le 1 - \alpha$ (since $\partial_2 d_L \cdot u_e \le 1$). Summing, $t_f(\delta^*[u_e],u_e) \le t_0 + \tfrac{\|x_{p0} - x_{e0}\| - l}{\alpha - 1}$ — as printed, with the $l$ term retained although $l = 0$ in this section (apparent typo carried over from Theorem 7). Tools: ODE continuous dependence, four-case trigonometric comparison, re-use of Theorem 7(a).
- **Remark 16 (p. 9)**: the condition $\|x_{p0}\| < \alpha\|x_{e0}\|$ (vertex outside $\overline{D(x_{p0},x_{e0})}$) also implies the pursuer reaches the obstacle vertex before the evader does.

### 5.5 Application to static target defense (Section 6, pp. 12–13)
A target region $\mathcal{T} \subseteq X$ is added; the evader starts outside $\mathcal{T}$; the question is whether the pursuer has a non-anticipative strategy capturing the evader before it reaches $\mathcal{T}$. **Without obstacles** the problem "is perfectly solved": such a strategy exists **if and only if**
$$D(x_{p0},x_{e0}) \cap \mathcal{T} = \emptyset \quad (48)$$
(as printed; per Theorems 4 and 7 the object in the obstacle-free case is $D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ — see critical observations), necessity coming from Theorem 4 (the evader reaches any point of its dominance region against any pursuer behavior) and sufficiency from Theorem 7(b) (confinement) plus (a) (finite capture). **With obstacles**, the printed statement is: $D_l(x_{p0},x_{e0}) \cap \mathcal{T} = \emptyset$ is "sufficient but not necessary", sufficiency "obtained from Theorem 5"; non-necessity is argued by Example 13 with $\mathcal{T} = D(x_{p0},x_{e0})^c$: then (48) holds, yet no winning non-anticipative strategy exists (as established in Example 13). Note the D/D$_l$ notation is interchanged between the two paragraphs relative to Sections 2–5 (see Section 7 of this archive).

### 5.6 Summary of the non-anticipative information structure
The paper works with Elliott–Kalton-style non-anticipative strategies (Definition 1, eq. (3)): the pursuer's answer to the evader's control function on $[t_0,t]$ may depend only on that function's restriction to $[t_0,t]$. All winning statements are of the form "there exists $\delta \in \Delta_{t_0}(x_0)$ such that for every $u_e$, [confinement/capture] holds", with the adversary free to pick $u_e$ (possibly discontinuous, piecewise continuous). Dominance regions are characterized **geometrically**: as sublevel sets of a weighted distance difference ($\|x-x_1\| - \alpha\|x-x_2\| > l$ or $d_L$-version), with boundaries made of first/second-type Cartesian ovals, Apollonius circles, and vertex-centered circles (Theorem 11), and their time evolution tracked through the normal velocity equation (23) rather than through HJI equations.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues
**Author-stated (p. 13, Conclusion)**:
- "the sufficient condition is derived in an overly special case" (single corner obstacle); general obstacle configurations are future work.
- Geometric methods could extend to richer dynamics, e.g., Dubins cars (Yan et al. 2023, 2024), and transferring these strategies to cases with obstacles is open.

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. **D/D$_l$ notation slip in Section 6 (p. 13)**: the iff condition (48) for the obstacle-*free* case is printed with $D(x_{p0},x_{e0})$ although Theorems 4/7 concern $D_l$; the obstacle paragraph then prints $D_l \cap \mathcal{T} = \emptyset$ as sufficient "from Theorem 5", which concerns the shortest-path region $D$. Read literally with $D$, the sufficiency claim would be contradicted by the authors' own Example 13 (there $\mathcal{T} = D^c$ gives $D \cap \mathcal{T} = \emptyset$ with no winning strategy); the intended reading presumably interchanges the two symbols. Users of this result must reconstruct the intended statement.
2. **Heuristic step in Example 13 (p. 8)**: the necessity of "the pursuer must move to $C_i$ directly" is asserted geometrically, not proven; the non-existence argument rests on this unformalized claim (proof gap).
3. **Current-control knowledge**: the strategy generator (4)–(5) uses the evader's instantaneous control $u_e(t)$; the practical justification is deferred to the supplementary material (p. 4), so the main text alone does not establish implementability without that assumption.
4. **Local-approximation claim (p. 9)**: "general cases can be locally approximated as the case with a single corner obstacle" is stated without proof or formal statement; the bridge to multi-corner/polygonal environments is entirely future work.
5. **Differentiability restrictions**: Theorem 14 requires $d_L$ to be $C^1$ near $(x_{ci},x_{p0})$; $d_L$ is generally nonsmooth at cut loci and vertex shadow boundaries, so the necessary condition covers only smooth boundary patches; Theorems 10–11 show boundaries are typically piecewise curves assembled at vertices.
6. **Zero capture radius with obstacles (p. 3)** and constant speeds / Isaacs simple motion only; no inertia, no sensing constraints, no bounded control magnitude beyond unit speed.
7. **Typos observed (as printed)**: p. 4, $\delta_p$ described as a map "from $\mathcal{U}^e_{t_0}(x_{p0})$ to $\mathcal{U}^p_{t_0}(x_{e_0})$" (superscript/subscript swapped; Definition 1 has the correct orientation); p. 9, "(ρ1, θ1), (ρ2, θ2) denote the polar coordinates of x2, x2" (should be $x_1, x_2$); p. 12, Case 4 expression "$\partial_2 d_L(x_p(t), x_e(t), )$" carries a stray comma; Theorem 20(b)'s final bound keeps the "$-l$" term though $l=0$ in Section 5; p. 6, the $g_1$ endpoint evaluations are printed as non-transparent fractions (denominators $(\alpha\pm1)/(\sigma-l)$).
8. **No HJI connection**: unlike Fu & Liu (2023) and Lee & Bakolas (2024) cited in the introduction, this paper does not verify the geometric strategies against the Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs PDE; optimality (beyond qualitative winning) is not addressed.

## 8. Citation Value
This paper is the foundational reference for **dominance-region-based geometric methods under a non-anticipative (Elliott–Kalton) information pattern in obstacle environments**. It occupies a unique niche: it (i) rigorously justifies the long-assumed identity between the evader's shortest-path dominance region and its open-loop reachable region (Theorems 4–5), (ii) proves that the classical "aim at the exit point" strategy is a valid non-anticipative PELIDR strategy without obstacles with the Apollonius capture-time bound (Theorem 7), (iii) delimits the method with an explicit counterexample and a differential necessary condition (Example 13, Theorem 14) showing that obstacles can break non-anticipative confinement, and (iv) gives the first sufficient condition for confinement in the canonical single-corner-obstacle domain (Theorem 15/20) together with an iff winning criterion for obstacle-free static target defense (eq. (48)). In a future IEEE Transactions introduction it serves as the definitive citation that the geometric/Apollonius machinery is information-pattern-sensitive: typical citation sentences — "Although the evader's dominance region coincides with its open-loop reachable region even in the presence of obstacles [Huang et al. 2025], a non-anticipative strategy confining the evader to this region is guaranteed to exist only in obstacle-free environments, or under the vertex-outside condition $\|x_{p0}\| < \alpha\|x_{e0}\|$ for a single corner obstacle [Huang et al. 2025, Thms. 7, 15]." and "Huang et al. [2025] showed via a counterexample that polygonal obstacles can destroy the existence of non-anticipative dominance-preserving pursuit strategies, and derived a boundary-sensitivity necessary condition (their eq. (24)) for such strategies to exist."
