## 2. Research Background

Pursuit-evasion is ubiquitous in nature and is studied across mathematics, engineering, and biology; it is frequently cast as a differential game (Friedman 2013; Chen et al. 2016; Lopez et al. 2019; Yan et al. 2022; Zhou & Chen 2024), and two-player optimal strategies have also been obtained geometrically (Getz & Pachter 1981; Garcia et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2016) — but those strategies are hard to extend to multiple agents with nonlinear dynamics (p. 1). In nature the pursuer (predator) is typically faster but less agile: it has a larger turning radius when moving fast, whereas the prey has superior maneuverability, which is the prey's critical survival asset (Wilson et al. 2013) (p. 1). Classic maneuverability analyses (Howland 1974; Webb 1983) rested on two restrictive assumptions — both animals initially moving along the same straight line, and turning radius independent of (in Howland 1974 even constant with respect to) velocity; Weihs & Webb (1984) analyzed a constant escape angle; and the Homicidal Chauffeur line (Patsko & Turova 2004; Bopardikar et al. 2009) assumes constant speeds for both players — all unrealistic, e.g., cheetahs decelerate sharply before and during turns (Wilson et al. 2013) (p. 2). The paper deliberately abandons the differential-game formulation and instead builds a bio-inspired *dynamic control* framework: both animals/robots are unicycles with linear and angular velocity as inputs, subject to velocity bounds and to the natural anti-correlation between angular velocity and linear velocity (turning radius grows with speed). The goal is a framework that "broadly approximates general chasing and escape phenomena in nature" and discloses "clues of natural laws" (p. 2), validated by replicating measured animal chase behavior.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem** [authors' statement, p. 2–3]: design effective pursuit and escape strategies for unicycle robots that mimic natural predator–prey chasing; specifically, answer (i) how to model the quick-turning maneuvers observed in hunts, (ii) what strategy an evader can use to outmaneuver a faster pursuer, and (iii) which factors decide escape vs capture and how. The behavioral template (Sec. 2, p. 2): the pursuer runs directly toward the evader as fast as possible, the evader flees straight away, and once the inter-agent distance shrinks to an alert threshold ε₁ > 0 the evader suddenly turns left or right.

**Gap** [authors' claims, p. 1–2]: (1) *Model precision* — linear models (Li 2016; Li et al. 2023) cannot describe rigid-body animals/robots with velocity and turning-radius limits, motivating the unicycle model with both velocity constraints; (2) *Relaxing unrealistic assumptions* — removing the collinear-initial-motion and velocity-independent/constant turning-radius assumptions of Howland 1974, Webb 1983, Weihs & Webb 1984 and the constant-speed assumption of Bopardikar et al. 2009 and Patsko & Turova 2004; (3) *Maneuverability impact* — demonstrating quantitatively how centripetal acceleration, tangential acceleration, alert distance, and initial conditions decide the outcome even when the pursuer is faster; (4) *Group dynamics* — none of the above works handles multiple pursuers and evaders, whereas natural chases often involve groups; the paper adds aggregation-based cooperative/selfish escape and a predator target-changing mechanism motivated by Lee et al. 2006, Li & Yang 2024, Jackson et al. 2006. [Verified as stated in the paper's introduction; these are characterizations of the cited literature, not re-verified against those sources. The relation to the closest prior work is explicit: Li (2016, IEEE TAC 62(1):451–457) proposed a similar Alert-Turn algorithm but on linear models and only for one-pursuer-one-evader (p. 2).] Notably, the paper formulates **no differential game**: no cost functional, no value function, no barrier; the analysis is control-law-based (finite-time convergence, a capture sufficiency theorem, and parameter studies).

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Dynamic framework (modeling)**: both players modeled as unicycles (Eq. (1)) with constraints 0 ≤ v ≤ V^max, w ≤ W^max (Eq. (2)) and the predator–prey asymmetry V_p^max > V_e^max, W_p^max < W_e^max (Eq. (3)); angular velocity is additionally bounded by the centripetal limit r·v⁻¹ (turning radius r/v² grows with speed), encoding the empirically observed anti-correlation between speed and agility (Wilson et al. 2013).
2. **Alert-Turn escape/pursuit strategy (algorithm)**: Algorithm 1 partitions the encounter into a long-distance phase (straight flee/chase with finite-time heading alignment, Eqs. (4)–(6)) and a short-distance phase (decelerate-then-accelerate sharp turn with distance-dependent angular velocity, Eqs. (7)–(8)), plus a stop condition at capture radius ε₂.
3. **Two-level analysis (theory)**: single-run escape/capture patterns with mechanistic explanations (Sec. 3.3, Figs. 1–2); parameter-range studies over centripetal acceleration r, tangential acceleration a, initial distance/angle (∆d₀, ∆θ₀), and alert distance ε₁ (Sec. 3.4, Figs. 3–5) yielding prescriptive "natural law" conclusions. Formal results: Theorem 1 (finite-time heading alignment), Theorem 2 (sufficient conditions for capture within one pursuer turning window π/(2W_p^max)), Theorem 3 (aggregation convergence of the evader swarm).
4. **Multi-agent extension (modeling/algorithm)**: Eqs. (9)–(18) — main-group evaders blend aggregation (swarm at configurable clearance d_ic^des, with pursuer-repelling heading bias) and escape commands weighted by the "selfish parameter" α ∈ [0,1] producing cooperative, selfish, or mixed escape patterns; isolated evaders blend rejoining and escape via a decreasing weight β(∆d_pi); pursuers chase with decelerate-to-align logic and inter-pursuer repulsion for collision avoidance (Eq. (16)); a target-changing mechanism reassigns targets every ∆t̄ based on predicted capture times and threshold pt.
5. **Quantified multi-agent behavior (analysis)**: dispersion degree vs α (Fig. 8, approximately exponential) and capture-rate statistics over (∆t̄, pt) (Fig. 9: capture predominantly for ∆t̄ ∈ [0,1], pt ∈ [0.28, 0.67] — frequent target detection but neither too frequent nor too infrequent target changes).
6. **Empirical validation (experiment)**: replication of five real animal chase videos (two dogs vs one rabbit; one cheetah vs one deer in three scenarios) using YOLOv8 detection at 10 fps, comparing relative-distance profiles, turning times, and capture times (Fig. 10 and supplementary videos).

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.1 Dynamics, information structure, termination (Sec. 3.1, pp. 2–3)

- **Dynamics (Eq. (1))**: unicycle models — ẋ_p = v_p cos θ_p, ẏ_p = v_p sin θ_p, θ̇_p = w_p; ẋ_e = v_e cos θ_e, ẏ_e = v_e sin θ_e, θ̇_e = w_e. Position col(x,y) := [x y]^T ∈ ℝ²; control u = col(v, w) ∈ ℝ². Full-state information: each player knows the relative position (bearing ∆θ_pe = atan((y_e−y_p)/(x_e−x_p)) is the orientation of the vector from pursuer to evader); no noise/delay modeled.
- **Constraints (Eq. (2))**: 0 ≤ v_p(t) ≤ V_p^max, 0 ≤ v_e(t) ≤ V_e^max (no reverse motion), w_p(t) ≤ W_p^max, w_e(t) ≤ W_e^max — printed without absolute-value bars on w (the intended bound is |w| ≤ W^max, cf. (4), (6), (8)).
- **Role asymmetry (Eq. (3))**: V_p^max > V_e^max, W_p^max < W_e^max — the predator is faster, the prey more agile.
- **Maneuverability model**: angular velocity commands are bounded by min(r v⁻¹, W^max), where r is the centripetal acceleration and r/v is the inverse turning radius; this makes the achievable turn rate decrease with speed — the key deviation from constant-speed/classic analyses.
- **Termination/winning condition**: Algorithm 1 stops all motion when ∆d_pe(t) < ε₂ (capture, radius ε₂). t_d = capture time (t_d = ∞ if never captured; by convention t_d := t_f if not captured by the deadline t_f) (Sec. 3.3, p. 4). No cost functional is defined anywhere — this is not an optimal-control or game formulation.
- **Event times**: t̄_p, t̄_e denote the instants at which acceleration/deceleration events are triggered and are dynamically updated as new events occur (p. 3).
- **Helper functions**: sat(x, y) = 0 if x ≤ 0; x if 0 < x ≤ y; y otherwise. sat2(x, y) = y if x ≤ y; x otherwise (so c_p V_p^max ≤ v_p ≤ V_p^max under sat2). Finite-time "sgn" (Eq. (5)): sgn(x, σ) = σ·sign(x) if ‖x‖ > σ^{1/γ}, and sign(x)·‖x‖^γ otherwise, with 0 < γ < 1 a constant; note sgn(x, σ) ≤ σ.

### 5.2 Alert-Turn control laws (Sec. 3.2, pp. 3–4)

- **Algorithm 1** (p. 3): if ∆d_pe(t) < ε₂ → v_p = v_e = w_p = w_e = 0, return (captured); else if ∆d_pe(t) > ε₁ → long-distance strategies (4)–(6); else (ε₂ < ∆d_pe ≤ ε₁) → short-distance strategies (7)–(8).
- **Long-distance evader (Eq. (4))**: v_e(t) = sat(v_e(t̄_e) + a_e(t − t̄_e), V_e^max) — accelerate at tangential acceleration a_e up to V_e^max; w_e(t) = −sgn(θ_e(t) − ∆θ_pe(t), min(r_e v_e⁻¹, W_e^max)) — drive the heading to the pursuer-to-evader bearing (flee along the line away from the pursuer), with the dual angular bound.
- **Long-distance pursuer (Eq. (6))**: v_p = sat(v_p(t̄_p) + a_p(t − t̄_p), V_p^max) if ‖θ_p − ∆θ_pe‖ ≤ θ̄ (aligned: accelerate); otherwise sat2(v_p(t̄_p) − a_p(t − t̄_p), c_p V_p^max) (decelerate to tighten the turn until nearly facing the evader, with speed floor c_p V_p^max, c_p ∈ [0,1)); w_p = −sgn(θ_p − ∆θ_pe, min(r_p v_p⁻¹, W_p^max)). Design intent: both players chase/flee along the connecting straight line at their respective maximum speeds (the Howland 1974 picture), here made feasible by the decelerate-to-align maneuver.

- **Theorem 1 (p. 3)**: Consider the system θ̇ = w, θ ∈ ℝ. With control w = −sgn(θ − θ₀, σ), where sgn(·) is defined in Eq. (5), θ = θ₀ in finite time if θ₀ is a constant.
  - *Assumptions*: θ₀ constant; σ > 0; 0 < γ < 1 (from Eq. (5)).
  - *Proof approach* (5 steps, Lyapunov/finite-time stability): (1) tracking error θ̄ = θ − θ₀ gives θ̄̇ = −sgn(θ̄, σ); (2) V = ½θ̄² gives V̇ = −θ̄ sgn(θ̄, σ) ≤ 0; (3) hence |θ̄| < σ^{1/γ} after a finite time (phase 1: constant-magnitude rate σ·sign); (4) then θ̄̇ = −sign(θ̄)|θ̄|^γ (power law); (5) V̇ = −θ̄ sign(θ̄)|θ̄|^γ = −|θ̄|^{1+γ} ≤ −V^{(1+γ)/2}, and since (1+γ)/2 < 1, V reaches zero in finite time.
  - *Decisive inequality*: V̇ ≤ −V^{(1+γ)/2} (p. 3) — the standard finite-time convergence condition.
  - *Scope*: global in θ (any initial heading error), analytic. *Physical meaning*: the bearing-alignment loop of (4)/(6) is justified as exactly finite-time, so the straight-line long-distance phase is well-posed despite the saturated, speed-dependent angular bound. *Proof gap (minor)*: step (3) is written "θ̄ < σ^{1/γ}" (absolute value implicit); σ in (4)/(6) is actually time-varying through v(t), whereas the theorem is stated for constant σ — the paper applies it pointwise in time without comment.

- **Short-distance phase — linear speeds (Eq. (7))**: v_e = sat(max{0, v_e(t̄_e) − a_e(t − t̄_e)}, V_e^max) if v_e > c_e V_e^max, else sat(v_e(t̄_e) + a_e(t − t̄_e), V_e^max); identically for v_p with c_p (c_e, c_p ∈ [0,1) are minimum-speed fractions during deceleration). Both players first decelerate to shrink the turning radius (as cheetahs do, Wilson et al. 2013), then accelerate back up.
- **Short-distance phase — angular speeds (Eq. (8))**: w_e = sat(−acot(k_e ∆d_pe)·v_e⁻¹, min{r_e v_e⁻¹, W_e^max}) if θ_e − θ_p > 0, and sat(+acot(k_e ∆d_pe)·v_e⁻¹, min{…}) otherwise; w_p = sign(w_e)·sat(acot(k_p ∆d_pe)·v_p⁻¹, min{r_p v_p⁻¹, W_p^max}), with k_e, k_p > 0. Design properties: (i) the evader turns left or right according to the relative orientation θ_e − θ_p; (ii) the pursuer copies the evader's turning direction (necessary for capture); (iii) the commanded turn rate grows as the inter-agent distance shrinks (acot decreasing ⇒ tighter turns close in); (iv) acot(·) can be replaced by any positive decreasing function, e.g., e^{−k·∆d_pe} (p. 4).

### 5.3 Theorem 2 — sufficient conditions for capture (pp. 4–5)

- **Theorem 2 (pp. 4–5, no equation number)**: For a given capture radius ε₂, assume V_p > V_e and W_e^max > W_p^max. Let ∆d_pe(t₀) = d₀ ≤ ε₁, θ_e(t₀) = π/2, θ_p(t₀) = θ_p ∈ [π/2 − γ, π/2 + γ]. If
  - (i) r_p ≥ V_p W_p^max and r_e ≥ V_e W_e^max;
  - (ii) acot(k_p ε₁)·v_p⁻¹ ≥ W_p^max and acot(k_e ε₁)·v_e⁻¹ ≥ W_e^max;
  - (iii) V_p − a_p·π/(2W_p^max) ≥ c_p V_p^max and V_e − a_e·π/(2W_p^max) ≥ c_e V_e^max;
  - (iv) W_p^max < W_e^max ≤ 3W_p^max;
  - (v) ε₁ < ϱ₁ + √(ε₂² − ϱ₂²), where ϱ₁ = V_p − V_e − 2·a_e/(W_e^max)² − (a_e π)/(2 W_p^max W_e^max) + (1 − π/2)·a_p/(W_p^max)² and ϱ₂ = 2·V_e/W_e^max − V_p/W_p^max + a_p/(W_p^max)² − a_e/(W_e^max)²,
  then the evader will be captured at time T with T ∈ (0, π/(2W_p^max)] for small enough γ.
  - *Interpretation of each condition* (Remark 1, p. 5): (i) the centripetal limits r ≥ V·W^max make r v⁻¹ ≥ W^max at top speed, so both players turn at their **maximum angular velocities** during the maneuver (centripetal bound inactive); (ii) at distance ε₁ the acot-law of (8) already commands the maximum angular rate, i.e., the sharp turn starts fully saturated; (iii) both players **decelerate throughout the window** t ∈ (0, π/(2W_p^max)] (speed at the end of a full window of deceleration still exceeds the floor c·V^max — note both bounds are governed by W_p^max, i.e., the pursuer's half-sweep time defines the common analysis window); (iv) the evader's agility advantage is bounded by a factor 3 — beyond that the pursuer can no longer follow the turn geometry; (v) is the geometric closure condition: the alert distance ε₁ must be below the distance the pair closes during the turning window plus the capture-radius term, i.e., the relative distance decreases over (0, π/(2W_p^max)] and is < ε₂ at t = π/(2W_p^max).
  - *Proof*: **not in the paper**. Remark 1: "Please see our arXiv version for the proof Zhou et al. (2024)" (arXiv:2410.16829). This is the paper's only capture guarantee and its proof is external to the published version — a traceability gap for the archive.
  - *Qualitative properties stated*: ϱ₁ > 0 since V_p > V_e; ϱ₂ can be made small if V_e > V_p/2; condition (v) is easily satisfied by choosing a_p and a_e small enough. *Worked illustrative example* (Remark 1): ε₂ = 1, d₀ = ε₁ = 1.31, V_p = 2, V_e = 1.6, W_p^max = 1, W_e^max = 2, θ_p = θ_e = π/2, r_p = 3, r_e = 4, with k_p, k_e, a_p, a_e small enough.
  - *Scope/locality*: a local, configuration-specific analytic result (headings nearly equal, evader heading π/2, d₀ ≤ ε₁); not a global value-function or barrier statement. *Physical meaning*: a faster but less agile pursuer is guaranteed to capture within roughly one pursuer turning window (≤ π/(2W_p^max), the time to sweep π/2 rad at W_p^max) if the prey reacts no earlier than ε₁ and the accelerations are small — conversely, an evader alerted early (large effective ε₁) escapes, which is exactly the paper's later prescription "flee as early as possible".
  - *Observations (critical)*: as printed, (iii) uses W_p^max in **both** bounds — consistent with the common analysis window but easy to misread as a typo; in ϱ₁ the term (1 − π/2)·a_p/(W_p^max)² has a negative coefficient (1 − π/2 ≈ −0.57), so larger pursuer tangential acceleration *shrinks* the capture region in (v) as printed — dimensionally/semantically surprising and worth checking against the arXiv proof; γ appears both in the sgn law (Eq. (5)) and as the initial-heading tolerance in Theorem 2 (overloaded notation).

### 5.4 Multi-agent control laws (Sec. 4.1, pp. 6–7)

- **Grouping**: evaders are split into a **main group** and **isolated group**; an evader is "isolated" if it falls behind the others after its turning maneuver; group center [x_c, y_c]^T = (1/N)[x₁ + … + x_N, y₁ + … + y_N]^T.
- **Main-group evader, long distance (Eq. (9))**: v_{e,i,m} = (1−α)v^agg_{e,i,m} + α v^esc_{e,i,m}, w_{e,i,m} = (1−α)w^agg_{e,i,m} + α w^esc_{e,i,m}; α ∈ [0,1] is the **selfish parameter** (α = 0: pure collective/formation escape; α = 1: fully selfish escape).
- **Isolated evader (Eq. (10))**: v_{e,i,s} = (1−β(∆d_pi))v^joi_{e,i,s} + β(∆d_pi)v^esc_{e,i,s} (and likewise w), where β(∆d_pi) ∈ [0,1] is a decreasing function of the distance to the pursuer — the farther the pursuer, the more weight on rejoining the group.
- **Aggregation commands (Eq. (11))**: v^agg_{e,i,m} = −(1−σ_ii)[(x_{i,m} − x_c)cos θ_{i,m} + (y_{i,m} − y_c)sin θ_{i,m}] + V^max_{e,i,m}; w^agg_{e,i,m} = −[θ_{e,i,m} − (1−σ_ii)∆θ_{ic} − σ_ii ∆θ_{pc}]; σ_ii = 2/(1 + e^{−((d^des_ic)² − ∆d_ic²)}). Here d^des_ic is a configurable desired clearance to the group center (updated at a certain frequency), ∆θ_ic = atan((y_c−y_i)/(x_c−x_i)) and ∆θ_pc = atan((y_c−y_p)/(x_c−x_i)-analog with pursuer position). The ∆θ_pc bias in w^agg turns the group away from the pursuer **even when α = 0** (p. 7).
- **Reduced radial dynamics (Eq. (12))**: with q_di := [x_{i,m} − x_c, y_{i,m} − y_c]^T (so ‖q_di‖ = ∆d_ic), q̇_di = −q_di(1 − σ_ii) = −q_di(1 − 2/(1 + e^{‖q_di‖² − (d^des_ic)²})) — a Gazi & Passino (2004)-type attraction/repulsion sigmoid: outside the ring ‖q‖ > d^des the term 1 − σ_ii > 0 (contraction), inside it is negative (expansion).

- **Theorem 3 (p. 6)**: Let Ω_e = {q_di : ‖q_di‖ = d^des_ic} denote the invariant set of equilibrium points. Then q_di → Ω_e as t → ∞ if ‖q_di(t₀)‖ > 0.
  - *Proof approach* (4 steps, Lyapunov + LaSalle): (1) J(q_di) = q_di^T q_di; (2) substituting (12), J̇ = −2(1 − 2/(1+e^{‖q_di‖²−(d^des_ic)²}))·‖q_di‖²; (3) J̇ = 0 iff q_di ∈ Ω′ ∪ Ω_e with Ω′ = {q_di : q_di = 0}; ‖q_di(t₀)‖ > d^des_ic ⇒ J̇ < 0 (the clearance shrinks), 0 < ‖q_di(t₀)‖ < d^des_ic ⇒ J̇ > 0 (it grows); (4) by LaSalle's Invariance Principle (Khalil 2002) and ‖q_di(t₀)‖ > 0, q_di → Ω_e.
  - *Decisive relation*: the sign structure of J̇ across the ring ‖q‖ = d^des_ic. *Scope*: global on ℝ² \ {0} for the radial dynamics (12) of the *aggregation subsystem alone* — not a statement about the full pursuit dynamics with (9) active. *Physical meaning*: the swarm self-organizes onto a circle of configurable clearance d^des_ic around its center, so "cooperative escape" keeps a formation. *Proof gap (minor)*: LaSalle yields convergence to the largest invariant set in Ω′ ∪ Ω_e; the origin Ω′ is itself an equilibrium of (12) (hence invariant), and its exclusion for ‖q_di(t₀)‖ > 0 is only implicit (J is strictly monotone in the punctured interior); the unboundedness of the exterior domain (precompactness of trajectories) is not addressed. Both are fixable but unstated.
- **Escape commands (Eq. (13))**: v^esp_{e,i,m} = v_{e,i,m}(t̄_{e,i,m}) + a_{e,i,m}(t − t̄_{e,i,m}), w^esp_{e,i,m} = −(θ_{e,i,m} − ∆θ_pi) — accelerate along the current heading and steer to the pursuer-to-evader bearing (superscript "esp" as printed; "esc" intended). Note: no sat(·) is applied in (13)–(15) as printed.
- **Isolated-evader commands**: rejoining (Eq. (14)): v^joi_{e,i,s} = v_{e,i,s}(t̄_{e,i,s}) + a_{e,i,s}(t − t̄_{e,i,s}), w^joi_{e,i,s} = −(θ_{e,i,s} − ∆θ_ic) (head toward the group center); escape (Eq. (15)): same speed ramp, w^esp_{e,i,s} = −(θ_{e,i,s} − ∆θ_pi) (head away from the pursuer). Eq. (10) blends them.
- **Pursuers, long distance (Eq. (16))**: (16a) v_{p_j} = sat(v_{p_j}(t̄_{p_j}) + a_{p_j}(t − t̄_{p_j}), V^max_{p_j}) if ‖θ_{p_j} − ∆θ_{p_j,e_i}‖ ≤ θ̄, else sat2(v_{p_j}(t̄_{p_j}) − a_{p_j}(t − t̄_{p_j}), c_{p_j}V^max_{p_j}) — same accelerate/decelerate-to-align logic as (6); (16b) w_{p_j} = −sgn(θ_{p_j} − ∆θ_{p_j,e_i} − Σ_{k=1}^{N_p} rep_{p_j,k}, min(r_{p_j}v_{p_j}⁻¹, W^max_{p_j})); (16c) rep_{p_j,k} = (m/∆d_{p_j,k})·∆θ_{p_j,k} if ∆d_{p_j,k} < d_safe, else 0 — an inter-pursuer repulsion term (gain m) injected into the heading-alignment error; collision avoidance is achieved purely by orientation change. Target selection principle: closest distance or shortest predicted time; p_l(e_i) denotes the pursuer closest to evader i.
- **Target-changing mechanism (p. 6)**: pursuers detect targets every ∆t̄ time units; evader new_tg becomes the new target if t_new_tg < pt·t_old_tg, where t_new_tg, t_old_tg are the predicted capture times and pt is the threshold. A captured evader stops; its capturer stops or re-targets; other pursuers chasing it re-target as well.
- **Multi-agent short-distance phase (Eqs. (17)–(18))**: evader i re-enters the short-distance phase when its distance to the closest pursuer p_l(e_i) is below ε₁; then (17) replicates (7) per agent (decelerate below c_{e,i}V^max_{e,i}, then accelerate) with w_{e,i} = sat(−acot(k_e ∆d_pei)v_{e,i}⁻¹, min{r_{e,i}v_{e,i}⁻¹, W^max_{e,i}}) if θ_{p_l} − θ_{e_i} > 0, and the + sign otherwise; (18) gives pursuers w_{p_j} = sign(w_{e,i})·sat(acot(k_p ∆d_pei)v_{p_j}⁻¹, min{r_{p_j}v_{p_j}⁻¹, W^max_{p_j}}) and the same speed profile as (7) with c_{p_j} — i.e., (17)–(18) ≡ (7)–(8) index-wise (Remark 2).
- **Dispersion degree (Sec. 4.3, p. 8)**: dispersion = [Σ_{i=1}^{N_e}(pos_i(t_f) − pos_c(t_f)) − Σ_{i=1}^{N_e}(pos_i(t₀) − pos_c(t₀))] / Σ_{i=1}^{N_e}(pos_i(t₀) − pos_c(t₀)), where pos_i, pos_c are the i-th evader's and the group center's positions. As printed this is a ratio of vector sums (no norms); presumably norms are intended — a printing-level ambiguity.
- **No formal multi-agent theorem**: capture/escape outcomes for N_p, N_e > 1 are characterized only by single-run illustrations and statistical sweeps; there is no stability, capture-time, or completeness guarantee beyond Theorems 1–3 above.

**Figure → claim mapping**:
- **Fig. 1** validates that Alert-Turn lets the evader survive a 2× speed disadvantage: trajectories, the first-turn instant, and velocity profiles show periodic escape cycles; in (a) the evader escapes because it has larger centripetal acceleration (quicker first turn); in (b) it turns faster than the pursuer but by less.
- **Fig. 2** validates the capture side: (a) evader captured during its deceleration phase (pursuer also decelerates and turns effectively); (b) evader captured just after finishing deceleration, while accelerating; in both, the pursuer is faster than the evader at the capture instant — replicating the cheetah observation of Wilson et al. (2013) that predators run slightly faster than prey during the maneuvering phase.
- **Fig. 3(a)** (∆d_min surface and outcome map over (r_p, r_e)): capture likely when r_p is small (area ①) or r_e is small (area ②); but capture probability drops when r_p is very large (pursuer overshoots and turns back before intercepting), and even a large r_e (area ①) can be dangerous because the evader may meet the pursuer while turning back — prescription: choose relatively large r_e.
- **Fig. 3(b)** (outcome map over (∆θ₀, ∆d₀)): capture probability decreases with initial distance ∆d₀ (pursuer should close to ≈ 2–2.5 m here before attacking — matching why predators stalk close, Owen-Smith 2019), and capture clusters in ∆θ₀ ∈ [π/2, π] and [−π, −π/2] (evader initially facing the pursuer has to turn through a larger angle).
- **Fig. 4** (outcome map over (a_p, a_e)): escape in area ③ (pursuer's large deceleration ⇒ much quicker initial turn ⇒ overshoot; cf. Fig. 1(a)); capture in areas ④, ⑤, ⑥ (④ both decelerate mildly; ⑤ pursuer large acceleration vs evader's very small one — evader caught as the pursuer finishes decelerating and accelerates; ⑥ evader caught while accelerating despite a faster initial turn). Conclusion shared with Wilson et al. (2018): escaping at lower speed lets prey exploit maximum maneuvering performance; prescription: evader should use smaller a_e when a_p is small and larger a_e when a_p is large.
- **Fig. 5(a)**: the lowest ε₁ at which the evader still escapes decreases with r_e and saturates for r_e ≳ 0.6 (because w is then capped by W^max, so extra r_e has no effect). **Figs. 5(b)–(d)**: capture rate falls steeply with ε₁ then flattens — prescription: the evader should trigger the maneuver as early as possible within the alert distance.
- **Fig. 6**: increasing α morphs the escape from tight cooperative formation (α = 0) to fully selfish scattering (α = 1), the latter resembling antelope/zebra flushes.
- **Fig. 7**: 4 evaders vs 8 pursuers run — evader 2 captured at t = 8.6 s, evader 1 at ≈ 23.15 s (pursuers 5–6 retarget evader 4; pursuers 1, 3, 4 target evader 3), evaders 3 and 4 at 37.85 s and 43.9 s; no inter-pursuer collisions with d_safe = 0.8 — validates the target-changing mechanism, retargeting after capture, and (16c) collision avoidance.

## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated (Sec. 6, p. 8)**: systematic selection of parameter values is left for future work; extension to obstacle-rich environments and to the inverse optimal control problem (learning the animals' objective functions) is planned.

**Critical observations (with page/eq.)**:
1. **No game-theoretic optimality**: the framework is deliberately non-game-theoretic; there is no value function, no barrier analysis, and no optimality claim — the capture guarantee (Theorem 2) holds only for a restricted initial configuration (d₀ ≤ ε₁, θ_e(t₀) = π/2, θ_p(t₀) within γ of π/2) and its proof is **not in the published paper** (deferred to arXiv:2410.16829, Remark 1, p. 5), so the published record cannot be checked against the stated conditions.
2. **Theorem 2 condition quirks**: (iii) bounds the *evader's* speed margin with W_p^max (p. 4) — consistent with the common window (0, π/(2W_p^max)] but fragile as stated; in (v) the coefficient (1 − π/2) ≈ −0.57 multiplying a_p/(W_p^max)² makes larger pursuer tangential acceleration *shrink* the admissible ε₁ region as printed, which is counter-intuitive and warrants checking against the arXiv proof; γ is overloaded (sgn power in Eq. (5) and heading tolerance in Theorem 2).
3. **Theorem 3 proof gaps**: LaSalle is applied on an unbounded domain without a precompactness argument, and the origin equilibrium Ω′ = {0} (invariant, repelling) is excluded only implicitly via the strict monotonicity of J in the punctured disk (p. 6). Moreover Theorem 3 concerns the aggregation subsystem (12) alone — with escape commands (13) active, the swarm-center motion and the guarantee's applicability are unanalyzed.
4. **Printed-definition inconsistencies**: Eq. (2) omits absolute values on w (p. 3); Eqs. (13)–(15) carry the superscript "esp" (for "esc") and omit saturation on the speed commands; the dispersion metric (p. 8) is written as a ratio of vector sums without norms; the isolated-evader rejoining rule reads "re-joins the main group if its distance to the center ... **exceeds** a predefined threshold" (p. 6), which is the opposite of the intended hysteresis (presumably "falls below").
5. **Information assumptions**: perfect knowledge of relative positions/bearings at all times, instantaneous detection at ε₁, no sensing noise, delay, or field-of-view limits; ε₁ is treated as a design parameter although in biology it emerges from perception.
6. **Multi-agent analysis is statistical only**: outcomes over (∆t̄, pt) (Fig. 9) are settings-specific grids; the target-selection principle ("closest distance or shortest time", p. 7) is left unspecified as to which is used where; predicted capture times are not defined operationally; no comparison against any baseline multi-pursuer strategy (e.g., Voronoi or assignment-based pursuit) is provided.
7. **Validation scope**: the "replication" claim rests on five video segments, bounding-box-center positions from YOLOv8 (no calibration/projection to ground coordinates described), and trend-level agreement of relative distances/times; parameter units and the mapping between simulation parameters and animal biomechanics (e.g., r, a values vs measured cheetah accelerations) are not established.
8. **Reproducibility**: key quantitative details (integrator, step, run counts, initial conditions, γ, θ̄) are unreported in the paper itself; some are presumably in the arXiv version or the YouTube supplements.

## 8. Citation Value

**Paragraph role**: P114 is the reference example of the *bio-inspired, non-game-theoretic* branch of pursuit–evasion research: it replaces the Isaacs/value-function machinery with a two-phase behavioral control law (Alert-Turn) on unicycles whose angular-rate bound r·v⁻¹ encodes the speed-dependent turning radius — the quantitative core of the "faster but less agile predator vs slower but more agile prey" trade-off. It is most useful to this library as (a) the modernized, nonlinear-dynamics successor of Li (2016, TAC) with a multi-agent aggregation/selfish-parameter/target-changing layer, (b) a source of the capture sufficiency theorem on the window t ∈ (0, π/(2W_p^max)] under bounded centripetal/tangential acceleration — a rare analytic guarantee under *acceleration*-level maneuverability constraints rather than constant-speed kinematics, and (c) an empirical template for validating pursuit strategies against real animal chase data (YOLOv8-extracted dog–rabbit and cheetah–deer chases). When writing papers in this library, cite it in introductions to contrast game-theoretic optimal-strategy approaches (geometric/HJI/reach-avoid) with heuristic nature-inspired control, to justify unicycle models with velocity-coupled turning limits, or to motivate "evade early, decelerate to turn" prescriptions with both theory and animal data.

**Suggested citation sentences**:
- "Beyond game-theoretic formulations, nature-inspired control laws on unicycle models with speed-dependent turning limits can reproduce predator–prey capture and escape behaviors; Zhou et al. (2026) prove that a faster, less agile pursuer captures an evader within one turning window π/(2W_p^max) under sufficient conditions on the centripetal/tangential accelerations and the alert distance [P114]."
