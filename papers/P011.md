## 2. Research Background

Since Isaacs introduced differential games in 1965 [1], reach-avoid games have been a canonical "game of kind": an attacker (evader) tries to reach a target region while a defender (pursuer) tries to intercept it or delay it indefinitely, and the solution object is the **barrier** — a hypersurface in state space separating the two players' winning regions. The HJI approach solves low-dimensional versions [17]; geometric/analytic barriers have been built for two-defender-one-attacker games on rectangular domains [28], lifeline games [20], and cooperative target defense [34, 35]; multiplayer versions are handled by maximum matching [25, 26], risk-based feedback [27], Voronoi partitions [21], and perimeter-defense decompositions [29–31]; target-attacker-defender variants appear in [32, 33]. The authors observe that essentially all of these works assume **full and symmetric state information**, whereas real platforms carry sensors with limited range, and that only a few works (notably Yan et al.'s lifeline game with limited lifetime [36]) impose **maximum operating times** on the players. Their motivating military picture is an attacking aircraft/bomber (with the initiative, knowing the defender's radar radius and position) approaching a target line guarded by a defender that is passive until its radar detects the intruder.

## 3. Research Problem and Gap

**Problem.** Solve the game of kind for a planar reach-avoid game in which (i) the attacker has a **maximum operating time** $t_A$ (equivalently a maximum range $R_A = v_A t_A$), and (ii) the defender is **static and blind until the attacker enters a circular detection range** $R_D$ (eq. (2), p. 5). Given the initial configuration, $v_A, v_D$ (speed ratio $\alpha = v_A/v_D < 1$), $t_A$, $R_D$, $y_{D,0}$, decide which player wins and exhibit the complete barrier $\mathscr{B}$, whose restriction to fixed $x_D$ is a 2-D curve partitioning the plane into the attack dominance region (ADR) and defense dominance region (DDR) — terminology taken from Yan et al. [28].

**[Authors' claim of the gap]** (p. 2): "most of these works consider only the situation when both sides of the game have full state information of the other"; sensors' detection ranges are always limited in reality; "only a few works have considered the maximum operation times of the players," e.g., Yan et al. [36]; and (p. 3, Contribution list; repeated p. 23) "To our best knowledge, it is the first time that someone brings the time limit and the detection range to the reach-avoid game simultaneously."

**[Verified from cited text as described in the paper]** The related-work paragraph shows that the surveyed papers ([17]–[35]) indeed assume observable opponents; the closest prior art acknowledged is Yan et al. [36] (time limit only, no detection range) and visibility-based pursuit-evasion with circular obstacles [18, 19] (limited visibility, no time limit, no target line). The claim that the *combination* is new is consistent with what the paper itself surveys; I could only verify the claim against the descriptions printed in P011, not against the cited originals. Internally the claim is substantiated: the paper does deliver a complete barrier for the combined model (Table 2, p. 21) covering all parameter regimes. One honest caveat: the "detection" model is strongly idealized (defender perfectly static before first detection, no false alarms, no tracking model after loss of contact beyond a "random walk" clause, p. 5, which never enters the analysis), so the practical gap closed is narrower than the military narrative suggests.

## 4. Core Contributions

1. **Theory (attacker detected initially)**: Theorem 1 (p. 6) — under detection, equilibrium play is straight-line at full speed (via HJI on the decoupled simple-motion payoff (9)–(14)), and the attacker can never leave the detection range once detected (Apollonius-circle monotone-separation argument); hence the game reduces to Yan et al.'s time-limit-only game [36] and the barrier is $\mathscr{B}^{\text{inner}}$ (Theorem 2, eq. (17)), plus the degeneracy condition $\mathscr{B} = \mathscr{B}^T$ of Theorem 3 (eq. (18)–(19)). The authors stress this "cannot leave" result is reusable in other games with time limit + detection range.
2. **Theory (equilibrium terminal-state reduction)**: Theorem 4 (pp. 8–9) — for a penetrating trajectory the neutral outcome $\mathscr{S}_{N,1}$ (capture on the target line before $t_A$) is impossible; hence on the barrier outside the detection range the attacker must arrive at $\mathcal{T}$ exactly at its time limit, i.e., its optimal trajectory is the **shortest trajectory guaranteeing arrival at $\mathcal{T}$ without prior interception**, a time-limit-independent object. This shrinks the candidate terminal set and converts the barrier construction into a path-length vs. $R_A$ comparison.
3. **Algorithmic/geometric classification of optimal attacker trajectories** (Section 4, pp. 8–16): a complete case split over $(R_D, y_{D,0}, R_A)$ giving the shortest guaranteed path — vertical drop (Area 1), tangent–arc–drop bypassing trajectory (Lemma 4), directly penetrating, or bypassing-penetrating trajectory with the entry angle thresholds $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3$ (Theorems 5–8). Valid verbatim also for **sectorial** detection areas (infrared seeker / vision sensor), Figure 7, p. 16–17.
4. **Theory (complete barrier)**: closed-form barrier expressions in every parameter regime — eqs. (61), (62) ($y_{D,0} \ge R_A$), (65) ($y_{D,0} < R_A$, bypassing regime), (68) ($y_{D,0} < R_A$, penetrating regime), summarized in Table 2 (p. 21); claimed as the first complete solution of the combined game, illustrated in Figures 8–14.
5. **Experiment (validation)**: four numerical case studies (Section 6, pp. 19–23, Figures 15–19) simulating the derived optimal strategies and checking outcomes against the barrier prediction.

## 5. Key Mathematical Results

### 5.0 Model, information structure, termination (Section 2, pp. 3–5)

- **Geometry**: $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^2$ split by the target line $\mathcal{T} = \{(x,y): y = 0\}$ into play region $\Omega_P = \{y>0\}$ and target region $\Omega_T = \{y<0\}$. Coordinate origin on $\mathcal{T}$ below $D$, so $x_D(0) = (0, y_{D,0})$.
- **Dynamics (eq. (1), p. 4)**: simple motion, $\dot{x} = (v_A\cos\varphi_A,\ v_A\sin\varphi_A,\ v_D\cos\varphi_D,\ v_D\sin\varphi_D)^T = f(x)$, with constant max speeds $v_A, v_D$, $\alpha = v_A/v_D < 1$ (defender faster), headings $\varphi_A, \varphi_D \in [-\pi, \pi)$ chosen simultaneously and instantaneously.
- **Time limit**: only the attacker is energy-limited (defender is deployed near the friendly target area): max operating time $t_A$, maximum range $R_A = v_A t_A$. (Typographic note: the quantity is printed as $t_A$ in Table 1 and eq. (15) but as $\bar{t}_A$ in eqs. (3), (7), (8), (63), (66), (68) and on p. 22 — same quantity.)
- **Information structure**: detection condition (eq. (2), p. 5) $\mathscr{D} = \{x : \|x_A(t)-x_D(t)\|_2 < R_D\}$ — note the **strict** inequality, so sitting exactly on the "circle of detection" counts as undetected. $D$ stays still ($\dot x_D = \dot y_D = 0$) until first detection; if contact is lost after a detection, $D$ performs a random walk (p. 5) — this clause is never used in the analysis. $A$ knows $R_D$ and $D$'s location (attacker's initiative, p. 4); $D$ cannot foresee the intrusion.
- **Capture/termination**: point capture; capture set (eq. (3)) $\mathscr{C} = \{x: \|x_A(t_f)-x_D(t_f)\|_2 = 0,\ t_f \le t_A\}$; terminal set (eq. (4)) $\mathscr{S} = \mathscr{S}_A \cup \mathscr{S}_D$; attacker win (eq. (5)) $\mathscr{S}_A = \{x: y_A(t_f) \le 0,\ \|x_A(t)-x_D(t)\|_2 > 0,\ t \le t_f \le t_A\}$; defender win (eq. (6)) $\mathscr{S}_D = \{x: y_A(t)>0,\ x(t)\in\mathscr{C},\ t\le t_f\} \cup \{x: y_A(t_f)>0,\ x(t_f)\notin\mathscr{C}\}$.
- **Neutral (equilibrium) states** (eqs. (7)–(8), p. 5): $\mathscr{S}_{N,1} = \{y_A(t_f)=0,$ capture$,\ t_f < t_A\}$; $\mathscr{S}_{N,2} = \{y_A(t_f)=0,$ no capture$,\ t_f = t_A\}$; $\mathscr{S}_{N,3} = \{y_A(t_f)=0,$ capture$,\ t_f = t_A\}$.
- Two regimes: attacker initially inside ($\|x_A(0)-x_D(0)\| < R_D$, Section 3) vs. outside ($> R_D$, Sections 4–5); outside, the pre-detection phase degenerates into a single-agent optimization for $A$ (p. 5–6).

### 5.1 Detected attacker: straight-line play and the inner barrier (Section 3, pp. 5–8)

- **Theorem 1 (p. 6)**: *When the attacker is inside the detection range, the players' optimal trajectories are straight lines and the attacker remains inside the detection range for the rest of the game.*
  - *Approach*: HJI on the terminal payoff (eq. (9)) $J = G(x(t_f),t_f) + \int_0^{t_f} F\,dt$ with $G = a_1\|x_A(t_f)-x_D(t_f)\|_2 - a_2 y_A(t_f)$, $a_1,a_2>0$, $F \equiv 0$; value (eq. (10)) $V = \min_{\varphi_D,v_D}\max_{\varphi_A,v_A} J$; Hamiltonian (eq. (11)) $H = \lambda^T f(x)$; control-decoupled saddle condition (eq. (12)); optimal controls (eq. (13)): $\cos\varphi_A^* = \lambda_1/\rho_1$, $\sin\varphi_A^* = \lambda_2/\rho_1$, $\rho_1 = \sqrt{\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2}$, $v_A^* = v_A$; $\cos\varphi_D^* = -\lambda_3/\rho_2$, $\sin\varphi_D^* = -\lambda_4/\rho_2$, $\rho_2 = \sqrt{\lambda_3^2+\lambda_4^2}$, $v_D^* = v_D$; $H^* = v_A\rho_1 - v_D\rho_2$. Co-state dynamics (eq. (14)) $\dot\lambda = -\partial H/\partial x = 0$ ⇒ constant co-states ⇒ constant optimal headings ⇒ straight lines.
  - *Second half of proof (p. 7)*: citing Isaacs [1], along these strategies $A$ and $D$ ultimately coincide (equilibrium terminal position), so the separation "monotonically decreases to zero" ⇒ once detected, never undetected. All possible coincidence points form the **Apollonius circle** with center $O = \big((x_A-\alpha^2 x_D)/(1-\alpha^2),\ (y_A-\alpha^2 y_D)/(1-\alpha^2)\big)$ and radius $r_O = \tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha^2}\|x_A - x_D\|_2$ (p. 7). *Proof style: heuristic but standard; the monotonicity claim is asserted along equilibrium play, not proved for arbitrary defender deviations.*
- **Lemma 1 (see [1], p. 7)**: for a pair of straight-moving agents the Apollonius circle partitions the plane; every point **inside** it is reached by $A$ before $D$ regardless of $A$'s time limit; hence if the Apollonius circle intersects the target region, $A$ wins by heading to any point inside both the circle and the target region.
- **Time-limit-only barrier** (from Yan et al. [36], restated without proof), **eq. (15), p. 7**, valid for $y_{D,0} < R_A/\alpha$:
$$\mathscr{B}^T = \{(x,y):\ x^2 + (1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha^2})y^2 + (1-\alpha^2)y_{D,0}^2 = 0,\ x\in(-p_1,p_1)\}$$
$$\cup\ \{(x,y):\ (x - \sqrt{(R_A/\alpha)^2 - y_{D,0}^2})^2 + y^2 = R_A^2,\ x\in[p_1,p_2)\}\ \cup\ \text{(mirror arc, } x\in(-p_2,p_1])$$
$$\cup\ \{(x,y):\ y = R_A,\ x\in(-\infty,-p_2]\cup[p_2,+\infty)\},$$
  a hyperbola-type central branch ($y \ge \alpha y_{D,0}$) joined at $x = \pm p_1$ to two arcs of the radius-$R_A$ circles centered at $(\pm\sqrt{(R_A/\alpha)^2 - y_{D,0}^2}, 0)$, closed by the horizontal line $y = R_A$, where $p_1 = (1-\alpha^2)\sqrt{(R_A/\alpha)^2 - y_{D,0}^2}$, $p_2 = \sqrt{(R_A/\alpha)^2 - y_{D,0}^2}$ (continuity at $x=p_1$ checked: $y^2 = (1-\alpha^2)R_A^2 + \alpha^4 y_{D,0}^2$ from both branches). **Eq. (16)**: if $y_{D,0} \ge R_A/\alpha$, $\mathscr{B}^T = \{(x,y): y = R_A\}$; thereafter only $y_{D,0} < R_A/\alpha$ is discussed.
- **Lemma 2 (p. 7)**: on $\mathscr{B}^T$ with $y_A(0) = R_A$, the defender is irrelevant; $A$'s optimal trajectory is the vertical drop to $\mathcal{T}$, arriving exactly at $t_A$ (equilibrium caused by the time limit only). Consequently $\{y > R_A\}$ is always inside the DDR (range limit).
- **Theorem 2 (p. 7, eq. (17))**: *the barrier when the attacker is initially detected is the part of $\mathscr{B}^T$ inside the detection range*: $\mathscr{B}^{\text{inner}} = \{(x,y): (x,y)\in\mathscr{B}^T,\ \sqrt{x^2+y^2} < R_D\}$. Proof omitted ("this theorem is obvious", p. 8) — **proof gap (minor)**: immediate given Theorem 1, but the omission is never flagged for boundary/tangency cases.
- **Theorem 3 (p. 8, eqs. (18)–(19))**: for a barrier point $(x_B, y_B)$, if $x_B \ge \min\{p_2, R_D\}$ then $y_B = R_A$ (undetected vertical drop: for $x_B \ge R_D$ the drop never triggers eq. (2); for $x_B \ge p_2$ use Lemma 2). Moreover, if
$$R_D \ge \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_A y_{D,0}}\quad (18)$$
then the full barrier of the combined game is $\mathscr{B} = \mathscr{B}^T$: the condition is exactly that the junction point $(p_2, R_A)$ lie inside the detection range, $R_D \ge \sqrt{p_2^2 + (R_A - y_{D,0})^2}$ (eq. (19); identity verified: $p_2^2 + (R_A-y_{D,0})^2 = \tfrac{R_A^2}{\alpha^2} - y_{D,0}^2 + R_A^2 - 2R_Ay_{D,0} + y_{D,0}^2$). Sections 4–5 henceforth assume $R_D < \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_A y_{D,0}}$ and $y_{D,0} < R_A/\alpha$.

### 5.2 Attacker undetected: shortest guaranteed trajectories (Section 4, pp. 8–16)

- **Theorem 4 (pp. 8–9)**: *if the attacker moves along a penetrating trajectory, the related neutral states cannot be in $\mathscr{S}_{N,1}$.* Proof by contradiction: inside the detection range the optimal trajectory is the straight line of Theorem 1 entering at the **penetration point** $I_P$; if $A$ and $D$ were to coincide on $\mathcal{T}$ at time $t_1 < t_A$, the coincidence point is $P_l = (x_l, 0)$ or $P_r = (x_r, 0)$, the intersections of the $I_P$-based Apollonius circle with $\mathcal{T}$; since the Apollonius center $x$-coordinate is $x_{I_P}/(1-\alpha^2) > x_{I_P}$, $|I_PP_r| > |I_PP_l|$; all $\mathcal{T}$-points between $P_l$ and $P_r$ are inside the Apollonius circle (Lemma 1 ⇒ reachable regardless of the time limit); so if the neutral terminal were $P_r$, retargeting to any nearer point in $[P_l, P_r]$ along the same outside arc $\mathscr{C}_{out}$ wins strictly before $t_1$; if it were $P_l$, some $P_N$ right of $P_l$ satisfies $|I_PP_N| - |I_PP_l| < v_A(t_A - t_1)$, again winning before the limit. Contradiction in both cases.
  - *Decisive consequence (p. 9)*: when $A$ starts undetected, $\mathscr{S}_N = \mathscr{S}_{N,2} \cup \mathscr{S}_{N,3}$, so **on the barrier outside the detection range the attacker reaches $\mathcal{T}$ exactly at $t_A$ along the shortest trajectory that guarantees unintercepted arrival** — a quantity independent of $t_A$; the barrier then follows from comparing that path length with $R_A$.
- **Lemma 3 (p. 9, eq. (20))**: shortest path from $P_1$ outside a circle to $P_2$ on its boundary without penetrating: $L_S = P_1P_2$ if $P_2 \in \Omega_1$ (directly visible arc), $= P_1P_i + P_2P_i$ if $P_2 \in \Omega_2$, where $P_{L,R}$ are the two tangent points from $P_1$ and $P_i$ the tangent point nearer to $P_2$. (Stated without proof; standard.)
- **Lemma 4 (p. 9, stated without proof)**: in Area 1 ($x \in (-\infty,-R_D)\cup[R_D,+\infty)$ plus the region below the circle, Figure 3, p. 9) the optimal strategy is the vertical drop; in Area 2 (above the circle, $|x| < R_D$) the optimal **bypassing** trajectory is: straight segment to the lower tangent point from $A$ → arc along the circle of detection to the point with $y$-coordinate $y_{D,0}$ (i.e., $(R_D, y_{D,0})$) → vertical drop to $\mathcal{T}$.
- **Feasibility of penetration (pp. 10, eqs. (21)–(23))**: $I_P = (x_P, y_P)$ must satisfy $y_P \ge y_{D,0}$ (Area 2, Lemma 4) and $y_P < R_A$ (Lemma 2), hence $y_{D,0} < R_A$ is necessary; and the $I_P$-based Apollonius circle must meet $\mathcal{T}$ — lowest point on/below $\mathcal{T}$:
  eq. (21): $\dfrac{y_P - \alpha^2 y_{D,0}}{1-\alpha^2} - \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha^2}\sqrt{x_P^2 + (y_P - y_{D,0})^2} \le 0$, simplifying to **eq. (22)**: $y_P^2 - \dfrac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha^2}x_P^2 \le \alpha^2 y_{D,0}^2$ (algebra verified). Polar system (eq. (23)): pole $(0, y_{D,0})$, axis parallel to $x$; $I_P = (R_D, \theta_P)$, $I_F = (R_D, \theta_F)$ with $R_D\cos\theta_i = x_i$, $R_D\sin\theta_i = y_i - y_{D,0}$, $i \in \{P, F\}$.
- **Theorem 5 (p. 10–11, eqs. (24)–(26))**: *if $R_D \in (0, \tfrac{1-\alpha^2}{\alpha}y_{D,0})$, the attacker cannot enter the detection range without losing; only the bypassing trajectory is available.* Proof: rewrite (22) as $f_1(\theta_P) = (R_D\sin\theta_P + y_{D,0})^2 - \tfrac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha^2}(R_D\cos\theta_P)^2 \le \alpha^2 y_{D,0}^2$ (eq. (25)) ⟺ **eq. (26)** $R_D < \tfrac{1-\alpha^2}{\alpha}y_{D,0}$ (verified by re-derivation; no radical here — see Theorem 6 for the distinct radical threshold).
- **Theorem 6 (p. 11, eqs. (27)–(30))**: *if $R_D \in \big(\tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0},\ \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_A y_{D,0}}\big)$ and $y_{D,0} < R_A$, the optimal trajectory of $A$ in Area 2 must be penetrating.* Decisive step: at the point $(R_D, y_{D,0})$ the left Apollonius intersection satisfies $x_l = \tfrac{R_D}{1-\alpha^2} - \sqrt{\big(\tfrac{\alpha R_D}{1-\alpha^2}\big)^2 - y_{D,0}^2}$, and $x_P - x_l > 0$ (eq. (29)).
  - **The open corridor** (p. 11): for $R_D \in \big[\tfrac{1-\alpha^2}{\alpha}y_{D,0},\ \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}\big]$ all three trajectory types are possible candidates — "the most complicated situation", resolved a posteriori by Theorem 8.
- **Theorem 7 (pp. 11–15, eqs. - **Theorem 8 (pp. 15–16, eqs. (53)–(54))**: *if $R_D \in \big(\tfrac{1-\alpha^2}{\alpha}y_{D,0},\ \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}\big)$, the optimal trajectory is the bypassing trajectory.* Proof: here $\eta \in (\alpha/\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}, \alpha/(1-\alpha^2))$, $p \in (\alpha\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}, \alpha)$ (eq. (53)), so (eq. (54)) $\min_{\theta_P}\partial l/\partial\theta_P > \big(\partial l/\partial\theta_P\big)\big|_{\theta_P=0,\,p=\alpha\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}} = R_D$; hence $L(\theta_P) > 0$ everywhere: the directly penetrating trajectory is always longer than moving clockwise along the circle; since for $R_D < \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}$ one has $x_l > R_D$ at $(R_D, y_{D,0})$ (negation of (28)), the vertical drop from $(R_D, y_{D,0})$ is optimal — exactly the bypassing trajectory of Lemma 4. **This closes the corridor**: bypassing holds for all $R_D \le \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}$.
- **Summary of optimal trajectories (p. 16)**: (1) $y_{D,0} \ge R_A$ ⇒ bypassing; (2) $y_{D,0} < R_A$ and $R_D \in \big(\tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}, \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_Ay_{D,0}}\big)$ ⇒ penetrating, subtyped by Theorem 7 ($\theta_F \gtrless \theta_1$, vertical drop if $\theta_F \le \theta_2$); (3) $y_{D,0} < R_A$, $R_D \le \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}$ ⇒ bypassing; (4) $R_D > \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_Ay_{D,0}}$ ⇒ barrier is $\mathscr{B}^T$ by Theorem 3, no trajectory analysis needed.
- **Sectorial extension (pp. 16–17, Figure 7)**: the same trajectories remain optimal when the detection area is a sector (infrared seeker/vision sensor); one only additionally compares the special points $(R_D, \theta_i), i\in\{1,2,3\}$ with the sector-arc endpoints $E_1, E_2$ (e.g., if $E_1$'s polar angle exceeds $\theta_1, \theta_3$, part of the optimal path is a vertical drop from $E_1$).
- **Physical meaning**: the attacker's optimal undetected behavior is "graze the radar boundary": it skims exactly on the circle of detection (legal because detection uses strict $<$), which is the knife-edge geometric idea underlying Lemma 4/8 and the barrier shapes of Figures 10–14.

### 5.3 Complete barrier — game of kind (Section 5, pp. 16–19 + Table 2, p. 21)

By symmetry only $x_A > 0$ is analyzed, and only $R_D < \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_Ay_{D,0}}$ (else Theorem 3).

- **Regime $y_{D,0} \ge R_A$ (Section 5.1)**: no feasible penetration point exists ($y_P \ge y_{D,0} \ge R_A$ contradicts $y_P < R_A$), so the attacker only bypasses. Intersecting the circle of detection with the hyperbola branch of $\mathscr{B}^T$ (system (55)) yields **eq. (56)** $\tfrac{y^2}{\alpha^2} - 2y_{D,0}y + \alpha^2 y_{D,0}^2 = R_D^2$, i.e. $(\tfrac{y}{\alpha} - \alpha y_{D,0})^2 = R_D^2$, solution $y = \alpha^2 y_{D,0} + \alpha R_D$ (eq. (57)); substituting back gives **eq. (58)** $x^2 + \big((\alpha^2-1)y_{D,0} + (\alpha-1)R_D\big)\big((\alpha^2-1)y_{D,0} + (\alpha+1)R_D\big) = 0$, which has real roots iff (59) ⟺ **eq. (60)** $(1-\alpha)y_{D,0} < R_D$ — i.e., $\mathscr{B}^T$ and the circle of detection intersect in two points iff $R_D > (1-\alpha)y_{D,0}$, and are tangent at one point at equality.
  - **Subsection 5.1.1, $(1-\alpha)y_{D,0} \ge R_D$ (eq. (61), p. 17, Figure 8)**: at most one (tangency) point ⇒ no $\mathscr{B}^{\text{inner}}$ component, and
    $$\mathscr{B} = \{(x,y):\ y = y_{D,0} - \sqrt{R_D^2 - x^2},\ x\in[-m,m]\}\ \cup\ \{(x,y):\ y = y_A\ \textbf{[sic]},\ x\in(-\infty,-m)\cup(-m,+\infty)\},\quad m = \sqrt{R_D^2 - (R_A - y_{D,0})^2}.$$
    The lower semicircle of the detection circle **is itself the barrier**: below it the vertical drop is undetected and shorter than $R_A$; on/above it the attacker is detected or forced to bypass and loses. The second family prints "$y = y_A$" — **typo for $y = R_A$** (confirmed by the surrounding text "if $y_{D,0} \ge R_A$, the expression of the barrier is $y = R_A$" and by eq. (62)); with $y = R_A$ the two pieces join continuously at $x = \pm m$ since $y_{D,0} - \sqrt{R_D^2 - m^2} = R_A$. Note $m$ is real only for $y_{D,0} \le R_A + R_D$; beyond that the circle bottom lies above $R_A$ and the barrier degenerates to $y = R_A$, matching the printed remark. The authors note the detection range **expands ADR** (benefits the attacker) relative to $\mathscr{B}^T$.
  - **Subsection 5.1.2, $(1-\alpha)y_{D,0} < R_D$ (eq. (62), p. 17, Figure 9)**: the circle cuts $\mathscr{B}^T$ at $I_{R,L} = (\pm x_{I_R}, y_{I_R})$; when $y_{I_R} < R_A$,
    $$\mathscr{B} = \{(x,y)\in\mathscr{B}^T,\ x\in[-x_{I_R}, x_{I_R}]\}\ \cup\ \{(x,y):\ y = y_{D,0} - \sqrt{R_D^2-x^2},\ x\in[-m,-x_{I_R})\cup(x_{I_R}, m]\}\ \cup\ \{(x,y): y = R_A,\ x \notin [-m,m]\}.$$
- **Regime $y_{D,0} < R_A$ (Section 5.2)**: for $R_D \le (1-\alpha)y_{D,0}$, eq. (61) again applies (Table 2, row 3 block 1). Otherwise:
  - **Subsection 5.2.1, $R_D \in \big((1-\alpha), \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}\big]y_{D,0}$ (eqs. (63)–(66), pp. 18–19, Figures 10–11)**: bypassing is optimal (Theorem 8). An attacker initially on the barrier exhausts the range along the bypassing trajectory:
    **eq. (63)**: $h_2(x,y) = l_F(x,y) + R_D|\theta_F| + y_{D,0} = v_A t_A$, with **eq. (64)** $l_F(x,y) = \sqrt{(x - R_D\cos\theta_F)^2 + (y - (R_D\sin\theta_F + y_{D,0}))^2}$ (segment to first-touch point $I_F$) — the three terms being tangent length, arc length to $(R_D, y_{D,0})$, and final drop. The barrier (eq. (65)) is the union of: (i) $\mathscr{B}^T$ for $|x| \le x_{I_R}$; (ii) the circle of detection arc $x = R_D\cos\theta,\ y = R_D\sin\theta + y_{D,0}$ for $\theta \in (\pi-\theta_{high}, \pi-\theta_3)\cup(\theta_3, \theta_{high})$, where $\theta_3 = \arctan\big((y_{I_R}-y_{D,0})/x_{I_R}\big)$ is the polar angle of the $\mathscr{B}^T\cap$ circle intersection and $\theta_{high}$ is defined by **eq. ## 7. Limitations and Open Issues

**Author-stated** (pp. 4–5, 23): simple motion and mass-point players only; unbounded planar domain; point capture; single attacker vs. single defender; only the attacker is time-limited (the defender is assumed to have ample energy near the friendly target); detection footprint restricted to circular (and, by extension, sectorial) shapes; future work = richer dynamics and environments with obstacles.

**Critical observations (with locations)**:
1. **Modeling knife-edge**: detection uses the strict inequality $\|x_A - x_D\| < R_D$ (eq. (2)), so the optimal bypassing trajectory *walks exactly on the circle of detection* (Lemma 4, Theorem 8) and the barrier pieces meet at exact tangencies. Any sensor noise, state uncertainty, or adversarial deviation destroys this zero-margin construction; robustness is never discussed.
2. **Information structure is idealized and partially vestigial**: the defender is static until first detection, has no tracking/memory model, and the "random walk after losing contact" clause (p. 5) never enters the analysis; symmetric knowledge is assumed in reverse (the attacker knows $R_D$ and $D$'s exact position). The practical novelty over Yan et al. [36] is therefore confined to the pre-detection phase.
3. **Printed errata** (all verified against the 200-dpi renders): eq. (61) prints "$y = y_A$" for $y = R_A$ (p. 17); eq. (65)'s $h_2$-piece is delimited by $\pm p_2$ where $\pm R_D$ is required (p. 18; conflicts with Theorem 3 and with $h_2(R_D,y) = y$); Table 2's penetrating-regime entry cites "(67)" where the barrier is (68) (p. 21); Case 2 cites barrier "(66)" (p. 21) and its initial state sits inside the detection set as printed (see Section 6); $t_A$ vs. $\bar{t}_A$ oscillates typographically (Table 1 vs. eqs. (3), (7), (8), (63), (66), (68)).
4. **Rigor gaps**: Theorem 2's proof omitted (p. 8); Lemmas 3–4 unproved; the barrier is asserted, not verified against the Isaacs/HJI construction (no proof that the union curve is the viability boundary under nonanticipative strategies); Theorem 1's persistence claim is equilibrium-specific.
5. **The "first simultaneous introduction" claim** should be read within this exact model class (simple motion + target line + static blind defender); visibility-limited pursuit-evasion [18, 19] and sensing-limited pursuit [cf. Maity et al. style work] study related but different information constraints, and bounded-lifetime ideas exist in [36] — the genuinely new part is the coupled shortest-path/barrier analysis of Sections 4–5.
6. **Scalability**: everything is two-player; the closing suggestion that a DDR attacker "sacrifice itself to help its teammate" (p. 23) hints at multiplayer extensions that are not modeled here (cf. the same group's companion work [37], and Chen 等 2023 geometric approach with time limits in the same PDF collection).

## 8. Citation Value

This is the reference for **barrier analysis of reach-avoid games under asymmetric, range-limited detection plus an attacker time limit** — i.e., the "blind until detected defender / fuel- or range-limited attacker" model class on a half-plane with a straight target line. Its reusable assets are: (a) the two-threshold classification $(\tfrac{1-\alpha^2}{\alpha}y_{D,0}, \tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{\alpha}y_{D,0}, \sqrt{\tfrac{1+\alpha^2}{\alpha^2}R_A^2 - 2R_Ay_{D,0}})$ governing bypass vs. penetrate behavior (Theorems 5, 6, 8); (b) the equilibrium terminal-state reduction via Theorem 4 (barrier outside the sensor = shortest guaranteed path compared against $R_A$), a template transferable to any reach-avoid variant with detection constraints; (c) the explicit angle thresholds $\theta_1, \theta_2$ (eqs. (31)–(32)) and closed-form barrier pieces (eqs. (61), (62), (65), (68)) that later works on detection-limited or lifeline reach-avoid games (e.g., the same authors' 2023 time-limit geometric approach, Deng's ellipse-tool paper, partial-information target-defense games in this collection) build upon or compare against. Cite it as: "Chen et al. constructed the complete analytic barrier for a reach-avoid game in which a fuel-limited attacker faces a defender that activates only within a circular (or sectorial) detection range, showing that the attacker must either graze the sensor boundary or penetrate at the critical entry angle $\theta_1$ [P011]."
